## Summary (~500 words)

John Gillingham's essay traces the dramatic reversal in American policy toward occupied Germany — from the Morgenthau Plan's vision of de-industrialization and pastoralization to the Marshall Plan's commitment to economic revival and, finally, the Schuman Plan's institutional integration of Europe around the Ruhr's coal-steel complex. The arc of the essay is the arc of the "reverse course" in Germany: from punitive dismemberment to economic reconstruction as Cold War imperative.

Gillingham opens by indicting Roosevelt's failure to plan for the postwar world. By deferring all reconstruction questions until after the conclusion of hostilities, FDR allowed the "criminally light-hearted, vengeful proposals" of Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau to fill the vacuum: raze Ruhr industry, flood the mines, return Germany to 1850 agricultural conditions. Secretary of War Henry Stimson blocked this scheme, but its substitute — JCS 1067 — was "still inadequate": vague, punitive, and incapable of providing either revival or reform. JCS 1067 turned "policy-making in Germany into an American political football."

The essay's first section (Part I) examines the period 1945–47. America was pulled between competing domestic factions — the New Deal left (seeking decartelization and reform), right-wing primitives (emerging McCarthyism), and pragmatic Wall Street types like General William Draper (Economics Division chief) who prioritized German economic recovery over structural reform. The decartelization program under James Martin, which sought to break up German industrial cartels, was gutted by internal OMGUS disagreements: "As winter 1946–47 turned into spring, the United States found itself proposing industrial revival without reform in occupied Germany."

Part II covers the Marshall Plan's genesis (1947) and the British-American tensions that nearly derailed it. The harsh winter of 1946–47 made European collapse seem imminent. George Marshall's Harvard commencement announcement (June 5, 1947) committed the U.S. to European economic reconstruction. George Kennan's Policy Planning Staff gave the plan strategic coherence: Western Europe (including the U.S. occupation zone) as the priority region; European political problems as internal/economic rather than military; no longer distinguishing between former enemies and allies. The Marshall Plan was thus a "break from the Grand Design's emphasis on disengagement and globalism" that "committed the United States to promoting German recovery in the context of European reconstruction."

Britain repeatedly frustrated American integration ambitions, refusing to cede sovereignty to supranational institutions. By spring 1950, "the Marshall Plan's integration policy was dead in the water." France — initially protectionist, fearful of German resurgence — proved the unexpected savior, largely through Jean Monnet's tireless advocacy of economic modernization and international cooperation. Monnet's genius was to reframe German industrial recovery not as a threat but as the foundation for Franco-German reconciliation through shared supranational governance of coal and steel.

The Schuman Plan (announced May 9, 1950) pooled French and German coal-steel production under a common High Authority, open to other nations. Adenauer enthusiastically endorsed it (see Adenauer W7PS). Gillingham argues the plan succeeded because it was primarily political, not economic: it "eliminated the causes of war" by binding France and Germany in structural interdependence. The U.S. backed the plan, recognizing it as the realization of Marshall Plan integration objectives.

For the course: This is the German "reverse course" — parallel to Japan's (Dower Ch. 17) but through a European integration rather than internal democratization path. The movement Morgenthau → JCS 1067 → Marshall Plan → Schuman Plan tracks perfectly with the course's "punitive vs. reformist" and "Cold War reversal" themes. It also connects to Adenauer's perspective from inside the German government and to Hahn's analysis (W6RR) of how the West German constitution was shaped under occupation pressure.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Morgenthau Plan:** Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau's 1944 proposal to deindustrialize Germany, raze the Ruhr, flood the mines, and reduce Germany to an agrarian economy. Blocked by Stimson but ideologically influential in JCS 1067. Represents the punitive extreme of occupation policy.
- **JCS 1067:** The April 1945 directive governing U.S. military occupation of Germany. Gillingham calls it the "de facto charter of OMGUS" — vague, punitive, designed to limit U.S. engagement to "disease and unrest" prevention. The starting point of the reversal narrative.
- **OMGUS (Office of Military Government, United States):** The bureaucratic body governing the U.S. occupation zone in Germany. Internally divided between reformers (decartelization advocates like Martin) and economic pragmatists (Draper).
- **Marshall Plan / European Recovery Program (ERP):** Announced June 5, 1947. The fundamental policy shift from disengagement to committed reconstruction of Western Europe, including former enemy Germany, as a Cold War strategic necessity.
- **Schuman Plan (May 9, 1950):** French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman's proposal (designed by Jean Monnet) to place French and German coal-steel production under a common High Authority. Led directly to the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC, 1951) and ultimately the EU.
- **Decartelization:** U.S. effort to break up German industrial cartels (IG Farben, etc.) held responsible for enabling Nazi militarism. Defeated internally by Wall Street-oriented officers who prioritized recovery. Parallel to land reform in Korea/Japan as a structural reform undermined by Cold War pragmatism.
- **OEEC (Organization for European Economic Cooperation):** Marshall Plan institutional body created April 1948. Gillingham shows British obstruction crippled it — Britain refused supranational authority. Replaced in effect by the more integrationist ECSC framework.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to JCS 1067 (W5PS):** Gillingham gives the full policy context for JCS 1067 — its origins in the Morgenthau-Stimson debate, its inadequacy, and its replacement by Marshall Plan logic. Reading JCS 1067's text alongside Gillingham makes its punitive/vague quality concrete.
- **Connects to Dower Ch. 17 (W7):** The German "reverse course" (from reform/punishment toward Cold War reconstruction) is the exact parallel to Japan's "reverse course" (Dower). Both involve the same chronology: 1945–46 reformist push, 1947–48 Cold War pivot. Gillingham = Germany; Dower = Japan.
- **Connects to Adenauer Ch. 14 (W7PS):** Adenauer's memoir covers the Schuman Plan from the German side — Gillingham provides the American and French context. Together they give a complete picture of the ECSC's origins.
- **Connects to Hahn, "U.S. Policy on a West German Constitution" (W6RR):** Hahn analyzes American influence on the Basic Law; Gillingham shows that economic policy was being simultaneously reformed (or reversed). Both reveal the tension between U.S. reform ambitions and sovereignty restoration.
- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4PS):** The Atlantic Charter's promise of "self-determination" and "free trade" was the rhetorical baseline; Gillingham shows how the Marshall Plan and Schuman Plan were the imperfect, politically compromised realizations of those ideals — with the U.S. "run[ning] this show."

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Gillingham / JCS 1067 / Dower**
Both Germany (Gillingham) and Japan (Dower) experienced a "reverse course" in which initial occupation directives (JCS 1067; SCAP's early reforms) gave way to Cold War economic priorities. JCS 1067's punitive framework was replaced by the Marshall Plan; Japan's labor and land reforms were curtailed as Tokyo became a Cold War partner. In both cases, anti-communist strategy ultimately prevailed over structural reform.

**Set 2: Gillingham / Adenauer / Atlantic Charter**
The Atlantic Charter promised self-determination; the Marshall Plan and Schuman Plan were its complicated fulfillment. Adenauer's eager acceptance of the Schuman Plan, and Gillingham's analysis of U.S. support for it, show how Germany's sovereignty was gradually restored through integration into Western economic institutions — a path from occupied enemy to equal partner, conditional on alignment with American Cold War strategy.

**Set 3: Gillingham / Kim / Ladejinsky**
Land reform in Korea (Kim), industrial reform in Germany (Gillingham), and land reform in Japan (Ladejinsky) all began as New Deal-inspired structural reforms and were all compromised by Cold War logic. In Korea, conservative landlords blocked redistribution; in Germany, Wall Street-oriented officers blocked decartelization; in Japan, land reform succeeded precisely because it served both reform and anti-communist goals simultaneously.
