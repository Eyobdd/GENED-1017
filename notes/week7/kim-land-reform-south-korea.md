## Summary (~500 words)

Inhan Kim's article directly challenges the revisionist (Cumings-era) consensus that U.S.-sponsored land reform in South Korea was merely a Cold War palliative — a reactive, half-hearted response to North Korea's radical 1946 land redistribution designed solely to contain communism. Drawing on declassified U.S. archival documents from both USAFIK (U.S. Army Forces in Korea) and SCAP/GHQ in Tokyo, Kim argues for a more nuanced tripartite interpretation.

**First finding:** The U.S. genuinely intended a land-to-tiller program as a dual-purpose instrument — anti-communist AND pro-democratic. New Deal ideologues in Washington believed concentrated landholding, like cartelized industry, threatened democracy and invited revolution. SWNCC 176/18 explicitly directed USAMGIK to "initiate a broad program of constructive economic and educational reforms for southern Korea looking toward the creation of conditions favorable to the development of a strong and lasting democratic system." Kim insists scholars must "distinguish between intent and effect."

**Second finding:** The partial, delayed nature of the March 1948 reform (distributing only 18.7% of total farmland — the formerly Japanese-owned "vested lands") resulted not primarily from Cold War urgency but from the volatile domestic political environment. When USAMGIK arrived in September 1945, it confronted a well-organized Korean Communist movement (KCP, Korean People's Republic) that controlled labor, peasant, and youth organizations. Security concerns pushed USAMGIK into an "anti-Communist marriage of convenience" with the Korean Democratic Party (KDP) — landed conservative elites who were tainted by collaboration with Japanese colonialism but who were pro-American and anti-Communist. This coalition delayed reform: the KDP blocked land redistribution to protect landlord interests while the U.S. felt unable to destabilize its only reliable partner. By 1947, as Communist strength receded after the violent suppression of the fall 1946 peasant uprisings, USAMGIK revived a more ambitious reform plan (including Korean-owned lands), but the now-entrenched conservatives in SKILA used procedural obstruction (engineering lack of quorum) to kill the bill, forcing USAMGIK to fall back on distributing only the vested Japanese lands unilaterally via Ordinance 173 (March 22, 1948).

**Third finding:** The significance of this partial reform should not be dismissed. It set legal precedent and normative parameters (compensated purchase, installment payments, acreage caps) that the new South Korean government inherited and built upon. The 1950 Korean land reform completed what USAMGIK started; by 1951, 80% of farm households were owner-farmers, compared to just 14% in 1945.

In terms of the course's three guiding questions: WHY occupy? — Korea was a Cold War testing ground (War Dept. draft: "Korea is a testing ground for the effectiveness of the American concept of democracy as compared to Soviet ideology"). WHAT did Americans do? — USAMGIK imposed conservative allies, delayed reform, then executed a partial land redistribution under pressure. HOW did it end? — Reform created a precedent that Korean actors themselves completed, illustrating that the occupied territory "is not a blank slate upon which the occupying power writes at will."

Kim's argument connects strongly to Dower (Ch. 17) on Japan's land reform (same SCAP network, shared New Deal ideology), to Ladejinsky (the SCAP advisor who implemented both Japan and Korea programs), and to Chang's "Independence Without Liberation" on the structural limits of U.S.-sponsored reform. It also speaks directly to JCS 1067 (W5PS) — both show how security imperatives overrode reformist intentions.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **USAMGIK (U.S. Army Military Government in Korea):** The occupation administration governing South Korea from September 1945 to August 1948. Its land reform record is the article's primary subject.
- **Vested Lands:** Formerly Japanese-owned agricultural properties seized by USAMGIK under Ordinance No. 33 (December 1945). Only these lands — 18.7% of total farmland — were redistributed in March 1948.
- **Korean Democratic Party (KDP):** Conservative political party of educated, often pro-Japanese elites who became USAMGIK's governing partners. Their landowner base was the primary obstacle to comprehensive land reform.
- **SKILA (South Korean Interim Legislative Assembly):** The interim legislature elected in October 1946. Despite a reformist mandate from USAMGIK, it was dominated by conservatives who obstructed the 1947–48 land reform bill.
- **"Anti-Communist marriage of convenience":** Kim's characterization of the USAMGIK-KDP alliance — pragmatic Cold War security logic that paradoxically undermined the democratic reform agenda.
- **Land-to-tiller program:** Policy principle (shared across Japan, Korea, Taiwan) transferring cultivated land to those who worked it; a SCAP-era reformist ideal rooted in New Deal thinking about dispersing economic power.
- **New Deal ideology in occupation policy:** The belief, prevalent among Truman-era policymakers, that concentrated economic power (cartels, large landholdings) bred authoritarianism and instability. Drove reform plans in Japan, Germany, AND Korea.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Dower (Ch. 17, "Engineering Growth"):** SCAP in Tokyo simultaneously sponsored Japan's land reform through the same administrative network. Ladejinsky (W7PS) was a SCAP advisor who worked on both. SCAP and USAMGIK coordinated closely — the article repeatedly shows SCAP officials directing and monitoring Korea's land reform.
- **Connects to Ladejinsky (W7PS):** Ladejinsky's "Plow Outbids the Sword" explicitly celebrates MacArthur's Japan land reform as the anti-communist weapon Kim describes. Kim's academic analysis and Ladejinsky's policymaker account are two sides of the same reform logic.
- **Connects to Chang, "Independence Without Liberation" (W5RR/W6RR):** Chang shows Korea was treated as occupied rather than liberated; Kim's account of USAMGIK allying with pro-Japanese collaborators (KDP) and suppressing leftist organizations confirms the structural limits Chang identifies.
- **Connects to SWNCC 176 (W5PS):** Kim cites SWNCC 176/18 repeatedly as the directive authorizing democratic and economic reform. The gap between SWNCC's stated intentions and USAMGIK's actual conservative alliance illustrates the same tension visible in the document itself.
- **Connects to JCS 1067 (W5PS) via Gillingham:** Both JCS 1067 (Germany) and USAMGIK's Korea policy began with punitive/reformist frameworks and were compromised by Cold War security priorities — the "reverse course" pattern.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Kim / Ladejinsky / Chang**
Land reform in South Korea and Japan was simultaneously a Cold War anti-communist strategy and a New Deal democratic project. Both Kim and Ladejinsky show that the "plow" was wielded against communist appeal; but Chang reminds us that in Korea, "liberation" never meant genuine self-determination — the U.S. chose conservative collaborators over leftist nationalists, limiting reform's democratic promise.

**Set 2: Kim / SWNCC 176 / Dower**
SWNCC 176's directive for democratic reform in Korea mirrored SCAP's reformist agenda in Japan (Dower Ch. 17), but Kim reveals how Cold War security pressures transformed these documents' ideals into partial, politically compromised outcomes — the gap between policy intention and implementation is the central story of U.S. occupation in both countries.

**Set 3: Kim / JCS 1067 / Gillingham**
JCS 1067, SWNCC 176, and U.S. land reform plans all began with reformist (or punitive-reformist) intentions toward occupied societies but were redirected by Cold War logic — in Germany (Gillingham) toward industrial revival and European integration, and in Korea (Kim) toward alliance with landed conservatives. "Reverse course" operated across all three case studies.

### Essay Questions (Part 2 practice)

1. "Americans occupied Korea, Japan, and Germany with genuine reformist intentions, but Cold War priorities consistently trumped those intentions." Using Kim, Dower, and Gillingham, assess the extent to which the "reverse course" concept accurately describes U.S. occupation policy across all three case studies.

2. To what extent did occupied peoples shape the outcomes of U.S. reform programs? Using Kim's Korea case and at least one other reading (Chang, Dower, or Adenauer), analyze the role of local political actors in determining what Americans could actually accomplish.
