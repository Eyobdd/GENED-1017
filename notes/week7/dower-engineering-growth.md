## Summary (~500 words)

Chapter 17, "Engineering Growth," covers the economic dimensions of the U.S. occupation of Japan in its later phase — roughly 1947–1952 — with particular attention to the "reverse course" in economic policy and the structural transformation of the Japanese economy from the SCAP-directed reform agenda toward Cold War reconstruction priorities.

**The reform agenda and its limits.** In the early occupation (1945–47), SCAP pursued a comprehensive economic transformation program rooted in New Deal ideology: land reform (redistribution of agricultural land from landlords to tenants), decartelization (breaking up the zaibatsu industrial conglomerates held responsible for militarism), labor union promotion, and economic democratization. Dower's account of these reforms in Ch. 17 shows how they were simultaneously genuine attempts at structural change AND instruments of U.S. Cold War strategy — the same dual logic Kim identifies in Korea and Ladejinsky articulates explicitly.

**Land reform as the reform's success story.** Japan's land reform, implemented through MacArthur's December 15, 1945 directive (cited by Ladejinsky, W7PS), was among the most successful of SCAP's programs. By the early 1950s, the tenancy rate had fallen dramatically; the reform created a class of small owner-farmers who became economically stable and politically moderate — exactly the "middle-of-the-road" constituency Ladejinsky celebrated. Dower contextualizes this within the broader SCAP reform program and notes the deep contradictions: the same SCAP that promoted labor unions and land reform also began suppressing radical labor organizing as the Cold War hardened.

**The "reverse course" (1947–1952).** The key movement in Ch. 17 is the shift from reform to stabilization. As the Cold War intensified, Washington's priority in Japan shifted from dismantling the structures of Japanese militarism to rebuilding Japan as a stable anti-communist partner in Asia. This meant:
- Curtailing the decartelization program (zaibatsu were rebuilt under new names)
- Moving against radical labor unions (the 1947 General Strike ban)
- Implementing the "Dodge Line" (1949) — deflationary austerity imposed by Joseph Dodge that prioritized balanced budgets and export-led growth over social welfare
- Accelerating the peace treaty process and Japan's integration into the U.S.-led Cold War security system

**"Engineering Growth."** The chapter title itself signals the shift: by the late occupation period, SCAP was no longer primarily "engineering democracy" but "engineering growth" — managing Japan's economic recovery as a Cold War strategic asset. The Korean War (1950) accelerated this dramatically: U.S. military procurement orders in Japan ("special procurements") essentially kick-started Japanese industrial recovery, reintegrating the same industrial capacity SCAP had tried to dismantle. Japan became a critical rear base and supplier for the Korean War effort.

**Connection to course themes.** Dower's Ch. 17 is the Japan anchor for the Week 7 theme of "Remaking Economy and Society." It provides the macro-context for Ladejinsky's specific land reform account and mirrors Gillingham's Germany narrative: in both cases, the U.S. moved from reform/punishment to reconstruction/stabilization under Cold War pressure. The chapter also raises questions about occupied peoples' responses: Japanese business elites, bureaucrats, and conservative politicians actively worked with SCAP to shape the "reverse course" in their favor — a dynamic Kim also identifies in Korea with the KDP.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Reverse course (逆コース, gyaku kōsu):** The shift in U.S. occupation policy from reform/democratization (1945–47) to Cold War stabilization/reconstruction (1947–52). In economic terms: from decartelization and labor empowerment to industrial revival and export-led growth. The Japan case of a pattern also visible in Germany (Gillingham).
- **Zaibatsu:** Pre-war Japanese industrial and financial conglomerates (Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, etc.) SCAP initially targeted for dissolution as engines of militarism. Partly broken up but reconstituted as *keiretsu* — the "reverse course" halted full decartelization.
- **Dodge Line (1949):** The deflationary austerity program imposed by Detroit banker Joseph Dodge as SCAP's financial stabilization policy. Fixed the exchange rate at ¥360/$1, slashed government spending, reduced inflation — but caused unemployment and hardship. A Cold War economic stabilization measure that contradicted early reform goals.
- **Special procurements (特需, tokuji):** U.S. military purchasing in Japan during the Korean War (1950–53). Estimated at $2–3 billion. Effectively rebooted Japanese industrial production and is often cited as the real start of Japan's "economic miracle." The most dramatic evidence of "engineering growth."
- **Land reform (農地改革):** MacArthur's directive of December 15, 1945, enacted into law October 1946. Transferred ~5.8 million acres; reduced tenancy from ~46% to ~11% of cultivated land. The reform most often cited as SCAP's greatest success — and the one Ladejinsky championed.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Ladejinsky (W7PS):** Ladejinsky's two pieces are the practitioner's account of the Japan land reform Dower Ch. 17 historicizes. Reading them together gives both the policy rationale (Ladejinsky's "plow outbids the sword") and the historical context (Dower's "reverse course" shows how the reform fit within — and was later exceeded by — SCAP's Cold War pivot).
- **Connects to Kim (W7RR):** Kim explicitly cites Dower (pp. 220–221) and shows the SCAP-USAMGIK connection: the same network of New Deal officials and the same land reform model operated in Japan and Korea. Dower's Ch. 17 provides the Japan side of a comparison Kim makes central.
- **Connects to Gillingham (W7RR):** Dower (Japan) and Gillingham (Germany) are the two pillars of the Week 7 "reverse course" theme. Both show the same arc: reform → Cold War reconstruction. Both show the same mechanism: domestic U.S. politics (anticommunism) overriding reformist impulses. The comparison is explicit midterm material.
- **Connects to Dower Ch. 6, "Neocolonial Revolution" (W5):** Ch. 6 covered the early reform phase (labor, constitution, demilitarization). Ch. 17 shows what happened to those reforms under Cold War pressure. Reading the two together traces the full arc of the occupation.
- **Connects to Chang, "Independence Without Liberation" (W5RR):** Chang's Korea and Dower's Japan both show how "liberation" was conditional — in Japan, the liberation from landlordism succeeded, but the liberation of labor was reversed when it threatened Cold War stability.

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Dower Ch. 17 / Ladejinsky / Kim**
Land reform in Japan (Dower/Ladejinsky) and Korea (Kim) was the signature achievement of SCAP's economic reform program — and the reform that best survived the "reverse course." Both Dower and Kim show that land reform succeeded in part because it served Cold War goals (undermining communist appeal among peasants) while also genuinely redistributing economic power. The "plow outbids the sword" logic was both idealistic and strategic.

**Set 2: Dower Ch. 17 / Gillingham / JCS 1067**
JCS 1067 and SCAP's early directives both began with reformist intentions (decartelization in Germany, zaibatsu dissolution in Japan) that were derailed by Cold War stabilization priorities. Gillingham and Dower tell parallel stories: by 1947–48, "engineering growth" had replaced "engineering democracy" in both occupied countries. The Korean War and the Marshall Plan respectively completed each country's transformation from reform object to Cold War partner.
