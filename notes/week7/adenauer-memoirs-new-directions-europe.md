## Summary (~300 words)

This chapter of Adenauer's *Memoirs 1945–53* covers the period January–June 1950, centering on two momentous events: Germany's entry into the Council of Europe and Adenauer's reception of the Schuman Plan. It is an essential primary source because it presents these turning points entirely from the perspective of the occupied/former enemy becoming a voluntary participant in European integration — a perspective absent from Gillingham's analytical account.

**The Saar Question and Robert Schuman (pp. 256–258):** Adenauer opens by recounting his October 1948 meeting with French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman, the first of many encounters. The Saar region (under French economic annexation since 1945) was a persistent obstacle to Franco-German reconciliation. Adenauer's careful navigation of this "nerve-point" — resisting French annexation while building personal trust with Schuman — frames the entire chapter. He delayed provoking a showdown over the Saar because he saw German participation in the Council of Europe as a higher priority. His cabinet agreed: "the Saar question, which has been handled without free and frank exchanges of views...must not be allowed to serve as an excuse for withdrawal from European cooperation which transcends it in importance."

**The Schuman Plan (pp. 256–266, centered on May 9, 1950):** The chapter's climax is Adenauer's account of receiving Schuman's letter on May 9, 1950 — the day the French cabinet announced what became known as the Schuman Plan. Schuman's personal letter explained the proposal's purpose was "not economic, but eminently political": placing French and German coal-steel production under a common High Authority would bind the two nations in structural interdependence, making rearmament detectable and war impossible. Adenauer's response was immediate and wholehearted: "Schuman's plan corresponded entirely with the ideas I had been advocating for a long time concerning the integration of the key industries of Europe. I informed Robert Schuman at once that I accepted his proposal wholeheartedly."

Adenauer's Bundestag speech (June 13, 1950) defending entry into the Council of Europe and endorsing the Schuman Plan is the chapter's rhetorical centerpiece. He framed the choice in Cold War terms — two power blocs (Soviet Union, U.S.) threatening to divide the world, with Europeans needing to create "a third force" strong enough to matter. He argued that rejecting the Council of Europe would be "a declaration against the West" and that German participation in a federated Europe was both a moral obligation (given the "guilt incurred by the war") and a practical necessity for Berlin and the German East. The Bundestag voted 220 to 152 to join.

For the course: Adenauer shows Germany not as passive recipient of occupation policy but as active agent choosing integration over resistance. His perspective complements Gillingham's American-focused analysis and illuminates how the occupied party could embrace the occupier's framework when it aligned with national interest. The Schuman Plan discussion also raises the course question of self-determination — Germany's entry into European institutions was formally voluntary but occurred within a post-occupation framework the Allies designed.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Schuman Plan (May 9, 1950):** French FM Schuman's proposal to pool French and German coal-steel production under a common "High Authority." Designed by Jean Monnet; primarily political (eliminating rearmament early warning systems and mutual fear). Led to the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC, 1951).
- **Council of Europe:** Established 1949; a parliamentary consultative body for Western European states. Germany's entry (voted June 15, 1950, 220–152) was the first formal step of West German rehabilitation into the community of European nations.
- **Saar Question:** France's contested annexation of the Saar region (coal-rich border territory) as part of postwar reparations. A persistent obstacle to Franco-German reconciliation, eventually resolved in Germany's favor by 1957. Adenauer carefully managed it to avoid derailing European integration.
- **European federation:** The long-term political goal Adenauer endorsed — a supranational European entity strong enough to balance the U.S. and USSR. The Schuman Plan and Council of Europe were first steps.
- **Occupation Statute:** The legal framework governing Allied control of West Germany after 1949. Adenauer expected (correctly) that European integration would accelerate its revision and Germany's sovereignty restoration.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Gillingham (W7RR):** Gillingham tells the Schuman Plan story from the American and French sides; Adenauer tells it from the German side. Together they give a 360-degree view. Gillingham's "Schuman Plan restored [international cooperation]" = Adenauer's "accepted his proposal wholeheartedly."
- **Connects to Hahn, "U.S. Policy on a West German Constitution" (W6RR):** Hahn shows American influence on the Basic Law's design; Adenauer shows how the Federal Republic then strategically engaged with European institutions. Constitutional foundation (Hahn) + European integration (Adenauer) = West Germany's postwar rehabilitation path.
- **Connects to Adenauer Ch. 8 (W6PS):** The previous Adenauer memoir chapter (W6) covered the Parliamentary Council's writing of the Basic Law. Ch. 14 (W7) shows the next phase: the new state using its constitution to engage internationally. Together they document Germany's reconstruction from occupied enemy to sovereign partner.
- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4PS):** The Atlantic Charter promised "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live." Adenauer's chapter raises the question of how voluntary Germany's "choice" to integrate was — structurally constrained by occupation, but Adenauer genuinely believed in the path.
- **Connects to Chang, "Independence Without Liberation" (W5RR):** Chang's Korea parallels Adenauer's Germany: in both cases, the occupied state was given a form of self-governance within a framework designed by the occupier. But where Korean nationalists resisted, Adenauer embraced integration — the difference in outcomes was shaped by how occupation was structured and what integration offered.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Adenauer / Gillingham / Atlantic Charter**
The Atlantic Charter promised self-determination; the Schuman Plan was its realization for Germany — or was it? Adenauer embraced the plan as genuine European federation; Gillingham shows the U.S. backed it primarily as Cold War strategy; together they reveal how self-determination in postwar Europe was achieved through, not despite, American hegemony. Germany became sovereign by accepting the framework its former occupiers designed.

**Set 2: Adenauer / Hahn / Dower**
Constitutional rewriting (Hahn for Germany, Dower for Japan) and economic integration (Adenauer's Schuman Plan) were parallel tracks of occupation-era transformation. In both Germany and Japan, the occupier designed constitutional and economic frameworks that the occupied elite then claimed as their own — raising the question of where occupation ends and genuine self-governance begins.
