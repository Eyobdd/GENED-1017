## Summary

This packet bundles three Vietnamese documents spanning seventeen years of anti-colonial and anti-imperial struggle (1945–1962). Together they form a political genealogy: the founding declaration of Vietnamese independence, the formal communist party platform at the height of the First Indochina War, and the NLF's revolutionary program on the eve of large-scale US intervention.

**Ho Chi Minh's Declaration of Independence (September 2, 1945)** is the central document. Its rhetorical opening move is deliberate and pointed: Ho quotes the American Declaration of Independence and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man verbatim, then immediately turns them against their authors. France claimed to bring "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity" to Vietnam; the Declaration catalogs eighty years of French brutality — more prisons than schools, forced opium and alcohol, monopolization of trade, and ultimately, capitulation to Japan in 1940 and then again in 1945. The "double yoke" of French and Japanese domination culminated in a famine that killed over two million Vietnamese in 1944–45. Ho's argument is elegant: because France failed on its own stated terms — and actually "twice sold our country to the Japanese" — Vietnam has not only the moral right but the already-accomplished *fact* of independence. The Vietminh had fought alongside the Allies; Vietnam therefore deserves recognition under the principles the Allies themselves proclaimed at Tehran and San Francisco (i.e., self-determination). This is a direct appeal to American anti-colonialism, framing Vietnamese independence as consistent with — even demanded by — American values.

**The Laodong Party Manifesto (February 1951)** marks the public formalization of the Vietnamese Communist Party (renamed Laodong/Workers' Party) after years of operating covertly. Written six years into the First Indochina War, it explicitly names both France *and* "American interventionists" as enemies, and aligns Vietnam with the Soviet Union, China, and "people's democracies." The class coalition is inclusive — workers, peasants, intellectuals, national bourgeoisie, landlords, religious minorities, overseas Vietnamese — a united front strategy designed for maximum mobilization. It also gestures toward Indochina-wide liberation (Cambodia, Laos), signaling Vietnamese communist ambitions beyond national borders.

**The Viet Cong Program (January 1962)** is a 10-point platform of the NLF at the onset of US military advisory escalation. Its targets are dual: the Diem government and US influence ("economic monopoly," "military advisers," "American cultural enslavement"). The program is explicitly nationalist as well as socialist — land reform, democratic liberties, gender and ethnic equality, and reunification — but it is careful to frame reunification as peaceful and to position itself as defending sovereignty against foreign interference, not as a Soviet or Chinese proxy. It echoes the 1945 Declaration's rhetorical move of appealing to universal democratic principles while identifying Americans (no longer the French) as the new imperial power.

**So what:** Reading these three documents together reveals Vietnam's continuous, self-articulated challenge to the gap between American professed values (self-determination, democracy, anti-imperialism) and American practice. Ho Chi Minh in 1945 invited the US to honor its own principles; by 1962 the Viet Cong was naming the US as the successor imperial power. The course theme of **self-determination vs. imperial control** (Theme 6) runs directly through all three. The documents also illustrate **collaboration and resistance** (Theme 8) — not passive resistance but active political mobilization that appropriated Western liberal language.

**Guiding Questions:**
- **WHY** occupy? — These documents help explain *why the US eventually occupies Vietnam* through Vietnamese eyes: each American step (advisers, aid to France, support for Diem) was experienced as a betrayal of the 1945 promise.
- **WHAT** did they do / how did people respond? — The Viet Cong Program shows the organized political-military alternative that American occupation would face.
- **HOW** does occupation end? — The Declaration's insistence that independence was already a *fait accompli* ("it is so already") foreshadows the intractability that would define the Vietnam War.

## Key Terms

- **Democratic Republic of Vietnam (DRV):** The government proclaimed by Ho Chi Minh on September 2, 1945 in Hanoi; the political successor to the Vietminh resistance. Its legitimacy claim rested on both nationalist struggle and Allied wartime principles.
- **Vietminh (Viet Nam Doc Lap Dong Minh Hoi):** "League for the Independence of Vietnam" — the communist-led but broad nationalist front founded 1941; fought both French colonialism and Japanese occupation; referenced in the Declaration as having tried to ally with France against Japan.
- **Double yoke:** Ho's phrase for the 1940–1945 period of simultaneous French colonial and Japanese fascist domination — used to indict France for collaboration and delegitimize any French claim to resume colonial rule.
- **Self-determination:** The principle (invoked from Wilson's Fourteen Points through the Atlantic Charter to the UN Charter) that peoples have the right to determine their own political status. Ho weaponizes it against France and anticipates it will obligate the Allies to recognize Vietnam.
- **Laodong (Workers') Party:** The renamed Vietnamese Communist Party (VCP), formally announced February 1951; public emergence marks the shift from covert united-front politics to overt party leadership of the resistance.
- **NLF / Viet Cong:** National Liberation Front of South Vietnam (1960); called "Viet Cong" (Vietnamese Communist) by the Diem government and US. The 1962 Program is the NLF's public platform — mixing communist, nationalist, and democratic rhetoric for broad coalition appeal.
- **Agrovilles / resettlement centers:** Diem-era strategic hamlet program that forcibly relocated rural Vietnamese — targeted in the 1962 NLF program as evidence of the Diem government's coercive anti-peasant character.
- **"American cultural enslavement":** NLF phrase for US cultural influence under Diem — echoes anti-colonial critiques of Western education (cf. Constantino on Philippines) and anticipates later anti-Americanism in occupied societies.
