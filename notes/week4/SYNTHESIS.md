# Week 4 Synthesis: Americans as Occupiers, Again
**Case Study:** Germany (occupation), Japan (planning phase)
**Lectures:** Back to the Future: Impact of World War / Planning for Occupation and Reform

---

## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 4 pivots from the Philippines to WWII-era Germany and Japan, but its deepest purpose is to force a question: what changed, structurally, between 1898 and 1945? The four texts — Duara's theoretical essay, Merritt's retrospective on Germany, and the Atlantic Charter and Reischauer memo as paired primary sources — work together to show that the answer is more complicated than either "everything" or "nothing."

The Atlantic Charter and the Reischauer memo are the week's most productive juxtaposition. The Charter (August 1941) proclaims universal principles: self-determination, no territorial aggrandizement, "victor or vanquished" equal access to world trade. It is the WWII-era Wilsonian vision articulated at the highest level. The Reischauer memo (September 1942, thirteen months later) shows what a Harvard faculty member working on war strategy was actually thinking: preserve the Japanese Emperor as a "puppet," deploy Japanese Americans as ideological assets for "winning the peace," manage public perception of Hirohito to preserve his postwar utility. The two documents are not in contradiction — Reischauer would have argued he was *implementing* the Charter's goals through realistic means — but their juxtaposition makes visible what the Charter's language obscures: that self-determination and strategic client-state planning were being developed simultaneously, in the same circles, for the same postwar order.

Duara provides the analytical framework that makes this simultaneity intelligible rather than paradoxical. His "imperialism of free nations" argument is precisely that the US, Japan, and the Soviet Union operated through anticolonial rhetoric — self-determination, sovereignty, development — while building structures of effective control. The Atlantic Charter is not the opposite of Reischauer's puppet; the Charter is the ideological vehicle through which Reischauer's arrangement would be legitimated. Both documents instantiate the same formation.

Merritt gives Germany the empirical texture that Duara's comparative abstraction requires. His key observations map onto the week's conceptual tensions with precision. First, formal policy and practice diverged immediately: JCS 1067's harsh prescriptions were "virtually a dead letter within months" as commanders adapted on the ground. Second, institutional democratization (Basic Law, denazification, entrenching anti-Nazi personnel) substantially succeeded; attitudinal democratization (schools, pamphlets, exchange programs) was fitful and self-undermining — the Americans' assertion of cultural superiority made Germans bristle rather than comply. Third, the Cold War reversal in Germany anticipated Japan's by roughly two to three years: reform-minded German allies were sidelined in 1948, before SWNCC 150 had even been in operation a full year in Japan.

The four texts also triangulate around the week's governing tension — punitive vs. reformist — in a way that connects back to the Philippines. Merritt names this explicitly as the minimalist/maximalist debate within American policy circles. The Atlantic Charter's Point 4 ("victor or vanquished") stakes out the reformist position at the diplomatic level: defeated nations should be rebuilt as trading partners, not permanently punished. Reischauer's memo reinforces this from the Japan side — harsh treatment of Hirohito in the wartime press would ruin postwar utility, so press coverage must be managed in service of occupation planning. Duara's structural point is that the choice between punishment and reform is not primarily a moral choice but a strategic one: the "imperialism of free nations" develops client-states because that structure serves long-term competitive purposes better than extraction or destruction. Reform is instrumentally rational from the imperialist's perspective, not altruistic.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 1 (Fradera, "Reading Imperial Transitions"):**

Fradera built the course's comparative vocabulary: exceptionality, dangerous symmetries, imperial transition. Duara explicitly extends this framework into the 20th century. Where Fradera showed that late-19th-century empires formed "dangerous symmetries" by borrowing techniques from each other, Duara demonstrates that Japan, the US, and the Soviet Union repeated this pattern one generation later — Japan's Manchukuo drew on US Monroe Doctrine logic and Soviet nationality policy; postwar US empire drew on client-state structures Japan had pioneered. Both argue against American exceptionalism at the structural level: the US did not invent a new form of internationalism in 1945; it perfected a form already visible in 1932 Manchuria.

Merritt's observation that "the theme of the universal relevance of the American way of life runs like a red thread" through US foreign policy from the founders through WWII is Fradera's "justificatory discourse" argument stated in miniature. The ideological continuity Fradera traced across three centuries — liberal universalism advancing alongside imperial expansion — is what Merritt observes in occupying officials who genuinely believed they were spreading principles proven valid everywhere, not merely projecting power.

The Atlantic Charter's self-determination language also connects to Fradera's "dangerous symmetries." The Charter propagated the same rights-language that colonized peoples had been turning against their occupiers since at least the Philippine-American War. Colonized Asians and Africans cited Point 3 ("the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live") to demand independence — the British Empire's subjects in India and Africa most prominently. The Charter's framers intended it for European nations under Nazi occupation; its language was structurally incapable of being so limited.

**Week 2 (Kramer, Beveridge, Schurz, Teller/Platt):**

Duara provides the through-line from Beveridge's Senate speech to WWII occupation planning. Beveridge's "wardship" logic — Filipinos not yet capable of self-government, requiring American tutelage — persisted in Lane's 1922 "limited self-determination, a leading-string law" (quoted by Duara, p. 233). This is the same ideological structure, twenty-four years later, now applied to the Caribbean basin. Reischauer's 1942 memo continues the pattern: the Emperor is the "best possible puppet" the US can use to achieve cooperation that genuine Filipino-style self-determination would never produce.

Kramer showed how racial ideology shaped US colonial practice transnationally — assembled at the intersection of Spanish colonial archives, British precedents, and domestic American racial formations. Reischauer's memo reveals the same racial structure in WWII planning. Japan frames the Pacific War as a "holy crusade of yellow and brown peoples for freedom from the white race" — pan-Asianism as military strategy. American internment of Japanese Americans validates this narrative. Reischauer's proposed counter-strategy — use Japanese American military service to prove the US fights militarism, not Asians — addresses racial optics without challenging the racial structure of American society. This is Kramer's pattern exactly: race managed and instrumentalized, not resolved.

**Week 3 (May, Mabini, Constantino, Worcester, NYT/Smith):**

Merritt's account of the German democratization program is structurally identical to May's analysis of the Philippine colonial education program. Both operated on the premise that cultural transformation through schools, exchange programs, and curriculum reform could reshape a population's political values. Both ran into the same obstacles: local cultural pride, the inherent tension between imposing democratic values and the democratic spirit, and the resilience of practices shaped over generations. Merritt's frank admission — "complete control over every aspect of personal and social life for a very long time might have had the desired effect (and in this regard the Soviet Union was far more successful)" — has no equivalent in May, but it states explicitly what May's data implies.

Mabini's natural law argument — authority resides in the people themselves, external sovereignty cannot be morally imposed — anticipated precisely the tension the Atlantic Charter formalized in 1941. Mabini's claim, in 1903, was that the US proclamation of democratic ideals was structurally incompatible with colonial occupation. The Charter's Point 3, forty years later, formally acknowledged this claim — and then the Reischauer memo showed how it would be circumvented. The philosophical position Mabini articulated from the losing side of the Philippine-American War eventually became official Allied war aims; it was then operationalized as "puppet" governance in practice.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is the week's dominant theme and the axis on which all four readings turn. The Atlantic Charter makes self-determination official Allied ideology. Reischauer's memo shows what it means in practice for Japan. Merritt shows how it was managed in Germany — Germans were not given the choice to choose any government they wished but were "guided" toward specific institutional forms (federalism, constructive vote of no confidence, five-percent threshold). Duara provides the theoretical explanation: the imperialism of free nations operates precisely through sovereign nation-state forms, not against them. Self-determination and imperial control are not opposites in this formation; they coexist structurally.

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy and planning — punitive vs. reformist)** is introduced for the Germany/Japan cases this week. Merritt's minimalist/maximalist typology structures the debate; JCS 1067's harshness vs. its practical obsolescence demonstrates that formal policy and operational reality diverge immediately. Reischauer's memo is the reformist position applied to Japan planning: do not destroy the Emperor, preserve what can be used, rebuild what can be rebuilt. The Atlantic Charter's "victor or vanquished" (Point 4) is the ideological anchor for this position.

**Theme 5 (Race, civilization, and imperial ideology)** appears most sharply in Reischauer's memo — Japan's pan-Asian racial framing of the Pacific War, American internment as confirmation of that frame, and the strategic deployment of Japanese American military service to counter it. Duara's analysis of how racial ideology (anti-Asian exclusion in the US, white supremacy) simultaneously fueled Japanese pan-Asianism and damaged US credibility in Asia adds structural depth. Race is not just background context here; it is a live operational variable in occupation planning.

**Theme 4 (Cold War reversal / "reverse course")** is introduced this week via Merritt. The Germany reversal (1947–1948) is less famous than Japan's but preceded it: reform-minded Germans were sidelined, conservative anti-Soviet Germans elevated, and the goal of social transformation gave way to the goal of alliance-building. Duara's framework explains why this was not a deviation from the "imperialism of free nations" but structurally built into it — strategic competition, not democratic idealism, is the driving force, and the Cold War simply made this explicit.

**Theme 2 (Democratic reform and constitutionalism)** receives its first substantial treatment this week via Merritt's account of the Basic Law. The "constructive vote of no confidence," federalism, and the five-percent threshold are American contributions to West German constitutional design — institutional engineering aimed at preventing the repeat of Weimar's failure. This sets up the Week 6 comparative study of Japan, Germany, and Korea constitutions.

## Combined Key Terms

**From Duara:**
- **New imperialism / "imperialism of free nations"** (Duara, p. 212): 20th-century imperial form exercised by US, Japan, USSR — client-states, anticolonial ideology, developmental investment, regional economic blocs. Manchukuo (1932–1945) is the prototype; postwar US is the "apogee."
- **Strategic autarky** (Duara, p. 221): The concept that imperial powers need self-sufficient regional economic blocs for long-term competition — requiring client-states to be industrialized, not backward. Explains Manchukuo's developmental character and anticipates Marshall Plan logic.
- **Pan-Asianism** (Duara, pp. 222–225): Japanese ideological claim of Asian civilizational solidarity against Western imperialism; cover for Japanese empire. Connects to Reischauer's analysis of Pacific War racial framing.
- **Concordia Association** (*kyowakai*) (Duara, p. 226): Manchukuo's mass mobilization organization; formally multiethnic, functionally an instrument of the Kwantung Army. Connects to May (W3) on education as organizational control.
- **Kingly way** (*wangdao*) / **Concord of nationalities** (*minzoku kyowa*) (Duara, pp. 224–228): Manchukuo's dual legitimacy claims — classical benevolent rule and multiethnic harmony — in lieu of the nation-state legitimacy the regime lacked.
- **Tutelage / limited self-determination** (Duara, p. 233; Lane, 1922): The ideological device deferring full self-determination while maintaining client-state control; direct line from Beveridge's wardship logic (W2) to postwar US practice.
- **Ultraimperialism** (Duara, p. 233): Post-WWII US global empire — military bases, IMF/World Bank/GATT structures managing cooperation among capitalist powers and developmental imperialism in the decolonized world.

**From Merritt:**
- **OMGUS** (Office of Military Government, United States): The American military government authority in Germany, 1945–1949; more aggressive denazification than British or French equivalents.
- **Denazification** (Merritt, pp. 94–96): Allied program removing Nazi officials from authority; uneven application, relaxed as Cold War priorities shifted.
- **Minimalists vs. Maximalists** (Merritt, p. 92): American policy factions; minimalists sought only to end German belligerency, maximalists wanted permanent subjugation (Morgenthau Plan). JCS 1067 lay between them and became a "dead letter within months."
- **Basic Law** (*Grundgesetz*) (Merritt, pp. 96–98): West German constitution (1949); American contributions included federalism, constructive vote of no confidence, five-percent electoral threshold, bill of rights, judicial review.
- **Constructive vote of no confidence** (Merritt, p. 97): Parliament must elect a replacement chancellor before forcing the incumbent's resignation; designed to prevent Weimar-style cabinet instability.
- **Cold War reversal (Germany)** (Merritt, p. 100): 1947–1948 shift from democratization to alliance-building; reform-minded Germans sidelined for conservative anti-Soviet counterparts. Precedes and anticipates Japan's "reverse course."

**From Atlantic Charter:**
- **Self-determination (Point 3)**: "The right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live"; most politically explosive principle. Applied selectively; colonized peoples cited it to demand independence.
- **"Victor or vanquished" (Point 4)**: Explicit inclusion of defeated nations in the postwar economic order; repudiation of Versailles punitive logic. Shapes Marshall Plan and rebuilding of Germany and Japan as trading partners.
- **Freedom from fear and want (Point 6)**: FDR's Four Freedoms embedded in the Charter; defines postwar order in terms of individual welfare, not just state sovereignty.
- **Wilsonianism**: The belief (attributed to Roosevelt in the Charter's introductory note) in international organization, democratic processes, and self-determination as the basis for lasting peace; 1941-era iteration of Wilson's Fourteen Points.

**From Reischauer:**
- **Scapegoat problem** (Reischauer, p. 399): Japan's unique postwar challenge — no Hitler, Mussolini, or discredited party for the defeated population to repudiate; shapes the entire case for preserving the Emperor.
- **"Winning the peace"** (Reischauer, pp. 399, 402): The ideological-political challenge after military victory; requires strategic planning as careful as military operations.
- **Emperor as puppet** (Reischauer, p. 400): Reischauer's frank proposal to preserve Hirohito as a "puppet" and future ally — explicitly invoking Japan's Manchukuo experience as the model. Instantiates Duara's client-state framework in a planning document.
- **Racial war / pan-Asianism** (Reischauer, p. 401): Japan's framing of the Pacific War as "a holy crusade of yellow and brown peoples for freedom from the white race"; internment validates this narrative.
- **"Unsullied" Emperor** (Reischauer, p. 400): Prescription that wartime press not associate Hirohito with Hitler/Mussolini, preserving his post-war utility for the client-state arrangement.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: Atlantic Charter / Reischauer / Duara**
*(Within Week 4 — idealism, strategic planning, structural explanation)*
The Atlantic Charter proclaimed self-determination for all peoples — the high-water mark of Wilsonian liberal internationalism. Thirteen months later, Reischauer's memo planned for a Japan built around a preserved Emperor, explicitly called a "puppet," drawn on Japan's own Manchukuo experience. Duara's framework dissolves the contradiction: the "imperialism of free nations" operates *through* sovereign forms and anticolonial rhetoric rather than against them. The Charter is not the opposite of Reischauer's arrangement; it is its ideological vehicle. Self-determination rhetoric enables, rather than forecloses, client-state imperialism.

**Set 2: Tutelage / Beveridge / Cold War Reversal**
*(Duara + Beveridge W2PS + Merritt W4RR — across three weeks)*
Beveridge in 1900 justified Philippine annexation through tutelage: Filipinos were not yet capable of self-government, requiring American guidance of indefinite duration. Duara shows this logic persisted into mid-20th-century US foreign policy as "limited self-determination" — Lane's "leading-string law" applied across the Caribbean basin. Merritt's Cold War reversal in Germany demonstrates the structural endpoint: when strategic competition sharpened in 1948, the tutors abandoned reform-minded allies for conservative ones who offered anti-Soviet usefulness, not democratic progress. Tutelage was always conditional on strategic interest, not developmental achievement.

**Set 3: Scapegoat Problem / Mabini / Miseducation**
*(Reischauer W4PS + Mabini W3PS + Constantino W3PS — spans Week 3 and Week 4)*
Reischauer diagnosed Japan's scapegoat problem: no single figure could absorb defeat's blame, so military defeat might paradoxically strengthen military control. His solution — preserve the Emperor, manage perception, deploy Japanese Americans as ideological counter-narrative — was strategic communication before the occupation began. Mabini, in 1903, had argued from the other side that coercive pacification cannot generate genuine confidence — the Filipino population would not internalize American authority simply because it was imposed. Constantino showed Mabini was half-right: coercion alone failed, but systematic education eventually succeeded as cultural conquest. Reischauer's "winning the peace" is the Japan-side planning for exactly the cultural project Constantino analyzed in the Philippines.

**Set 4: JCS 1067 / Dead Letter / Democratization Program**
*(Merritt W4RR — within-text, but connects to Atlantic Charter and Reischauer for the broader planning-practice gap)*
The Atlantic Charter set the ideological framework; Reischauer's memo illustrated wartime planning logic; JCS 1067 was the formal occupation directive for Germany — and it was "virtually a dead letter within months," as Merritt observes. Local commanders adapted to operational reality, entrenching anti-Nazi personnel, negotiating the Basic Law with German delegates who often rejected Allied suggestions, and eventually pivoting to Cold War alliance-building. The three-stage pattern — visionary framework, formal directive, on-the-ground adaptation — describes not just Germany but the structure of American occupation practice across all cases. Merritt's most useful contribution is demonstrating that the gap between planning and practice is not a failure but a feature.

**Set 5: Dangerous Symmetries / Pan-Asianism / Fradera**
*(Fradera W1RR + Reischauer W4PS + Duara W4RR — Week 1 through Week 4)*
Fradera argued that liberal empires propagate rights-language that colonized peoples turn against them — an irresolvable contradiction exhausting empires from within. Duara extends this into the 20th century: Japan's pan-Asianism was the most powerful instantiation, framing the Pacific War as a "holy crusade of yellow and brown peoples for freedom from the white race." American internment of Japanese Americans validated this frame, giving Japanese propaganda its strongest argument. Reischauer recognized the problem and proposed a counter-strategy — not by ending the racial structure but by managing its optics through Japanese American military service. The cycle Fradera described in the 19th century continued in the 20th: liberal empire propagates universal rights, colonial subjects weaponize those rights, empire manages the contradiction without resolving it.

## Essay Questions

1. Duara argues that the postwar United States represented "the apogee of the imperialism of free nations" — that the Atlantic Charter's self-determination principles and the actual client-state arrangements Reischauer planned for Japan were not in contradiction but structurally complementary. Using Duara, the Atlantic Charter, Reischauer, and Merritt, assess this claim. What evidence from the planning documents and from Germany's actual occupation either supports or complicates Duara's thesis? Is there a meaningful distinction between genuine democratization and client-state formation, or does Merritt's Germany case suggest the two can be indistinguishable in practice?

2. Merritt identifies two separate US goals in Germany — eradicating Nazism and building democratic institutions (substantially achieved) vs. "democratizing" the German people through attitudinal change (only partially achieved). Drawing on Merritt, and connecting forward to at least two earlier weeks (Beveridge/May for the Philippines context, or Constantino/Mabini), analyze whether US occupations have consistently shown this gap between institutional and cultural transformation. What does the pattern across cases suggest about the limits and possibilities of occupation as a tool of political change?

3. The Atlantic Charter's Point 3 promised "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live" — a principle that Mabini (Week 3) had invoked from the colonial side of US power, and that colonized peoples across Asia and Africa cited after 1941 to demand independence. Using the Atlantic Charter, Duara, and at least two prior-week readings (Fradera, Kramer, Mabini, or Constantino), analyze the career of self-determination as a concept across Weeks 1–4. What does it mean that the same principle could be simultaneously a colonial resistance tool (Mabini), an Allied war aim (Charter), and — in Duara's analysis — the ideological vehicle for a new form of imperial control?
