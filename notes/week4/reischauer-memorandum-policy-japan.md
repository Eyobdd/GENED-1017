## Summary (~200 words)

Edwin Reischauer's memorandum, dated September 14, 1942 — less than a year after Pearl Harbor — is a wartime planning document making two strategic arguments about how the US should approach postwar Japan. It is remarkable for its foresight, its frank instrumentalism, and its willingness to engage questions of race and empire that most wartime officials avoided.

Reischauer's first argument concerns the Emperor. Unlike Germany and Italy, Japan has no convenient scapegoat — no Hitler or Mussolini, no discredited party that the defeated population can repudiate to reassure itself that "the people" were not at fault. The Emperor cannot serve that function; military leadership is anonymous. Reischauer's solution is counterintuitive: preserve the Emperor as a "puppet" and future ally. If the US allows the Emperor to be portrayed as Japan's Hitler, it ruins his post-war utility. If the Emperor is kept "unsullied" and can be won to cooperation, he alone can lead the Japanese to repudiate their military leadership. Reischauer explicitly calls this a "puppet regime" and invokes Japan's own experience with puppet governments (notably Manchukuo) as the strategic model.

His second argument concerns Japanese Americans. The internment of Japanese Americans has handed Japan a propaganda gift — proof that the US wages a racial war. Reischauer argues the US should reverse this: enable Japanese Americans to serve in the armed forces, deploy them in the Pacific theater at war's end, and use their willing service as proof that the conflict is against Japan's "military clique," not the Japanese people. 100,000 Japanese American troops could be a "strategical advantage in the great struggle to win the peace in Asia."

The memo anticipates the actual structure of the US occupation of Japan: the Emperor was preserved, SWNCC 150 maintained the imperial institution, and Japanese Americans did serve in the 442nd Regimental Combat Team. Reischauer later became US Ambassador to Japan (1961–1966).

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Scapegoat problem**: Reischauer's diagnosis of Japan's unique post-war challenge: unlike Germany and Italy, Japan has no party or charismatic dictator the defeated population can repudiate to externalize blame. This structural absence shapes the entire occupation approach — the Emperor must be preserved because he is the only figure who can perform the necessary psychological function for the Japanese people.
- **"Winning the peace"**: Reischauer's phrase for the ideological-political challenge after military victory; he argues it requires as much strategic planning as military operations. This framing anticipates the SWNCC 150 language and the broader concept that occupation is a continuation of the war by other means.
- **The Emperor as puppet**: Reischauer explicitly uses the word "puppet" for the Emperor, drawing the parallel to Japan's own Manchukuo strategy. This is candid acknowledgment that the planned occupation arrangement — preserving imperial institutions while directing policy — is a form of indirect rule, not genuine self-determination.
- **Racial war / pan-Asianism**: Reischauer identifies Japan's framing of the Pacific War as a "holy crusade of yellow and brown peoples" against white supremacy as a dangerous propaganda strategy that the US was inadvertently validating through internment. His counter-strategy uses Japanese American military service to reframe the conflict.
- **Japanese American military service**: Reischauer's proposal that Japanese Americans serve in the armed forces — both as morale-building within the community and as strategic demonstration to Asia that the US does not wage racial war. The 442nd Regimental Combat Team was created in 1943, suggesting the memo had some influence.
- **"Unsullied" Emperor**: Reischauer's key prescription: wartime media must not associate Hirohito with Hitler and Mussolini, because doing so would make post-war cooperation impossible. This is a direct argument for managing American public opinion in service of occupation planning.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Fradera (W1)**: Fradera argued imperial powers deploy justificatory narratives that adapt to new conditions. Reischauer's memo is a masterclass in this: he argues for preserving Japanese imperial institutions (not dismantling them) in order to use them as instruments of American post-war influence — precisely the "indirect rule through existing structures" model Fradera identifies as a recurring imperial strategy.

- **Connects to Beveridge/Kramer (W2)**: Kramer showed how racial ideology shaped US imperial practice in the Philippines. Reischauer's memo shows racial ideology operating on two levels in the Pacific War: Japan's pan-Asianism as anti-white-supremacy propaganda, and America's internment as inadvertent confirmation of that propaganda. Reischauer's proposed solution — Japanese American soldiers — addresses the racial optics without challenging the underlying racial structure of US society, paralleling Kramer's analysis of how race is managed rather than resolved.

- **Connects to Mabini (W3)**: Mabini warned that US "benevolent" occupation concealed a project of control. Reischauer's frank language — "puppet," "strategical advantage," "our ideological war" — confirms Mabini's suspicion from the other side: the US was indeed thinking in terms of instrumentalized cooperation, not genuine Filipino (or Japanese) self-determination. The memo is the planning document for exactly the kind of occupation Mabini resisted.

- **Connects to Duara (W4RR)**: Duara argues the "imperialism of free nations" operates through client-state formation and the preservation (or creation) of nominally sovereign structures. Reischauer's proposal to use the Emperor as a "puppet" is the textbook application: maintain formal Japanese sovereignty and imperial institutions while directing policy from behind. Duara explicitly names postwar Japan as a US client-state in this formation. Reischauer is drafting the blueprint in 1942.

- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4PS)**: The Charter promises "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live." Reischauer's memo, written one year later, shows what that meant in practice for Japan: choose your government, but we will plan which institutions survive, who serves as your symbolic leader, and how your post-war cooperation with us is structured. The tension between the Charter's idealism and Reischauer's strategic realism is one of the defining tensions of Week 4.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Reischauer, Duara, Atlantic Charter**
The Atlantic Charter proclaimed self-determination for all peoples. Duara's framework shows that the "imperialism of free nations" deploys such rhetoric while building client-state control. Reischauer's 1942 memo is the planning document for the client-state arrangement: preserve the Emperor as a "puppet," manage Japanese psychology through strategic communication, and deploy Japanese Americans as ideological assets. All three texts show how liberal universalism (the Charter) and strategic imperialism (Reischauer, Duara) coexist without contradiction in American foreign policy.

**Set 2: Reischauer, Kramer, Beveridge**
Beveridge argued Filipinos were not yet capable of self-government — racial hierarchy justified American tutelage. Kramer showed how racial ideology shaped US colonial practice transnationally. Reischauer's memo reveals the same racial structure in WWII planning: Japan's pan-Asianism is dangerous *because* American racism (internment, exclusion laws) gives it credibility. The solution Reischauer proposes — Japanese American soldiers as demonstration of racial equality — is a strategic fix, not a structural remedy, mirroring the pattern Kramer identifies.

**Set 3: Reischauer, Merritt, SWNCC 150**
Merritt shows that JCS 1067's harsh prescriptions for Germany were "virtually a dead letter within months" — on-the-ground planners adapted pragmatically. Reischauer's memo anticipates the same pragmatic logic for Japan: harsh defeat must be managed carefully to preserve post-war utility. SWNCC 150 (W5PS) operationalized Reischauer's Emperor-preservation recommendation. Together the three texts show continuity between wartime planning, formal occupation directives, and actual practice.
