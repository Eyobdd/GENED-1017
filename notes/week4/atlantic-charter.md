## Summary (~200 words)

The Atlantic Charter is a joint declaration issued by President Roosevelt and Prime Minister Churchill on August 14, 1941 — four months before US entry into World War II — following their first personal meeting aboard a warship in Argentia Bay off Newfoundland. The document articulates eight principles for the postwar international order: no territorial aggrandizement; no territorial changes without the freely expressed wishes of affected peoples; the right of all peoples to choose their own form of government; equal access for all states to world trade and raw materials; improved labor standards and social security through international economic collaboration; post-Nazi peace enabling all nations to live within their borders free from fear and want; freedom of the seas; and global disarmament pending a "wider and permanent system of general security."

The Charter is the foundational ideological document for the WWII-era American occupation projects. Its Third Point — "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live" — would be invoked repeatedly in debates over occupation policy in Germany and Japan, and by colonized peoples worldwide claiming self-determination. The Soviet Union ultimately declined to sign, and Churchill was himself ambivalent; only Roosevelt, a Wilson administration veteran, fully believed in the "one world" vision the Charter represents. The gap between the Charter's promises and actual occupation practice is one of the central tensions of Weeks 4–7.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Self-determination (Point 3)**: The right of peoples to choose their own form of government; the most politically explosive principle in the Charter. Colonized peoples in Asia, Africa, and the Middle East cited it immediately; the US and Britain applied it selectively. Central to debates over Korea, Vietnam, and the meaning of occupation.
- **"Victor or vanquished" (Point 4)**: The Charter explicitly includes defeated nations in the postwar economic order — a direct repudiation of the Versailles punitive logic. This principle shaped the Marshall Plan logic and the rebuilding of Germany and Japan as economic partners rather than permanently weakened dependencies.
- **Freedom from fear and want**: Two of FDR's "Four Freedoms" (January 1941 State of the Union) embedded in Point 6; signals that the postwar order is not only about state sovereignty but individual welfare — the basis for social security, labor standards, and the welfare-state aspects of occupation reforms.
- **Wilsonianism**: The belief (explicitly attributed to Roosevelt in the Charter's introductory note) in international organization, democratic processes, and self-determination as the basis for lasting peace. The Charter is the WWII-era iteration; its fate parallels Wilson's Fourteen Points — widely invoked, selectively applied.
- **Lend-Lease context**: The Charter was issued months before US entry into the war, at a moment when the US was already supplying Britain under the Lend-Lease program; its idealism reflects Roosevelt's effort to articulate what the war was *for* even before formally joining it.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Fradera (W1)**: Fradera analyzed how imperial powers develop justificatory discourses that mask territorial ambitions. The Atlantic Charter is the WWII-era version: a statement of universal principles (self-determination, no aggrandizement) issued at the moment the US was preparing to become the dominant world power. Fradera would recognize the pattern — liberal ideology and imperial expansion advancing together.

- **Connects to Beveridge/Mabini (W2, W3)**: Beveridge justified US empire through civilizational hierarchy; Mabini resisted by appealing to universal rights and self-determination. The Charter, in Point 3, formally vindicates Mabini's position — yet the US occupation of Japan and Germany would raise the same questions Mabini raised about the Philippines: can an occupation genuinely respect self-determination while imposing institutional reforms?

- **Connects to Duara (W4RR)**: Duara argues the "imperialism of free nations" operates precisely through the language of self-determination and anticolonialism. The Atlantic Charter is the premier example: it declares no aggrandizement while planning for US-led postwar order. Duara's analysis of "limited self-determination" as tutelage (Lane's "leading-string law") shows the gap between Charter rhetoric and actual policy.

- **Connects to Reischauer memo (W4PS)**: Written a year after the Charter, Reischauer's memo shows how a US government planner thought about postwar Japan in strategic rather than self-determination terms — preserving the Emperor as a "puppet," using Japanese Americans as ideological assets. The memo's strategic logic sits in tension with the Charter's idealism, yet Reischauer would likely have claimed consistency: building a cooperative Japan was how the Charter's goals would be achieved.

- **Connects to JCS 1067 / SWNCC 150 (W5PS)**: The occupation directives for Germany and Japan are the operational translations of (some of) the Charter's principles — demilitarization, democratic governance, open economic access. But they also show where Charter principles were not applied: SWNCC 150 preserved the Emperor (not self-determination), and JCS 1067's initial harshness contradicted "victor or vanquished" equal treatment.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Atlantic Charter, Mabini, Reischauer**
Mabini in 1899 appealed to universal principles of self-determination to resist US occupation of the Philippines. The Atlantic Charter in 1941 formally proclaimed those same principles as Allied war aims. Yet Reischauer's 1942 memo planning for postwar Japan speaks entirely in strategic terms — preserving the Emperor as a puppet, managing Japanese psychology. The gap between the Charter's idealism and Reischauer's realism shows how "self-determination" operates as legitimating rhetoric while strategic control is planned simultaneously.

**Set 2: Atlantic Charter, Duara, Fradera**
Fradera showed how imperial powers generate justificatory discourses. Duara demonstrates that the "imperialism of free nations" specifically invokes anticolonial ideology — self-determination, sovereignty, development — to legitimate domination through client-states. The Atlantic Charter is the WWII instantiation: issued by the leaders who would plan the postwar order, it articulates universal principles (Points 2–3) while preparing for US global hegemony secured through military bases, the IMF, and the World Bank.

### Essay Questions (Part 2 practice)

1. The Atlantic Charter's Third Point states that all peoples have "the right to choose the form of government under which they will live." Drawing on the Charter alongside Merritt (Germany), Reischauer (Japan planning), and Duara, evaluate the extent to which US occupation policy in the immediate postwar period honored or violated this principle. Was the tension between self-determination and occupation reform a failure of US idealism, or was it structurally built into the Charter from the beginning?

2. The introductory note states that the Charter's "one world" vision did not appeal to Stalin, and Churchill was "not particularly thrilled" — only Roosevelt truly believed it. What does this tell us about the limits of liberal internationalism as an organizing framework for the postwar order? Use the Charter, Duara, and at least one other Week 1–4 reading to develop your argument.
