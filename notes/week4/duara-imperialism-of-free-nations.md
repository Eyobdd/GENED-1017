## Summary (~500 words)

Prasenjit Duara's chapter argues that the twentieth century produced a qualitatively new form of imperialism — the "imperialism of free nations" — which differs from both nineteenth-century European colonial imperialism and the older British "imperialism of free trade" (the latter term borrowed from Robinson and Gallagher). Duara's central claim is that powers such as Japan, the United States, and the Soviet Union pursued domination through legally sovereign client-states rather than formal colonies, espoused anticolonial ideologies, made heavy developmental investments in their dependencies, and organized their relationships around regional economic blocs aimed at global supremacy. He uses Manchukuo — the Japanese puppet state in northeast China (1932–1945) — as the first full-blown historical instance of this new imperialism, and argues it is the prototype for postwar American and Soviet imperial formations.

The argument has three interlocking layers. First, Duara traces the intellectual and structural conditions that produced the new imperialism: the rise of anti-imperialist nationalism after World War I (the March 1919 Korean movement, China's May Fourth movement), the discourse of multiple civilizations challenging the singular European "Civilizing Mission," and the logic of competitive nation-states seeking "strategic autarky" — economic self-sufficiency through regional blocs rather than extraction from backward colonies. Japan's contradictory position as both victim and perpetrator of Western racism generated pan-Asianism, which provided ideological cover for empire while compelling Japanese planners to invest genuinely in modernization (Manchukuo industrial production tripled 1933–1942; Japanese per-capita investment in Korea was $38 versus Britain's $8 in India in 1938).

Second, Duara anatomizes Manchukuo itself as a developmental client-state. The regime rested on two legitimacy claims: the "kingly way" (*wangdao*) as an expression of pan-Asian civilization, and the "concord of nationalities" (*minzoku kyowa*) promising multiethnic harmony under Japanese leadership. The Concordia Association managed this through a corporatist mobilization structure reminiscent of totalitarian parties. Yet Manchukuo also built modern banks, unified currency, invested in urban public health and education, and created the most industrialized zone in Asia outside Japan itself. The brutal underside — Unit 731 human experiments, massacres, land dispossession of Chinese farmers — coexisted with the developmental record. The key analytical point: this duality is not a contradiction but the structural logic of the new imperialism, which needed a productive client-state for regional competitive purposes.

Third, Duara extends the argument comparatively. The US practiced "imperialism without colonialism" in the Caribbean basin after 1898, using client-states, "free elections," and the Monroe Doctrine's logic of "limited self-determination" and tutelage — precisely the rhetoric Secretary of Interior Franklin Lane articulated in 1922. Woodrow Wilson declared no nation should impose its polity on another while simultaneously sending troops to the Dominican Republic. After World War II, Duara argues, the US became "the apogee of the imperialism of free nations": a global empire of military bases, the IMF, World Bank, and "political and military vassalage." Japan, postwar, itself became a US client-state in this new formation.

For this course, Duara's argument is crucial at Week 4 because it reframes the WWII occupations of Germany and Japan not as exceptional humanitarian projects but as episodes within a continuous history of imperial innovation. The "free nations" terminology allows Duara to expose how anticolonial rhetoric can serve as the ideological vehicle for domination — the same tension visible in the Atlantic Charter's self-determination principles and the actual occupation directives (JCS 1067, SWNCC 150). Duara also deepens Fradera's comparative framework: where Fradera showed the dangerous symmetries of late-nineteenth-century empires, Duara shows how WWII-era powers updated the toolkit by wrapping control in nation-state forms.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **New imperialism / "imperialism of free nations"**: Duara's term for the 20th-century form of empire exercised by the US, Soviet Union, and Japan — characterized by legally sovereign client-states, anticolonial ideology, developmental investment, and regional economic blocs; distinguished from both European colonial imperialism and Robinson-Gallagher's "imperialism of free trade."
- **Strategic autarky**: The idea, developed by Japanese military planners in the 1920s, that an imperial power needs a self-sufficient regional economic bloc for long-term global competition; the colony must be industrialized, not kept backward. The structural logic behind Manchukuo's developmental character.
- **Pan-Asianism**: Japanese ideological framework asserting Asian civilizational solidarity against Western imperialism; provided cover for Japanese empire by framing domination as brotherly leadership. Duara argues it was not simply a smokescreen but a genuinely contradictory ideology in which domination and solidarity coexisted.
- **Concordia Association** (*kyowakai*): The mass mobilization organization of Manchukuo, formally representing all nationalities but functioning as an instrument of the Kwantung Army; corporatist and surveillance-oriented, resembling fascist parties in Europe.
- **Kingly way** (*wangdao*): The Manchukuo regime's legitimacy claim rooted in classical Chinese ideal of benevolent rule; used alongside pan-Asian civilizational rhetoric to incorporate diverse constituencies (Confucian monarchists, redemptive societies) without the nation-state legitimacy the regime lacked.
- **Concord of nationalities** (*minzoku kyowa*): Manchukuo's claim to represent multiethnic harmony; Duara reads it as borrowing from Soviet nationality policy, designed to project legitimacy while the Concordia Association actually suppressed autonomy.
- **Tutelage / limited self-determination**: The ideological device used by the US (explicitly in the Philippines, implicitly in Latin America) to defer full self-determination indefinitely while maintaining client-state control; Duara links this to pan-Asian paternalism.
- **Ultraimperialism**: Term (from Carl Parrini) used by Duara to describe post-WWII US global empire: military bases + IMF/World Bank/GATT structures that coordinate cooperation among capitalist powers and manage developmental imperialism in the decolonized world.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Fradera (W1)**: Fradera showed dangerous symmetries across empires of the late 19th century — competitive imperial actors borrowing techniques from each other. Duara extends this into the 20th century, arguing that Japan, the US, and the Soviet Union similarly learned from each other (Manchukuo drew explicitly on US Monroe Doctrine and Soviet nationality policy). Both authors challenge "American exceptionalism" by embedding US practice in global imperial patterns.

- **Connects to Kramer/Beveridge (W2)**: Beveridge's "In Support of an American Empire" articulated the tutelage logic — Filipinos as a "people that has not yet learned the art of self-government." Duara shows this logic persists in Lane's 1922 "limited self-determination" formulation and the US client-state system in Latin America. Kramer's transnational history of racial formation connects to Duara's point that anti-Asian racism in the US (1922–1924 immigration exclusions) simultaneously fueled Japanese pan-Asianism and US credibility problems in Asia — the Reischauer memo (W4PS) makes exactly this point.

- **Connects to Mabini/May (W3)**: Mabini's resistance to US occupation and May's analysis of education as colonial instrument parallel Duara's discussion of Korean March 1919 nationalist uprising and Japan's "Cultural Rule" response. Both cases show occupied peoples generating nationalist resistance that forces imperial powers to adapt — from direct rule toward softer developmental and educational strategies. May's point about education creating collaborators resonates with the Concordia Association's enrollment of teachers and officials.

- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4PS)**: The Charter's Third Point — "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live" — is precisely what Duara shows the "imperialism of free nations" simultaneously professes and denies. Roosevelt's Wilsonianism was genuine at one level; at another level it was structurally continuous with the Monroe Doctrine tutelage the Charter's self-determination language was supposed to supersede.

- **Connects to Reischauer memo (W4PS)**: Reischauer's strategic argument for preserving the Emperor as a "puppet" usable in post-war Japan perfectly instantiates Duara's framework: the US was already planning, in 1942, a client-state relationship with postwar Japan rather than direct colonial rule — leveraging existing Japanese institutions of authority rather than dismantling them.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Duara, Beveridge, Mabini**
Beveridge justified Philippine annexation through civilizational hierarchy and tutelage — the colonized were not yet ready for self-government. Duara shows that this "limited self-determination" logic persisted into mid-20th century US client-state imperialism, becoming structurally indistinguishable from Japan's pan-Asian paternalism. Mabini's resistance to exactly this logic in 1899 anticipates the Korean March 1919 uprisings Duara analyzes — both forced imperial powers to adapt from direct rule toward subtler developmental strategies.

**Set 2: Duara, Atlantic Charter, Reischauer**
The Atlantic Charter proclaimed self-determination and sovereign rights for all peoples. Duara's framework explains why this proclamation was structurally compatible with continued US imperial practice: the "imperialism of free nations" operates precisely *through* sovereign nation-state forms rather than against them. Reischauer's 1942 memo concretizes the tension: he plans a postwar Japan built around a preserved Emperor — a client-state arrangement, not genuine self-determination — even as the Charter's rhetoric circulated globally.

**Set 3: Duara, Fradera, Kramer**
Fradera argued imperial powers in the late 19th century formed dangerous symmetries, learning techniques from each other. Kramer showed racial ideology crossing national boundaries. Duara extends both arguments: 20th-century imperial actors (Japan, US, USSR) openly borrowed each other's techniques — Japan's Manchukuo drew on Soviet nationality policy and US Monroe Doctrine logic; the US post-WWII empire drew on Japanese client-state models. Race ideology (pan-Asianism, US anti-Asian exclusion) structured both the competition between empires and their internal legitimacy claims.
