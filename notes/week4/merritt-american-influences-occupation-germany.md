## Summary (~500 words)

Richard Merritt's 1976 article provides a balanced retrospective assessment of American occupation policy in Germany between 1945 and 1949. Writing from the Free University of Berlin, Merritt argues that US policy pursued two overlapping but distinct goals: first, eradicating Nazism and building institutional frameworks for a stable democratic government; second, "democratizing" the German people — reshaping their political culture, habits, and values from the ground up. The first goal was substantially achieved; the second enjoyed mixed success and was significantly curtailed by the Cold War pivot.

Merritt opens by noting there was no single American policy toward occupied Germany. A "minimalist" faction (especially in the military) wanted only measures sufficient to end German belligerency; "maximalists" sought permanent subjugation or pastoralization (the Morgenthau Plan logic). Official policy, embodied in JCS 1067, sat between these poles — harsh in language but aimed at a peaceable, democratic outcome. Merritt's key analytical contribution is distinguishing the *structural* democratization program (institutional reform) from the *attitudinal* democratization program (changing minds), and arguing that the former had more durable success than the latter.

On the institutional side, Merritt traces denazification, demilitarization, and decentralization. The Nuremberg Trials presented documentary evidence of Nazi crimes and established international accountability norms. American denazification tribunals were more aggressive than British or French counterparts, though the program suffered from uneven application — harsher for those tried early, looser for later cases as Cold War logic demanded useful anti-Soviet Germans regardless of their Nazi past. The American Military Government (OMGUS) dismantled the Wehrmacht, broke up industrial cartels, licensed only democratic political parties, and banned Nazi symbolism. These measures entrenched anti-Nazi personnel in power by the time sovereignty was restored in 1949.

Building the Federal Republic proved more complicated. The quadripartite cooperation envisioned at Potsdam broke down rapidly; the US and Britain created a bizonal authority in 1947, and by 1948 the three Western powers were guiding the Parliamentary Council toward the Basic Law. American officials insisted on federalism, a "constructive vote of no confidence" (to prevent the cabinet instability that destroyed Weimar), a five percent threshold to block extremist parties, a bill of rights, and judicial independence. These were real institutional contributions, but Merritt carefully notes that German delegates were not passive — they often rejected Allied suggestions and shaped provisions on their own terms. The West Germans' subsequent reluctance to amend the Basic Law is, for Merritt, the best evidence that Allied reform succeeded: they accepted it because it reflected their own democratic aspirations.

The attitudinal democratization program — pamphlets, films, school reform, exchange programs, student councils, press independence training — was more fraught. Obstacles included centuries of ingrained habits, German cultural pride (few were willing to concede their school system was inferior to America's), and a fundamental tension Merritt names clearly: did not the effort to *indoctrinate* democracy itself contradict the democratic spirit? The program was also undermined by American hypocrisy — treatment of racial minorities, treatment of German prisoners at Katyn, General Patton's observation that the Nazi party was no different from the Democratic and Republican parties — which forceful Germans exploited. The Cold War pivot of 1948 was decisive: reform-minded Germans who had cooperated with OMGUS found themselves sidelined as American officials turned to conservative, anti-Soviet Germans instead.

Yet Merritt's overall verdict is cautiously optimistic. The cumulative effect of the American presence — including GI fraternization, the Berlin Airlift's demonstration of commitment, POW camp experiences, and the day-to-day behavior of soldiers who turned out to be "effective ambassadors of practical American democracy" — made a real contribution to West German democratic culture even if it cannot be precisely measured. JCS 1067's harsh prescriptions were "virtually a dead letter within months" as local commanders pragmatically adapted to what worked. This gap between formal policy and on-the-ground practice is one of the article's most useful observations for the course.

Merritt's article is essential for Week 4 because it gives the Germany case study texture: showing that even within a single occupation, there were fierce internal American debates about goals (punitive vs. reformist), and that the Cold War "reverse course" in Germany preceded — and anticipates — the better-known reverse course in Japan.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **OMGUS (Office of Military Government, United States)**: The American military government authority that administered the US zone of occupation in Germany, 1945–1949; responsible for denazification, institutional reform, and the democratization program. More aggressive than British or French equivalents.
- **Denazification**: Allied program to remove former Nazi party members and supporters from positions of authority; American tribunals were more thorough than other zones' but were applied unevenly — harsher early, relaxed as Cold War priorities shifted.
- **Minimalists vs. Maximalists**: Merritt's typology of American policy factions; minimalists sought only to end German belligerency, maximalists wanted permanent subjugation or pastoralization (Morgenthau Plan). Official policy (JCS 1067) lay between them.
- **Basic Law** (*Grundgesetz*): The West German constitution adopted in 1949, formally called "Basic Law" to signal its provisional character (pending reunification). Americans contributed key provisions: federalism, constructive vote of no confidence, five percent electoral threshold, bill of rights, judicial review.
- **Constructive vote of no confidence**: A provision inserted into the Basic Law at American suggestion requiring parliament to elect a replacement chancellor before forcing the resignation of an incumbent; designed to prevent the cabinet instability that helped destroy the Weimar Republic.
- **Democratization program**: The broader US effort, beginning in earnest 1946–1947, to reshape German political culture through schools, media, exchange programs, and civic organizations — distinguished from institutional reform and regarded by Merritt as only partially successful.
- **Cold War reversal (Germany)**: The shift beginning 1948, accelerated by the Berlin Blockade, in which the US prioritized West Germany as a Cold War ally over pursuing denazification and social reform; reform-minded Germans were sidelined for conservative, anti-Soviet alternatives.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Fradera (W1)**: Fradera traced how empires adapt their justifications across time. Merritt shows this in microcosm: within four years, Germany shifted from enemy requiring reform to ally requiring support. The "red thread" of American universalism Merritt identifies — manifest destiny, WWI/II democratic crusades — is exactly the ideological continuity Fradera theorizes at the macro level.

- **Connects to Kramer/Beveridge (W2)**: Beveridge's 1900 Senate speech argued Filipinos needed American tutelage; Merritt shows the same logic applied to Germans in 1945 — the conviction that "other peoples, freed from tyranny and able to make their own choice, would adopt a set of principles and practices that had proved successful in the United States." Both reveal the tension between democratic universalism and imperial condescension. Schurz's anti-imperialist objection that self-government cannot be exported also echoes in Merritt's honest assessment of the democratization program's limits.

- **Connects to May (W3)**: May analyzed American education policy in the colonial Philippines as a project of cultural transformation aimed at creating useful collaborators. Merritt's description of the German school reform program — introducing civics courses, student councils, replacing Nazi textbooks — operates on the same logic: education as the primary vehicle for long-term political-cultural change. Both cases showed the limits of imposed educational transformation and the resilience of locally shaped values.

- **Connects to Duara (W4RR)**: Duara argues that "free nations" imperialism invests in client-state modernization and uses institutional transfer rather than direct rule. Merritt's account of American Germany policy fits this model: rather than annexation or permanent occupation, the US built institutional structures (Basic Law, FRG), entrenched specific personnel, and then withdrew from formal governance — exactly the client-state formation Duara describes. The Cold War reversal in Germany also parallels Duara's observation that the US "imperialism of free nations" becomes developmentally oriented only when forced by strategic competition.

- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4PS)**: The Charter's principles — self-determination, no territorial aggrandizement — set the stated framework for Allied occupation policy. Merritt shows how those principles were both genuinely held and repeatedly compromised: Germans were not given self-determination but guided toward specific institutional forms, and the Cold War further distorted the reform agenda. The Charter is the idealistic statement; JCS 1067 and OMGUS practice are the operational reality.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Merritt, Beveridge, Duara**
Beveridge justified Philippine annexation through tutelage — Americans would teach Filipinos self-government. Merritt shows the same logic in Germany: Americans sought to "democratize" a people through education, exchange programs, and institutional imposition. Duara explains why both projects are structural imperialism: the "imperialism of free nations" operates through institutional transfer and client-state formation rather than formal colonialism, espousing anticolonial ideologies while maintaining effective control.

**Set 2: Merritt, Atlantic Charter, JCS 1067**
The Atlantic Charter promised self-determination and no territorial changes without the consent of affected peoples. JCS 1067 (the actual occupation directive for Germany) imposed harsh controls, banned parties, licensed publishers, and directed a thoroughgoing social transformation. Merritt navigates this tension: official American leaders "saw their actions as the only possible way to preserve American constitutional principles... and to give other peoples the opportunity to choose their own form of political, social, and economic life" — but the means of giving that opportunity was itself a form of imposition.

**Set 3: Merritt, Mabini, Constantino**
Mabini resisted American occupation of the Philippines as incompatible with genuine self-determination. Constantino argued that American education produced Filipinos who thought like Americans — "miseducated." Merritt shows German resistance to the same dynamic: German educators rejected the implicit claim that their system was inferior, and German politicians freely disregarded Allied suggestions they found inappropriate. The occupied peoples in both cases were not passive recipients of American values.

### Essay Questions (Part 2 practice)

1. Merritt argues that JCS 1067 "was virtually a dead letter within months" and that on-the-ground occupation officials exercised significant discretion. What does this gap between formal policy directives and actual practice tell us about how occupations work? Draw on Merritt alongside the Atlantic Charter and Reischauer's memo to assess how much the planning phase shapes actual occupation outcomes.

2. Merritt identifies a "Cold War reversal" in Germany in 1948: the US pivoted from democratization to alliance-building, sidelining reform-minded Germans for conservative anti-Soviet partners. Compare this pattern to what Duara argues about the structural tension between "enlightenment" and "self-interest" in the imperialism of free nations. Is the Cold War reversal an exception to American democratic idealism, or does Duara's framework suggest it was always structurally likely?
