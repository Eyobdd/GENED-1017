## Summary (~200 words)

Apolinario Mabini was the leading intellectual of the Philippine Revolution and a close adviser to Emilio Aguinaldo. Written after his return from exile in Guam (c. 1903), the "Introductory Manifesto" is his personal account of why he resisted unconditional recognition of American sovereignty and what led him ultimately to take the oath of allegiance. It is one of the most significant articulations of Filipino nationalist opposition to American rule.

Mabini records his negotiations with Generals MacArthur and Bell, who demanded he unconditionally recognize American sovereignty in exchange for contributing to "pacification." He refused, arguing that doing so would strip him of credibility among Filipinos and make him useless as a genuine peace mediator. He frames his resistance in natural law terms: sovereignty resides in the people themselves, and an oath contrary to natural law is spiritually and legally void. He ultimately takes the oath not from conviction but from necessity — to return to his people. His closing argument is pointed: the United States will only secure lasting peace if it recognizes that its sovereignty was imposed, not consented to, and that stability depends on fulfilling promises of self-government.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Natural Law Sovereignty**: Mabini's philosophical framework — authority resides inherently in the people; any externally imposed sovereignty is contrary to natural order and cannot generate binding moral obligations.
- **Pacification**: American military-political term for suppressing Filipino resistance; Mabini exposes the coercive logic beneath this bureaucratic language.
- **Oath of Allegiance**: Required condition for return from exile in Guam; Mabini's extended analysis of whether he can legitimately take it becomes the Manifesto's central philosophical drama.
- **Philippine Revolution (1896–1902)**: The independence struggle Mabini helped lead, first against Spain, then against the United States; his account insists it expressed genuine popular will, not elite conspiracy.
- **Irreconcilables**: American military term for Filipino leaders (Mabini, Aguinaldo) who refused to recognize U.S. sovereignty; their deportation is what Mabini reflects on.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Beveridge (W2PS)**: Beveridge argued Filipinos were "not capable of self-government." Mabini is the direct rebuttal — a sophisticated political philosopher articulating natural law theory, demonstrating exactly the capacity Beveridge denied.
- **Connects to Worcester (W3PS)**: Worcester insists the Philippine insurgent government was a "military oligarchy" that never had popular consent. Mabini insists the Revolution expressed "the ungratified aspirations of the people." They offer irreconcilable narratives of the same events.
- **Connects to Kramer (W2RR)**: Kramer's analysis of race and empire helps explain why Mabini's arguments — however philosophically rigorous — were dismissed by American generals as "pretext." Racial ideology rendered Filipino political reasoning illegible to American commanders.
- **Connects to Fradera (W1RR)**: Fradera's discussion of how imperial powers manage challenges to their legitimacy illuminates the American response to Mabini: deportation rather than negotiation, which Mabini himself predicts would "only serve to excite hatred."
- **Connects to NYT/Smith (W3PS)**: The Smith "kill and burn" order and Mabini's manifesto are two faces of the same moment — one shows what American "pacification" looked like on the ground; the other shows the Filipino moral and intellectual response to it.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 Practice)

**Set 1: Natural Law / American Sovereignty / Beveridge**
Beveridge grounded American empire in civilizational duty; Mabini challenged it on natural law grounds — asserting that authority resides in the people themselves, not in a conquering power's self-appointed mission. Their confrontation defines the ideological core of the Philippine occupation debate.

**Set 2: Pacification / Deportation / Resistance**
The American strategy of deporting "irreconcilables" like Mabini, which was meant to enable pacification, instead — as Mabini predicted — prolonged hostilities by removing the moderates most capable of brokering genuine peace. This pattern of coercion undermining its own goals appears across multiple U.S. occupations.

**Set 3: Mabini / Worcester / Self-Government**
Worcester argued Philippine independence was not yet "possible" due to Filipino unreadiness; Mabini demonstrated through the Manifesto itself that Filipinos were entirely capable of sophisticated political reasoning — the debate was never about capacity but about power.

### Essay Questions (Part 2 Practice)

1. "The Philippine-American War ended not because Filipinos accepted American rule but because they ran out of resources to resist it." How does Mabini's Manifesto support or complicate this claim? What does his account reveal about the nature of colonial consent?

2. Compare Mabini's and Worcester's views on Filipino capacity for self-government. What assumptions does each bring, and how do those assumptions shape their very different conclusions about what American rule in the Philippines should look like?
