## Summary (~200 words)

Renato Constantino was one of the leading Philippine nationalist historians of the twentieth century. "The Miseducation of the Filipino" (originally published 1966) argues that the American colonial education system was not a gift of literacy and democracy but a sophisticated instrument of conquest — designed to pacify resistance, Americanize Filipino minds, and secure colonial economic control by shaping the values of the governed.

Constantino's central argument is that the educational system succeeded precisely where May's analysis shows it failed economically: its true purpose was cultural, not vocational. By making English the medium of instruction, importing American teachers and textbooks, and systematically suppressing nationalist heroes while celebrating American ones, the system produced a generation that thought and acted "like little Americans." The result, in Constantino's pointed phrase, was that "in exchange for a smattering of English, we yielded our souls." The Jones Act's provision keeping the Department of Education under American control — even as other government departments were Filipinized — was, he argues, not coincidental but structural: education was the last instrument the colonizers would relinquish.

The piece also identifies economic attitudes fostered by colonial education: Filipinos were taught to regard themselves as primarily agricultural, making them passive toward industrialization and dependent on export of raw materials to the United States.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Miseducation**: Constantino's central concept — not lack of education but education designed to serve the colonizer's interests by producing colonial subjects incapable of nationalist resistance.
- **Thomasites**: The approximately 600 American teachers who arrived on the USS Thomas in July 1901; Constantino's "second army of occupation" — key symbol of educational colonialism.
- **English as Wedge**: Constantino's metaphor for the function of English-language instruction — separating Filipinos from their pre-colonial past and from non-English-speaking countrymen, creating class divisions rooted in colonial access.
- **Pacification through Education**: The explicit logic of General MacArthur's school appropriation request — education as a military tool, not a humanitarian one.
- **Jones Act (1916)**: U.S. legislation granting more Filipino political autonomy; Constantino notes it still reserved the education department for American leadership, showing which instrument of control was considered most essential.
- **Carbon Copy Colonial**: Constantino's term for the ideal product of colonial education — a Filipino who has internalized American values so thoroughly that coercive oversight is no longer necessary.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to May (W3RR)**: May shows the economic industrial education program failed; Constantino shows what succeeded instead — cultural Americanization. Together they offer complementary analyses of colonial education: May explains what the bureau tried and couldn't do; Constantino explains what it wasn't trying to do but accomplished anyway.
- **Connects to Worcester (W3PS)**: Worcester celebrates English-language education as a gift; Constantino shows it was a weapon. Their texts are in direct dialogue — Worcester produces the colonial justification, Constantino provides the nationalist critique.
- **Connects to Mabini (W3PS)**: Mabini's fear — that colonial rule would undermine Filipino political identity and nationalist will — is exactly what Constantino documents as outcome. Mabini predicted cultural subjugation; Constantino analyzes how it was accomplished.
- **Connects to Beveridge (W2PS)**: Beveridge's rhetoric of civilizing mission is the ideological cover for what Constantino shows was an extractive political project. "Benevolent assimilation" is the marketing; "miseducation" is the product.
- **Connects to Kramer (W2RR)**: Kramer's analysis of race and empire examines how racial ideology shaped American colonial structures; Constantino provides the Filipino-centered perspective on what those structures actually produced in terms of consciousness and identity.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 Practice)

**Set 1: Miseducation / Pacification / MacArthur**
Constantino demonstrates that General MacArthur's explicit framing of school appropriations as "adjunct to military operations" was not rhetorical excess but accurate description — the educational system was designed from inception as a pacification tool, making miseducation inseparable from colonial military strategy.

**Set 2: English Wedge / Cultural Displacement / Mabini**
Mabini feared that American rule would undermine Filipino nationalist will; Constantino shows the precise mechanism — English-language instruction that "separated Filipinos from their past" and produced a generation whose nationalist heroes were Washington and Lincoln rather than Rizal and the Katipuneros.

**Set 3: Second Army / Thomasites / Worcester**
Worcester celebrates the school system as American philanthropy; Constantino's "second army of occupation" label for the Thomasites reframes the same historical fact as colonial invasion. The contest between these metaphors is the contest between colonial apologia and nationalist history.
