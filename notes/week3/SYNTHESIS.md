# Week 3 Synthesis: Governing Others — The Philippines
**Case Study:** Philippines
**Lectures:** Governing Filipinos / (Re)building the Nation

---

## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 3 is the course's most densely sourced week on a single case, and the readings are in genuine dialogue — sometimes explicit, always productive. The structural tension is between two modes of evaluation: Worcester's colonial apologia and the nationalist critique assembled from Mabini, Constantino, the NYT/Smith article, and May's analytical history. But the week's richest insights come not from that binary but from the triangulations within each camp and across them.

**The Worcester-Mabini axis** is the week's primary source debate. Worcester and Mabini give irreconcilable accounts of the same events: Worcester calls the Philippine revolutionary government a "military oligarchy" maintained by terrorism; Mabini insists it expressed "the ungratified aspirations of the people." Worcester says independence was never promised; Mabini says natural law makes external sovereignty void regardless of promises. Worcester celebrates American benevolence; Mabini predicts that coercive governance will fail to produce consent. What is significant is that both men are operating at the level of political philosophy — this is not a debate about facts but about the framework through which facts are interpreted. Worcester's framework is stewardship (Filipinos as wards to be prepared for future self-government); Mabini's is natural law (authority resides in the people themselves and cannot be legitimately imposed from without). These frameworks are incommensurable, and the week as a whole demonstrates why.

**The NYT/Smith article is Worcester's direct empirical refutation.** Worcester writes, in 1914, that "never before have the officers and men of any civilized nation conducted so humanely a war" as the US in the Philippines. Secretary Root's letter in the NYT, written in 1902, confirms Smith's guilt while simultaneously deploying "cruel and treacherous savages" language to justify the broader Samar campaign. The Smith case — a court-martial producing only retirement, celebrated in Root's letter as "really a severe punishment" — is not an aberration but a demonstration of how the colonial state managed accountability: by individualizing excess (Smith's intemperate *language*) while immunizing systemic policy. Read together, Worcester and the NYT article show that colonial apologia and colonial violence were not contradictions but partners: the apologia required the violence to be invisible, and the accountability mechanism (court-martial → retirement) made it so.

**May and Constantino are structurally complementary.** May argues that the industrial education program failed to achieve its stated economic objectives: average basketry output worth under two pesos per student per year, the Monroe Commission damning the program in 1925, only embroidery showing qualified success — and even that extracting most value upward to American textile manufacturers. Constantino argues that colonial education succeeded as a system of cultural conquest: it produced Americanized minds that no longer required coercive oversight because they had internalized colonial values. The two arguments are not in tension — they describe different registers of the same program. May shows what the Bureau of Education tried economically and failed to do; Constantino shows what it wasn't explicitly trying to do but accomplished anyway. Together they generate the synthesis neither provides alone: colonial education was an economic failure that was simultaneously a cultural success, and the cultural success is what made the economic failure politically sustainable.

**Worcester and Constantino are in the most explicit direct dialogue** of the week. Worcester celebrates the spread of English as "spoken far more generally than Spanish was after three and a half centuries" — treating linguistic reach as straightforward achievement. Constantino's entire argument is that this was not an achievement for Filipinos but a mechanism of conquest: "the wedge that separated the Filipinos from their past and later was to separate educated Filipinos from the masses of their countrymen." The same historical fact — English displaced Spanish as the dominant colonial language — generates opposite evaluations depending on whose interests are centered. Worcester's "We have given" rhetoric collapses before Constantino's "we yielded our souls."

**Mabini and the NYT/Smith article are two faces of the same conjuncture.** Mabini's Manifesto, written c. 1903, argues that coercive pacification — deportation, military campaigns, forced oaths — cannot produce the genuine confidence that is the only basis for stable governance. The Samar campaign is precisely the coercion Mabini warned against, and Smith's mild punishment shows the state's incapacity to police its own violence honestly. Both documents also illuminate the role of racial language: Root's "savages" in the NYT mirrors the civilizationist vocabulary Beveridge used in Week 2, showing that racial dehumanization operated not just in the Senate chamber but in official military-legal documents managing colonial atrocity.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 1 (Fradera, "Reading Imperial Transitions"):**

1. **Dangerous symmetries → Mabini.** Fradera's most powerful concept is that liberal empires generate anticolonial opposition by teaching their subjects the language of rights — the contradiction is structural and irresolvable. Mabini's Manifesto is the perfect instantiation: a Filipino nationalist using natural law theory (the philosophical tradition the American republic itself rests on) to argue that American sovereignty was imposed, not consented to, and therefore illegitimate. The US taught Filipino elites to think in terms of universal rights; Mabini turned those tools against the occupation. This is exactly Fradera's "dangerous symmetry" — the colonizer's philosophical heritage deployed against the colonizer.

2. **Special regimes → May and the Bureau of Education.** Fradera defines the modern empire by its "special regimes" — formal construction of inferior rules for colonial populations within an otherwise liberal system. May's account of the Bureau of Education shows what this looks like institutionally: a school system explicitly modeled on American programs for "backward races" (African Americans, Native Americans), then transplanted to the Philippines and expanded to a scale that "dwarfed" anything in the US. The Philippines was not just administered differently but was made the test site for social engineering experiments that could not have been conducted domestically.

3. **Exceptionality → Smith case.** The court-martial that produced only a retirement for a "kill and burn" order is exceptionality in its punitive mode: formal accountability structures exist (military law, command responsibility) but are applied in a manner calibrated to protect the colonial enterprise rather than punish colonial violence. Root's logic — the orders were not *meant* literally, the subordinates who ignored them deserve credit — shows how imperial exceptionality operates as a legitimation mechanism even when adjudicating atrocity.

**Week 2 (Kramer, Beveridge, Schurz, Teller/Platt):**

1. **Imperial racial grammar → May and the NYT/Smith article.** Kramer argues that American colonial racial ideology was assembled from multiple sources (domestic Jim Crow, British imperial precedents, Spanish colonial archives) and constituted a shared grammar that both imperialists and anti-imperialists operated within. May provides the institutional evidence: "backward races" reasoning was explicitly invoked to justify industrial (rather than academic) education for Filipinos, with administrators citing its use for African Americans and Native Americans. Root's "savages" language in the NYT article shows the same grammar operating in a judicial proceeding — racial dehumanization as exculpatory argument.

2. **Benevolent assimilation → Worcester and Constantino.** Beveridge's "benevolent assimilation" rhetoric finds its most elaborated administrative expression in Worcester, who catalogs American gifts (sewers, artesian wells, roads, schools, English) as evidence of historically unprecedented colonial benevolence. Constantino's analysis is the nationalist counter-reading of exactly these "gifts": the schools were a "second army of occupation"; English was the wedge that separated Filipinos from their past; the Department of Education was the last colonial institution to be Filipinized because it was the most essential instrument of control.

3. **Filipino agency → Mabini.** Kramer's insistence that colonized Filipinos were not passive objects but active agents who shaped, appropriated, and contested colonial racial structures finds its most vivid primary source illustration in Mabini. His negotiations with Generals MacArthur and Bell — refusing unconditional recognition of sovereignty, analyzing the philosophical basis of his resistance, predicting the consequences of coercive governance — are not the behavior of a man who accepted the racial grammar of incapacity. He is engaging American political philosophy on its own terms and finding it wanting. The American response was deportation to Guam — the colonial state's answer to Filipino intellectual agency it could not dismiss as primitive resistance.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 7 (Education and cultural transformation)** is the week's dominant theme. Three of five readings address the colonial school system directly, and the Discussion Questions center on it. The key analytical move this week is to see education not as one colonial policy among others but as the site where WHAT Americans did and WHY they did it converge most revealingly. May shows the economic engineering function; Constantino shows the cultural conquest function; Worcester shows how it was self-represented as philanthropy; Mabini shows what Filipino nationalists feared it would do.

**Theme 5 (Race, civilization, and imperial ideology)** is active throughout and essential for connecting Weeks 2 and 3. May's "backward races" institutionalizes Beveridge's rhetoric; Root's "savages" in the NYT deploys it as legal-military exculpation; Worcester's "Filipino wards" deploys it as paternalistic benevolence; Constantino's "carbon copy colonial" inverts it as nationalist critique. Race is not background context this week — it is the operating logic of colonial governance at every level.

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is the Mabini-Worcester axis. The week's primary sources together define the deepest question: who decides when a people is ready for self-government? Worcester's answer is the occupier (and his judgment is that the legislature was created "prematurely"). Mabini's answer is that this question is structurally unanswerable within colonial logic — sovereignty resides in the people themselves, and cannot be delegated to or withheld by an external power.

**Theme 8 (Collaboration and resistance)** — Mabini embodies principled resistance; the oath of allegiance he ultimately takes reveals the coercive context in which "consent" to colonial rule was produced. The Smith case shows what armed resistance against "pacification" looked like and how the colonial state responded. The week as a whole raises the question of whether resistance and accommodation were genuine alternatives or a continuum of responses to structural conditions that offered no real exit.

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy — punitive vs. reformist)** appears in the tension between the school system (reformist ambition) and the Samar campaign (punitive reality). The Smith case forces recognition that these were not competing approaches but simultaneous ones — the "second army of occupation" in the schoolrooms and the actual army in Samar were operating at the same time, in the same colonial project.

## Combined Key Terms

- **Miseducation** (Constantino, pp. 45-49): Not absence of education but education designed to serve the colonizer's interests — producing colonial subjects who had internalized American values and lost nationalist will. The concept insists that the form of schooling can be more significant than its content.
- **Thomasites** (Constantino, p. 46): The ~600 American teachers arriving on the USS Thomas in July 1901; Constantino's "second army of occupation" — the most economical phrase for the connection between education and conquest.
- **English as Wedge** (Constantino, p. 47): English-language instruction as double displacement — separating Filipinos from pre-colonial history and from non-English-speaking countrymen, producing class divisions rooted in colonial access.
- **Industrial Education** (May, pp. 151-162): American colonial policy priority (1909-30) emphasizing manual training and handicraft production over academic instruction; explicitly rooted in "backward races" reasoning applied domestically to African Americans and Native Americans.
- **Commercialization / Standardization** (May, p. 152): Frank White's principles for the Bureau of Education — produce salable articles, enforce uniform quality — that transformed schools into colonial economic infrastructure.
- **Monroe Commission (1925)** (May, pp. 157-160): External review that indicted industrial education as educationally and economically inadequate; the colonial program's own evaluators condemned it.
- **Natural Law Sovereignty** (Mabini): The philosophical argument that authority resides inherently in the people themselves; externally imposed sovereignty is morally void regardless of military outcome. The intellectual foundation of Mabini's resistance.
- **Irreconcilables** (Mabini): American military term for Filipino leaders who refused to recognize US sovereignty; their deportation removed the moderates most capable of genuine peace-brokering, validating Mabini's prediction.
- **"Kill and Burn" Order** (NYT/Smith): General Smith's instruction to make Samar a "howling wilderness," killing all males over ten capable of bearing arms; the defining American atrocity of the Philippine-American War.
- **Command Responsibility (circumvented)** (NYT/Smith): Root's review held Smith accountable only for intemperate verbal expression, not operational policy — the colonial state's accountability mechanism calibrated to protect the enterprise while performing justice.
- **Benevolent Assimilation** (Worcester; connects to W2): McKinley's policy framework expressed through Worcester's administrative catalog of American gifts; challenged this week on multiple fronts simultaneously (Constantino, Mabini, NYT/Smith).
- **"Filipino Wards"** (Worcester, pp. 921-932): Worcester's preferred term positioning Filipinos as legal/moral dependents of American governance; the guardian-ward relationship that structures the entire colonial ideology of "preparing for self-government."
- **Stewardship** (Worcester, p. 933): The framing of American colonial rule as holding the Philippines in trust — temporary, benevolent, oriented toward eventual return. Challenged by Mabini's natural law argument (sovereignty cannot be held in trust by a conqueror) and by Worcester's own conditional threat ("we can take away as well as give").
- **Military Oligarchy** (Worcester, p. 922): Worcester's characterization of Aguinaldo's government — designed to delegitimize the Philippine Republic as tyranny, justifying American intervention as liberation. Directly contested by Mabini's account of the Revolution as popular nationalist expression.
- **"Backward Races"** (May, p. 152; connects to Kramer W2RR): The racialized category used by American educators to justify industrial rather than academic instruction for non-white populations; applied simultaneously to Filipinos, African Americans, and Native Americans, making the Philippines a colonial extension of domestic racial governance.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: Miseducation / Stewardship / Natural Law** *(Constantino + Worcester + Mabini)*
Worcester's "stewardship" framing held that Americans were preparing Filipinos for eventual self-government — which required controlling the Department of Education last, as Constantino shows, because cultural formation was the most essential instrument of colonial continuity. But Mabini's natural law argument demolishes the stewardship premise: sovereignty cannot be "held in trust" by a conquering power, because it resides inherently in the people themselves. These three concepts together trace the ideological architecture of American colonial governance and its foundational contradiction — you cannot prepare a people for self-government by ensuring they cannot think or govern as themselves.

**Set 2: Kill and Burn / Benevolent Assimilation / Pacification** *(NYT/Smith + Worcester + Mabini)*
McKinley's "benevolent assimilation" doctrine — Worcester's operating ideology — required that American governance be represented as a gift, not a conquest. The Smith "kill and burn" order on Samar, culminating in a presidential reprimand rather than punishment, shows what pacification looked like when the gift was refused. Mabini had predicted precisely this dynamic: coercive pacification cannot produce genuine confidence, and confident Filipino resistance will continue as long as the underlying injustice of imposed sovereignty remains unaddressed. The colonial state's solution — retire Smith, protect the institutional narrative — was accountability as legitimation strategy.

**Set 3: Industrial Education / Backward Races / Imperial Racial Grammar** *(May + Kramer W2RR + Beveridge W2PS)*
Beveridge's "God has made us master organizers" rhetoric found its institutional form in the Bureau of Education's industrial curriculum, which May shows was explicitly derived from American programs for "backward races" — African Americans and Native Americans — and then expanded to a scale that dwarfed any US model. Kramer's "imperial racial grammar" names the shared premise that made this transfer seamless: Filipinos, like all non-white populations in the American racial framework, were suited for manual labor and vocational training rather than academic development. May's economic failure narrative exposes the internal contradiction: a program premised on racial incapacity produced results so inadequate that the colonizer's own commission condemned it.

**Set 4: Dangerous Symmetries / Mabini / Worcester** *(Fradera W1RR + Mabini + Worcester)*
Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" — liberal empires propagate rights-language that colonized peoples deploy against them — is enacted directly in the Mabini-Worcester confrontation. Worcester insists that independence was never promised and that Filipino governance was tyranny; Mabini deploys the American republic's own natural law foundations to argue that authority resides in the people and that a sovereignty imposed against their will cannot generate binding moral obligations. The American response to Mabini's philosophically rigorous argument was deportation to Guam — the colonial state's acknowledgment that it had no answer within the terms of the argument and therefore had to remove the arguer.

**Set 5: English Wedge / Exceptionality / Carbon Copy Colonial** *(Constantino + Fradera W1RR + Constantino)*
Fradera shows that modern empires construct "special regimes" — formal inferiority of rights for colonial populations within otherwise liberal systems — and that the most stable form of colonial exceptionality is one the governed population internalizes rather than merely accepts under coercion. Constantino's analysis describes exactly this achievement: colonial education produced the "carbon copy colonial" — a Filipino who had absorbed American values, forgotten nationalist heroes, and ceased to require external enforcement of colonial order. The mechanism was English-language instruction as the "wedge" separating Filipinos from their pre-colonial past. The most effective special regime is the one that makes itself invisible by making the colonized desire their own colonization.

## Essay Questions

1. Week 3 presents three distinct modes of evaluating American colonial governance in the Philippines: colonial apologia (Worcester), nationalist critique (Mabini, Constantino), and critical social history (May). What are the stakes of choosing among these frameworks? Using at least three readings from Week 3 and at least one from Weeks 1–2, argue for the most analytically useful way to evaluate the legacy of American rule in the Philippines — and explain what the other frameworks illuminate and obscure.

2. "The most durable form of colonial control is the kind that makes external coercion unnecessary." Using Constantino's miseducation argument, May's account of industrial education, and at least one prior week reading (Fradera or Kramer), assess whether American colonial education in the Philippines achieved this outcome. What is the evidence for and against the claim that cultural conquest succeeded where economic engineering failed?

3. Both the Smith "kill and burn" case and Mabini's Manifesto suggest that American "pacification" in the Philippines was structurally self-defeating — that coercion foreclosed the genuine consent that is the only basis for stable colonial governance. Does the evidence from Week 3 support this argument? What would Worcester say in response, and is his response persuasive?
