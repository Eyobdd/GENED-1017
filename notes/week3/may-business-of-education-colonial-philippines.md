## Summary (~500 words)

Glenn Anthony May's essay examines the American colonial government's "industrial education" program in the Philippines from 1909 to 1930, arguing that despite enormous scale and administrative ambition, the program largely failed to achieve its stated economic objectives — and that this failure illuminates broader patterns in how colonial social engineering operates. May's thesis runs on three tracks: that Philippine policies were adapted from American domestic models; that once transplanted they expanded far beyond those models; and that, as in most modern empires, such attempts at social engineering only infrequently achieved their stated goals.

The program centered on teaching Filipino schoolchildren handicrafts (hat-making, lace-making, basketry, embroidery), farming techniques, and domestic science — collectively called "industrial education." When Frank White replaced David Barrows as head of the Bureau of Education in 1909, he reoriented the system around three principles: commercialization (making salable articles), standardization (enforcing uniform quality bureau-wide), and scale. By 1910 some 350,000 pupils were engaged in industrial work in primary schools alone; by 1924 the figure reached approximately 900,000 — dwarfing any American school district. May shows this made the Philippine system structurally resemble a "mammoth business operation," with Manila administrators acting as managers, local teachers as supervisors, and a million pupil-workers producing to bureau specification.

May uses two case studies — corn and basketry — to illustrate the gap between ambition and outcome. The corn campaign (1912–1916) attempted to shift Filipino dietary habits away from rice, modeled on U.S. rural 4-H movements; it attracted transient public interest but flagged within three years and was quietly discontinued. Basketry was elevated on the assumption that Filipino craft skill could be commercialized for export markets; instead the bureau discovered Filipino traditions lacked "commercial and artistic value" by its own standards, substituted externally-designed patterns (including Native American and West Indian designs), and never cracked U.S. markets — the average student output in basketry was worth less than two pesos per year. Only embroidery achieved qualified success, generating $7.8 million in exports by 1920 — but most profits flowed to American textile producers because duty-free status required use of U.S.-manufactured fabrics.

The 1925 Monroe Commission delivered a damning verdict: students were unenthusiastic, skills were not used after school, and returns were "inadequate, both economically and educationally." Industrial instruction subsequently declined; by the late 1920s only about half of pupils received it at all.

For the course's central questions, May's reading is essential on the WHAT of American colonial governance. Education was not merely benevolent uplift but a vector for economic engineering — the schools were designed to serve commodity markets and colonial development goals, not Filipino self-determination. May's framing connects directly to the racial logic underpinning the program: industrial education in the U.S. was explicitly linked to the instruction of "backward races," and colonial administrators applied this framework wholesale. That the program failed so visibly — "more about activity than accomplishment" — is itself analytically important: it demonstrates that colonial governance was neither as coherent nor as effective as its propagandists claimed, and that "social engineering in the American colonies only infrequently achieved the policy makers' stated objectives."

This reading pairs tightly with Constantino (W3PS), who argues that even the program's softer cultural outcomes — producing Americanized minds — succeeded where the economic ones failed.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Industrial Education**: American colonial policy priority (1909–30) emphasizing manual training, handicraft production, and vocational skills over academic instruction; rooted in racial assumptions about non-white capacity for "purely academic" study.
- **Commercialization**: Frank White's principle that school industrial work should produce "serviceable and salable" articles; reframed schools as factories serving markets rather than pupils.
- **Standardization**: Bureau-mandated uniformity of product quality, enforced via circulars, inspectors, and grade consequences; a disciplinary mechanism as much as a quality-control one.
- **Philippine Craftsman**: Bureau-published monthly magazine (1912–1917) promoting industrial education standards; functions as a propaganda vehicle for the colonial program.
- **Monroe Commission (1925)**: External review of Philippine education led by Paul Monroe of Columbia Teachers College; delivered an indictment of industrial education as educationally and economically inadequate, triggering reform.
- **Bureau of Education (Philippines)**: Colonial administrative body overseeing all public schooling in the archipelago; operated as a centralized policy authority with no local counterpart in U.S. schooling.
- **"Backward races"**: Racialized category used by American educators to justify industrial (rather than academic) instruction for non-white populations, explicitly applied to Filipinos, African Americans, and Native Americans.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Kramer (W2RR)**: Kramer's "race, empire, and transnational history" framework explains WHY industrial education targeted Filipinos specifically — racial ideology cast them as suited only for manual work. May provides the operational on-the-ground evidence for what Kramer theorizes.
- **Connects to Beveridge (W2PS)**: Beveridge argued Americans had a duty to "civilize" Filipinos; May shows how that civilizing mission was institutionalized through schooling — and reveals its economic extractive function beneath benevolent rhetoric.
- **Connects to Fradera (W1RR)**: Fradera's model of imperial transition and the role of "assimilation" policies maps onto May's account; the Philippines' industrial education is a case study in the tension between integrative and exclusionary colonial governance.
- **Connects to Constantino (W3PS)**: Constantino argues that American education succeeded as colonial control even where it failed economically — producing culturally Americanized Filipinos. May's economic failure narrative and Constantino's cultural success narrative together give a complete picture of what colonial education actually accomplished.
- **Connects to Worcester (W3PS)**: Worcester celebrates the same educational expansion May critiques, framing English-language schools and vocational training as gifts of civilization. Reading them together exposes how the same facts were narrated entirely differently by apologists vs. critical historians.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 Practice)

**Set 1: Industrial Education / Backward Races / Beveridge**
American educational policy in the Philippines translated Beveridge's civilizational hierarchy into institutional form: the belief that Filipinos — like African Americans and Native Americans — were suited only for manual labor justified an industrial curriculum that served colonial economic extraction while denying academic development.

**Set 2: Commercialization / Standardization / Constantino**
May shows that the Bureau of Education's drive to commercialize and standardize Filipino handicraft production failed economically; Constantino completes the analysis by showing the cultural standardization succeeded — producing Americanized minds that no longer required economic coercion because they had internalized colonial values.

**Set 3: Social Engineering / Colonial Periphery / Fradera**
May's central finding — that social engineering in colonies "only infrequently achieved policy makers' stated objectives" — supports Fradera's argument that imperial transitions generate unstable governance forms; the Philippines' failed industrial education program illustrates how metropolitan models, when transferred to colonial peripheries, are simultaneously expanded and distorted.
