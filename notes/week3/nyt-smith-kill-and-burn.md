## Summary (~200 words)

This 1902 New York Times article reports on President Roosevelt's decision to retire General Jacob H. Smith following his court-martial for ordering the infamous "kill and burn" campaign in Samar, Philippines. Smith had ordered his subordinate Major Waller to kill every Filipino male over the age of ten capable of bearing arms and to turn Samar into a "howling wilderness." The article reproduces Secretary of War Elihu Root's lengthy review letter, which is the primary analytical document.

Root's letter does several things simultaneously: it confirms that Smith was found guilty of "conduct to the prejudice of good order and military discipline"; it defends the broader U.S. war effort by insisting that Smith's intemperate language (rather than the orders themselves) was the offense; and it recommends only retirement — not imprisonment — on grounds of Smith's long and distinguished service record. Root's language is notably careful to insulate the broader American military enterprise from Smith's conduct, framing the orders as individual excess rather than systemic policy. Roosevelt endorses the findings and the mild sentence. The document is thus simultaneously a record of colonial atrocity and a demonstration of how the American state managed accountability for that atrocity — with a reprimand rather than punishment.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **"Kill and Burn" Order**: Smith's instruction to Major Waller to treat Samar as a "howling wilderness," killing all males over ten capable of bearing arms; became the defining American atrocity of the Philippine-American War.
- **Court-Martial**: Military judicial proceeding; Smith's conviction for "conduct prejudicial to good order and discipline" — notably not for war crimes as such.
- **Benevolent Assimilation**: McKinley's official policy framing for U.S. rule in the Philippines; the Smith case reveals the violent underside of this rhetoric.
- **Command Responsibility**: The principle that senior officers are accountable for orders they give subordinates; Root's review applies this only to verbal excess, not operational policy.
- **Reconcentration / Concentration Camps**: Context referenced in the article — the Samar campaign involved forcing civilian populations into controlled zones, echoing Spanish tactics in Cuba that Americans had loudly condemned.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Mabini (W3PS)**: Mabini predicted that "arbitrary and violent processes would never arouse [Filipino] confidence." The Samar campaign is the empirical evidence; Smith's "kill and burn" order is precisely the kind of coercion Mabini warned would prolong resistance.
- **Connects to Beveridge (W2PS)**: Beveridge's civilizational rhetoric ("savage and senile decay") provided the ideological frame that Root then deployed to defend Smith — calling Filipino resisters "cruel and treacherous savages." The NYT article shows Beveridge's language in operational use.
- **Connects to Worcester (W3PS)**: Worcester claims "never before have the officers and men of any civilized nation conducted so humanely a war" as the Philippine-American War. The Smith case is the direct rebuttal — it was notorious enough to reach the President's desk and generate a NYT front page.
- **Connects to Kramer (W2RR)**: Kramer analyzes how racial ideology shaped American colonial violence; the "savage foe" language in Root's letter is a primary source illustration of the racialized vocabulary Kramer theorizes.
- **Connects to Fradera (W1RR)**: Fradera's framework of imperial transitions includes debates about violence and legitimacy; the Smith case shows the American empire managing domestic dissent over colonial atrocity — the reprimand is a legitimation strategy as much as a punishment.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 Practice)

**Set 1: Kill and Burn / Benevolent Assimilation / Beveridge**
The Smith "kill and burn" order exists in direct tension with McKinley's "benevolent assimilation" policy that Beveridge championed; the NYT article reveals how American officials resolved that tension — by treating violent excess as individual aberration rather than systemic policy, protecting the civilizational narrative.

**Set 2: Command Responsibility / Racial Ideology / Worcester**
Root's review holds Smith accountable for intemperate language while deploying "savage foe" racial language to justify the broader campaign; Worcester similarly celebrates American humanitarianism in the Philippines while papering over the evidence — both documents show colonial ideology managing accountability.

**Set 3: Pacification / Coercion / Mabini**
Mabini argued that coercion could not produce genuine peace; the Samar campaign — culminating in Smith's retirement and a presidential rebuke mild enough to be called a pat on the back — illustrates exactly the dynamic Mabini described: force exhausting Filipino resistance without generating consent.

### Essay Questions (Part 2 Practice)

1. The Smith case produced a conviction but only a retirement. What does the American state's management of this scandal reveal about the relationship between colonial violence and colonial legitimacy? Draw on the NYT article and at least one other reading.

2. Compare how Worcester and the NYT article on Smith construct American conduct in the Philippines. How do both documents manage the tension between civilizational rhetoric and documented atrocity?
