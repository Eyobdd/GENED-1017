## Race, Civilization, and Imperial Ideology
*(Kramer, Beveridge, Schurz, Duara, **Fradera**)*

### Fradera ↔ [Beveridge, Kramer, Duara — to be added]
- **Exceptionality as racial logic**: Fradera shows that all modern empires resolved the contradiction between liberal universalism and colonial hierarchy through formal racial exceptionality (castas pardas excluded from Spanish citizenship, Jim Crow, British racial line in empire). This structural argument sets up Beveridge's civilizationist rhetoric and Kramer's analysis of how race shaped American imperial ideology specifically.
- **Continuity of domestic and overseas racism**: Fradera's argument that 1898 extended *internal* American racial practices overseas connects directly to Kramer's argument about race as the organizing logic of American empire.
- **"Dangerous symmetries" → anticolonial nationalism**: Fradera's theoretical frame for why liberal empires produce their own opposition predicts the resistance documented in Weeks 2–3 (Mabini, Constantino) and the self-determination debates in Weeks 4–7.

## Self-Determination vs. Strategic Interest
*(Mabini, Chang, Teller/Platt — Weeks 2–5)*

### Fradera ↔ [Mabini, Teller/Platt — to be added]
- **Insular cases + Platt Amendment**: Both are concrete examples of Fradera's "exceptionality" — constitutional frameworks designed to exclude colonized peoples from the rights the metropole claims to universalize.
- **Wardship doctrine → Philippines**: Fradera traces the legal lineage from *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* through the insular cases — the Philippines were governed as "wards" incapable of self-government, exactly the logic Mabini resisted.
