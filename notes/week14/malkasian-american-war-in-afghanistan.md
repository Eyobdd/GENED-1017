## Summary

Malkasian's *The American War in Afghanistan* (2021) is a comprehensive history of America's longest war by a scholar-practitioner who served as political advisor to senior US commanders in Afghanistan, learned Pashto, and later participated in Taliban peace talks in Doha. The book draws on Pashto-language Taliban sources, indirect surveys of fighters, and Malkasian's firsthand observation across fifteen provinces.

**Chapter 1 ("Thinking about America's War in Afghanistan")** frames the book's central thesis: the Taliban's alignment with what it fundamentally meant to be Afghan — resistance to foreign occupation and devotion to Islam — was a *necessary* condition for America's failure. Malkasian argues that existing explanations (government corruption, Pakistani sanctuary, tribalism) are necessary but insufficient. What they cannot explain is why Afghan soldiers — numerically superior, better equipped — repeatedly chose not to fight. The answer: soldiers could not "reconcile fighting for Afghanistan alongside an infidel occupier and against a movement that represented Islam" (p. 455). The Taliban inspired sacrifice; the government could not. This framing is analytically careful — Malkasian insists the argument is not that Islam is inherently violent but that foreign occupation contradicted national identity and religious duty, animating insurgents while sapping the will of government forces.

**Chapter 2 ("The Country and Peoples of Afghanistan")** provides the historical-cultural baseline essential for reading the rest of the book. Malkasian surveys Afghanistan's ethnic geography (Pashtun ~40%, Tajik ~30%, Hazara ~15%, Uzbek ~10%), explaining how the tribal system, Islam, and a centuries-deep tradition of resisting foreign invasion interlock. The Pashtun code of *Pashtunwali* — emphasizing honor, revenge (*badal*), hospitality, and subservience to no man — explains tribal fragmentation and the difficulty of building cohesive military forces. Religious leaders (mullahs, maulawis) operated independently of tribal structures and the state, with cross-cutting networks capable of legitimizing jihad across ethnic lines; they called forth jihads against Britain in 1840, 1880, 1896, 1919, and against the Soviets in 1980. The chapter then covers the Soviet-Afghan War (1979–1989): one million Afghans died, four million fled, state structures collapsed, poppy cultivation exploded, and a new class of war-hardened commanders replaced traditional elders and scholars. The civil war that followed Soviet withdrawal was in Malkasian's view "the darkest chapter," setting the environment for the Taliban's emergence.

**Chapter 21 ("Looking Back")** offers Malkasian's retrospective assessment. The US achieved its core goal (killing bin Laden, preventing another homeland attack) but failed to end the war on favorable terms. The chapter identifies three structural explanations for failure — government mistreatment of Afghans, Pakistani sanctuary for the Taliban, and Afghan government military dysfunction — before returning to the overarching argument: the Taliban had an edge in *will* that translated into an edge on the battlefield. Malkasian then asks whether failure was inevitable. He identifies four "missed opportunities": (1) Bush's exclusion of the Taliban from post-2001 settlement when key leaders including Baradar and Mansour were open to it; (2) the Bush administration's failure to build a competent Afghan army quickly enough before the Taliban resurged in 2006; (3) Obama's decision to surge too large rather than use a smaller force and pursue negotiations; (4) Trump's failure to use the 2019 peace talks with Baradar as leverage, instead pressing for unconditional withdrawal, producing an unenforceable agreement. The chapter closes with the fundamental moral question: the US may have prolonged Afghan suffering in order to protect American security — "We resuscitated a state of civil war so that we could sleep a little sounder at home" (p. 461).

**Connection to course themes:** This reading connects directly to the three guiding course questions. WHY occupy — driven by September 11 and counterterrorism, not nation-building; the rationale shifted over time and ultimately dissipated. WHAT did Americans do — counterinsurgency, state-building, institution-building for democracy — and how did Afghans respond — with a split between urban democratic aspiration and rural Taliban order. HOW did the occupation end — not with success or clear defeat but with a negotiated withdrawal that immediately collapsed, paralleling earlier occupations (Philippines, Korea) in terms of the gap between declared goals and actual outcomes.

## Key Terms

- **Pashtunwali**: The unwritten code of the Pashtuns emphasizing honor (*namus*), revenge (*badal*), hospitality (*melmastia*), and subservience to no man. A source of tribal fragmentation — revenge feuds prevented tribal unity — and also of fierce resistance to outsiders who violated honor.
- **Jihad**: Holy war against nonbelievers or a struggle to support Islam; individual religious duty (*farz 'ain*) when defending against infidel invasion. Islamic scholars legitimized jihads against the British four times and against the Soviets; the Taliban used the same legitimizing logic against the US occupation.
- **Mujahideen**: "Holy warriors" — the resistance fighters in the Soviet-Afghan War (1979–1989), organized in small cadres of 10–50 men, often based near their villages. US and Pakistani support channeled through ISI. Their tactics (guerrilla ambush, IED precursors) became the template for Taliban tactics against Americans.
- **Warlord**: Commander who rose to power through military prowess and guns during and after the Soviet war, displacing traditional tribal leaders (*khans*) and elders. Warlord networks and their abuses of power pushed communities toward the Taliban.
- **Taliban**: "Students" — Islamic movement that emerged from the civil war chaos of the early 1990s, initially from Kandahar. Drew on Pashtun identity, Islamic law, and anti-occupation sentiment. Malkasian argues their key strength was ideological coherence: they represented Islam and Afghan resistance in a way the US-backed government could not match.
- **ISI (Inter-Services Intelligence)**: Pakistan's military intelligence branch; coordinated support for mujahideen during Soviet war and (Malkasian argues) continued to provide the Taliban sanctuary and support during the American war. Pakistan's strategic calculus — fear of Indian encirclement, desire for "strategic depth" — made this support immutable regardless of US pressure.
- **Durand Line**: The 1893 border imposed by Britain dividing Pashtun tribes between Afghanistan and Pakistan. Never accepted by Afghans; created enduring cross-border tribal networks that the Taliban exploited for sanctuary and recruitment.
- **Counterinsurgency (COIN)**: US military doctrine emphasizing winning over the population as the path to defeating an insurgency; associated especially with the 2009–2011 Obama surge under McChrystal and Petraeus. Malkasian is skeptical — the surge's tactical successes washed away because the underlying structural problem (occupation animating the Taliban) was unchanged.
- **"Strategic depth"**: Pakistani military concept — the need to ensure Afghanistan remained a friendly buffer against India, preventing Pakistan from being encircled. Explains why Pakistani leaders consistently hedged by supporting the Taliban even while ostensibly cooperating with the US.
- **Fitna**: Quranic concept of civil war or strife — considered among the worst evils in Islam. The value on unity (oneness of God) gave Islamic leaders a powerful tool to rally people across tribal lines; the Taliban used Islamic unity to override the tribal fragmentation that weakened the government's forces.
- **Loya Jirga**: Grand council — a traditional Afghan consultative assembly of tribal and community leaders; invoked by the post-2001 Karzai government for constitutional legitimacy. Illustrates both the possibility of consensual governance and the fragility of any arrangement dependent on elite buy-in.

FRONT: What role did the Soviet-Afghan War (1979–1989) play in creating the conditions the US inherited in 2001?
BACK: It killed one million Afghans, displaced four million, destroyed state structures, empowered warlords over traditional elders, radicalized the clergy, and exploded the poppy economy. The subsequent civil war (1989–1996) was in Malkasian's view "the darkest chapter." The US entered into a society already broken by 20+ years of war. | Malkasian Ch. 2 | The US didn't just face a political challenge — it faced the accumulated wreckage of Soviet intervention.

FRONT: What were the four "missed opportunities" Malkasian identifies in the Afghan War?
BACK: (1) Bush excluded Taliban from 2001 post-invasion settlement when Baradar/Mansour were open to it. (2) Bush built the Afghan army too slowly — only 26,000 soldiers by 2006. (3) Obama surged too large rather than using smaller force + negotiations. (4) Trump pressed for unconditional withdrawal in 2019 rather than using the peace talks with Baradar as leverage. | Malkasian Ch. 21 | Malkasian doesn't say victory was possible, only a less costly stalemate or negotiated peace.

FRONT: Why did three US presidents (Bush, Obama, Trump) fail to withdraw from Afghanistan even when they wanted to?
BACK: Fear of terrorism — the political blowback from another 9/11-style attack was viewed as fatal. Domestic politics made leaving *more* dangerous than staying: generals opposed withdrawal, human rights advocates worried about women's rights, and Congress rarely pressured for exit. Terrorism fear only faded in 2020 with COVID-19 absorbing national attention and China becoming the primary strategic concern. | Malkasian Ch. 21 | Explains the structural trap — not just strategic miscalculation but political constraints.

FRONT: ISI and Pakistan's "strategic depth"
BACK: Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence funneled US/Saudi aid to mujahideen during the Soviet war, then provided the Taliban sanctuary after 2001. The logic: Pakistan feared being encircled by India and needed Afghanistan as a buffer ("strategic depth"). This calculus was *immutable* — US pressure on Pakistan consistently failed because no American incentive could override Pakistan's existential fear of India. | Malkasian Ch. 2 and Ch. 21 | Pakistan is a recurring structural explanation Malkasian engages but ultimately subordinates to the Islam/occupation argument.

FRONT: How does Malkasian's argument connect to the moral question of US intervention?
BACK: "We resuscitated a state of civil war so that we could sleep a little sounder at home." The US secured itself from terrorism at the cost of prolonged Afghan suffering — the intervention extended a civil war that might have wound down under Taliban rule. US leaders never consciously made the trade-off; they assumed democracy would be better for Afghans. But the intervention did more harm than good. | Malkasian Ch. 21 | Connects directly to course theme: self-determination vs. imperial control, and the gap between stated intentions and actual outcomes.

FRONT: What is the significance of Afghan identity's historical resistance to foreign invaders?
BACK: From Alexander to the Mughals to the British (three wars) to the Soviets, Afghans built a national identity around never being colonized. "None of the surrounding countries can claim to never have been colonized or ruled by other nations in modern history." This pride was cross-ethnic and cross-class — poets, scholars, bureaucrats, and fighters shared it. It meant US presence was always politically delegitimizing for any government it backed. | Malkasian Ch. 2 | Essential for understanding why reform programs (education, democracy, women's rights) couldn't overcome the occupation problem.
