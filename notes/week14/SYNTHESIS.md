## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 14 is the course's closing case study and its harshest verdict on American nation-building. Three readings triangulate the same failure from different angles — ideological/symbolic (Cullather), strategic/cultural (Malkasian), and institutional/bureaucratic (SIGAR) — producing a diagnosis more comprehensive and more damning than any single reading could achieve alone.

**Cullather's HAVA (1946–1979) is the prehistory that explains the future.** His central argument is that the Helmand Valley dam project cannot be excused by the usual alibis because it ran its full course: lavishly funded, comprehensively "integrated," and sustained over thirty years. It failed because it was always primarily about symbolic politics — Pashtun ethnic nationalism, Cold War credibility, modernization ideology — rather than agricultural development. This is Week 14's analytical foundation: the Helmand Valley is a controlled experiment in which modernization theory "ran out the clock" and still lost. HAVA established every pathology the other two readings then document at larger scale: the commitment/leverage paradox (once US credibility was invested, exit became impossible), development myopia (water scarcity as the single "solvable" variable while political complexity was ignored), and the self-renewing ideology that absorbed each failure as a reason to double down.

**Malkasian provides the mechanism Cullather describes from the outside.** His thesis — that the Taliban's alignment with what it fundamentally meant to be Afghan (Islam and resistance to foreign occupation) was a *necessary* condition for US failure — explains why better planning, more money, and superior military force couldn't overcome the structural problem. The very presence of American forces animated the Taliban and sapped the will of Afghan government soldiers who "could not reconcile fighting for Afghanistan alongside an infidel occupier" (p. 455). Critically, Malkasian's argument is not that Islam is inherently violent but that Afghan national identity had been defined for centuries — against Alexander, the Mughals, the British, the Soviets — by resistance to foreign control. Occupation didn't encounter this identity; it produced it as its most powerful enemy.

**SIGAR closes the loop at the institutional level.** Its seven lessons catalog the bureaucratic architecture of failure: no coherent strategy with aligned ends/ways/means; artificial timelines that forced reckless spending; programs designed for visibility rather than durability; personnel rotation ("annual lobotomy") that destroyed institutional memory; insecurity that undermined every program; contextual blindness to Afghan political economy; and M&E that measured outputs (schools built, soldiers trained) rather than outcomes (learning, security). The three readings together produce a complete diagnosis: HAVA shows that the failure was ideological from the beginning (modernization theory could not be saved by more time or money); Malkasian shows that the failure was structural (occupation itself was the problem, not its execution); SIGAR shows that the failure was institutional (the U.S. had no capacity to learn from its mistakes across twenty years or across prior occupations).

The most productive tension between the readings is between SIGAR's "if only we had understood the context better" and Malkasian's deeper argument that better context understanding could not have solved the alignment problem. SIGAR implies that with more cultural knowledge, better conditionality, and improved M&E, reconstruction might have succeeded. Malkasian's implication is more radical: "any Afghan government, however good and however democratic, was going to be imperiled as long as it was aligned with the United States" (p. 6). No amount of institutional improvement could change the fact that U.S. presence was the Taliban's most effective recruiting tool. SIGAR diagnoses curable pathologies; Malkasian identifies a structural condition. Read together, they suggest the most honest synthesis: the institutional failures made a bad situation catastrophically worse, but even their absence could not have overcome the fundamental dynamics Malkasian identifies.

Cullather's long 20th-century view bridges the two positions. HAVA's Pashtun nomads "met the bulldozers with rifles" (p. 534) not because the project was underfunded but because their political identity — cross-border mobility, clan sovereignty, resistance to sedentarization — was incompatible with what American engineers were trying to make them. SIGAR's "contextual blindness" is not simply an institutional failure: it is the operational face of the ideological certainty that Cullather identifies in modernization theory itself. You can fix a personnel rotation policy; you cannot easily fix a worldview that defines underdevelopment as a technical problem solvable by engineers.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 11 (Latham, "Modernization and Culture"; Ho Chi Minh; Eisenhower):** Cullather's HAVA is the single most complete case study that Latham's theoretical argument (Week 11) predicts and requires. Latham showed that modernization theory reproduced imperial tutelage in a "developmental stage" vocabulary; HAVA ran that theory for thirty years in a single valley and confirmed every prediction. Truman's Point IV program, which Latham analyzed as the Cold War renovation of "colonialism of capacity," arrived in Afghanistan just as HAVA's engineering failures became undeniable in 1949 — and converted those failures into a reason to spend more. Cullather's phrase for the result — "an economic Vietnam" (p. 532) — is Latham's argument materialized: the commitment/leverage paradox ensures that modernization ideology absorbs failure by generating new rationales rather than reconsideration. Week 14 is Week 11's empirical test case, run to completion.

**Week 12 (Dower, "Lessons from Japan" and "The Other Japanese Occupation"):** Dower's Essay 1 enumerated the seven conditions that made Japan's occupation succeed; Afghanistan meets none of them. There was no unconditional surrender conferring near-unlimited authority; the occupied population was ethnically fragmented and fiercely insurgent (the reverse of Japan's "not one instance of terror in seven years"); bureaucratic infrastructure was nearly nonexistent after twenty years of Soviet and civil war; there was no preexisting democratic tradition; and the US occupation was launched with strategic (counterterrorism) rather than reformist intent. SIGAR's seven institutional lessons map onto Dower's seven enabling conditions almost point for point — each SIGAR lesson names a failure where Japan succeeded. Week 14's Afghanistan is not merely the empirical refutation of the Japan-Iraq analogy that Dower analyzed in 2002; it is the course's demonstration that Dower's checklist is necessary rather than sufficient, because even meeting those conditions (as Japan partially did) produces fragile results, while Afghanistan's complete absence of those conditions produced the course's most comprehensive occupation failure.

**Week 10 (Montgomery, *Forced to Be Free*; Ladejinsky, 1959):** Montgomery's "artificial revolution" framework — eliminate despotic elites, encourage new democratic leadership, install institutional guarantees — requires comparison with Afghanistan to reveal its limits. Even Montgomery's modest checklist assumes an intact state to reform, a class of new democratic leaders to encourage, and institutions stable enough to carry guarantees. Afghanistan after twenty years of Soviet war, civil war, and Taliban rule had none of these prerequisites. Ladejinsky's "strange partnership" — indigenous reformer (Hiroo Wada) + occupier authority = durable land reform — never materialized in Afghanistan. The closest analog was the post-2001 moment, but warlords captured the reconstruction apparatus rather than reformers. Where Japan's land reform succeeded because an indigenous reform agenda used American authority as leverage, Afghanistan's reconstruction was captured by Afghan elites who used American funding as patronage. SIGAR's "elite capture" is the Afghan inversion of Ladejinsky's "strange partnership."

**Week 4 (Duara, "Imperialism of Free Nations"; Atlantic Charter; Reischauer):** Duara's argument that "free nations" constructed client states using the rhetoric of self-determination and development is realized most completely in Afghanistan. Point IV (Cullather), the post-2001 reconstruction mission (SIGAR), and the Alliance's institutional framework (Malkasian) all used the vocabulary of "development," "democracy," and "sovereignty" while constructing a state apparatus that could survive only with external support. Malkasian's most provocative line — "any Afghan government, however good and however democratic, was going to be imperiled as long as it was aligned with the United States" — is the structural confirmation of Duara's framework: client-state formation was not an accident of poor planning but a structural consequence of how US intervention was organized. Afghanistan could not be simultaneously sovereign and dependent; the alignment with the US destroyed the legitimacy that sovereignty requires.

**Week 9 (Jarausch, Kojima, Callam et al., Hurh, Jaima):** The cultural occupation theme of Week 9 — how occupied peoples responded to demands for performed transformation — finds its most stark expression in Malkasian's account. Where Week 9 showed Japanese and German occupied peoples navigating between passive resistance, opportunistic collaboration, and pragmatic adaptation, Malkasian's Afghanistan shows a society where the weight of identity — Islam, Pashtunwali, centuries of anti-colonial resistance — systematically prevented the "pragmatic adaptation" that Week 9's Michiko embodied. Cullather's Pashtun nomads who "met the bulldozers with rifles" are the 1960s analog of Kojima's Isa — refusing to perform the demanded transformation, but armed. The SIGAR report's finding that "we wanted to give them something they had never had before" (p. 63) echoes the Week 9 occupier impulse to reconstruct from scratch: the cultural transplantation (Lashkar Gah as "a piece of America inserted into the Afghan landscape") is the Week 9 theme taken to its physical extreme.

**Week 7 (Kim, "Land Reform in South Korea"; Dower Ch. 17; Gillingham):** Kim's argument that "the occupied territory is not a blank slate" — that indigenous class structures, political actors, and historical formations shape occupation outcomes — applies to Afghanistan with even greater force than to Korea. HAVA's resettlement program attempted to convert nomadic Pashtuns into sedentary farmers: the direct equivalent of the land redistribution programs Kim analyzed in Korea, but even more coercive because it sought to change not just property relations but an entire mode of life. Like Kim's Korean landlords, Afghan warlords and clan leaders used reconstruction resources to entrench their own power; off-budget assistance meant the Afghan government never built fiscal capacity, just as Korea's political elite used American support to evade genuine reform. Week 14 is Week 7's negative case at maximum intensity: every lever that failed partially in Korea failed completely in Afghanistan.

**Week 5 (Dower Ch. 6, Chang "Independence Without Liberation," SWNCC 150):** Dower's "democratic revolution from above" (Week 5) had its Afghan version in the Bonn Agreement (2001) and the 2004 constitution — democratic institutions imposed without the population having fought for them, drafted under international supervision, requiring ongoing foreign support to function. Chang's "independence without liberation" describes Korea's structural condition: formal sovereignty without economic, political, or security independence. Afghanistan's 2021 collapse — a government folding in weeks once US support withdrew — is the ultimate demonstration of Chang's thesis: nominal sovereignty and genuine occupation were never in contradiction because the nominal sovereignty always depended entirely on occupier presence. "Independence without liberation" is, if anything, too optimistic a description for Afghanistan: what collapsed in 2021 was not independent at all.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is Week 14's master theme, and Afghanistan is its most unambiguous case. Japan (Week 5–7) produced genuine constitutional moments; Korea (Chang) achieved formal independence in permanent dependency; Vietnam (Week 11) fought to expel the occupier. Afghanistan collapses all three: the formal state was never genuinely sovereign (SIGAR), the constitution was an externally drafted document without popular roots (Malkasian), and the resistance that Week 11 associated with Ho's nationalist evolution was present in Afghanistan *from the first day of occupation* (Malkasian Ch. 2's historical depth argument). Self-determination was not denied by a "reverse course" that abandoned an original reformist intent — it was never the genuine aim. Cullather's HAVA makes this legible from 1946: the project was "primarily about national prestige and only secondarily about the social benefits of increasing agricultural productivity" (p. 523). The course's entire arc on self-determination — from the Teller Amendment's promise to the Platt Amendment's actual control (Week 2), through Korea's "independence without liberation" (Week 5), through Vietnam's "tutelage without exit" (Week 11) — culminates in Afghanistan's occupation that was structurally incapable of producing the self-determination it claimed to advance.

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy & planning — punitive vs. reformist)** reaches its most complete diagnostic form in SIGAR's seven lessons. The course has tracked this tension from JCS 1067's initially punitive German directive through SWNCC 150's more reformist Japanese framework, through the reverse courses and land reforms of Weeks 5–7, through Vietnam's modernization projects. SIGAR's analysis reveals that the punitive/reformist distinction may be less fundamental than the capacity/incapacity distinction: the US had no civilian agency with the mandate, resources, and expertise to lead reconstruction. When DOD fills the vacuum, reconstruction takes on military logic — spend fast, show progress, measure inputs not outcomes. Reformist intent (improving Afghan governance, building democratic institutions) was genuine but institutionally unequipped. Afghanistan was therefore neither purely punitive nor successfully reformist — it was, in SIGAR's most damning formulation, a "pick-up game" (Hadley, p. 8) played with improvised tools by agencies that had deliberately forgotten how to do it.

**Theme 4 (Cold War reversal / "reverse course")** takes a new form in Week 14. The course established the "reverse course" as a shift within an ongoing occupation — Japan's move from democratization to Cold War stabilization, Korea's failure to ever reach the democratization phase. In Afghanistan, there was no equivalent of the original reformist moment: the mission was counterterrorism from the beginning, and democracy was attached to it as a secondary (and, Malkasian implies, instrumentalized) goal. The "war on terror" replaced Cold War competition as the organizing logic, but the structural consequences were identical: strategic priorities consistently trumped reformist ones, "conditionality" lacked credibility, and the occupation extended far beyond any achievable endpoint. SIGAR's "mission creep" — from destroying al-Qaeda to defeating the Taliban to fighting corruption to building a legitimate state — is the 21st-century version of the reverse course, but in reverse order: rather than starting ambitious and retreating, the Afghanistan mission started strategic and kept expanding its stated goals while never developing the capacity to achieve even the original narrow one.

**Theme 5 (Race, civilization, and imperial ideology)** operates in Week 14 through modernization theory's cultural assumptions. Cullather's Louis Dupree formulation — "a nation does not accept technology without ideology. A machine or a dam is a product of a culture" (p. 528) — is the clearest single statement in the course of what Week 5's Scott (high modernism) and Week 11's Latham (modernization theory) argued theoretically. The Lashkar Gah planned suburb — "a piece of America inserted into the Afghan landscape" (Toynbee, p. 512) — is the Cullather case's physical embodiment of modernization ideology's civilizational assumption: that America's spatial and cultural forms are universal templates. SIGAR's finding that the US "tried to impose Western technocratic models" (formal courts, banking, parliamentary governance, NATO-style military) onto a society where 80–90% of disputes were resolved through informal means is the 2001–2021 continuation of the same assumption. The civilizational grammar (Fradera Week 1, Kramer Week 2, Latham Week 11) persisted through every decade of US engagement with Afghanistan, and the "development myopia" it produced — seeing only the technical problem, not the cultural and political one — was consistent from Morrison Knudsen's 1946 dam surveys to USAID's 2019 school-construction programs.

**Theme 8 (Collaboration and resistance by occupied peoples)** achieves its richest expression in Malkasian's account. Where Week 9's Kojima offered three character types (passive resistance, opportunistic collaboration, pragmatic adaptation), Malkasian shows that in Afghanistan the same three categories had entirely different political weights. Opportunistic collaboration produced warlord capture of reconstruction resources (SIGAR's "elite capture"); pragmatic adaptation produced urban educated Afghans genuinely invested in democratic governance but unable to build a military force capable of defending it; passive resistance shaded into active insurgency as rural Pashtuns experienced US military operations as violations of Pashtunwali honor norms. Cullather's nomads resisting sedentarization add the crucial historical depth: Afghan resistance to externally imposed transformation was not a post-9/11 reaction to an unpopular war but a consistent pattern across eight decades of US presence. The Taliban's emergence from the Helmand Valley — the exact territory HAVA sought to transform — is Cullather's final dark irony: the resistance the modernization project generated eventually produced the political movement that made the post-2001 occupation unwinnable.

## Combined Key Terms

*Deduplicated across all three Week 14 readings, with cross-references to earlier weeks.*

- **Modernization theory** (Cullather + SIGAR): Framework positing that all societies travel a linear path from "traditional" to "modern"; U.S. technical assistance accelerates this. Cullather shows it functioned as a self-sealing ideology — each failure generated new rationales rather than reconsideration. SIGAR's "contextual blindness" lesson is its 2001–2021 institutional form. Cross-reference: Latham's full genealogy (W11); Point IV as its operational vehicle.

- **Point IV** (Cullather): Truman's 1949 foreign aid program reframing colonial intervention as mutual development; arrived in Afghanistan just as HAVA's engineering failures became undeniable and rescued the project by enrolling it in a global development mission. Cross-reference: Latham (W11) on Point IV as "old imperialism — exploitation for foreign profit" in liberal disguise.

- **Commitment/leverage paradox** (Cullather + SIGAR): Once U.S. credibility was invested in HAVA (Cullather's "economic Vietnam," p. 532), withdrawal became impossible even after failure was confirmed. SIGAR's "conditionality" lesson is the same paradox at scale: the U.S. could not credibly withhold funds essential to the Afghan government's survival, so reform demands were toothless. Cross-reference: Dower's "uses of history" (W12) — the same logic that made Vietnam unwinnable made HAVA and Afghanistan unexitable.

- **Development myopia** (Cullather, Lloyd Baron): Identifying a single technical constraint (water scarcity) as the cause of underdevelopment while subordinating complex social, political, and ecological problems. Generates new technical solutions to fundamentally political problems. Cross-reference: SIGAR's "strategy = money expended" lesson; Latham's "tutelage" (W11) as structural cousin.

- **Pashtunwali** (Malkasian): Unwritten Pashtun code of honor (*namus*), revenge (*badal*), hospitality (*melmastia*), and subservience to no man. Created both fierce resistance to outsiders and tribal fragmentation that prevented unified government military effectiveness. Cross-reference: Cullather's nomads resisting sedentarization — Pashtunwali's "subservience to no man" as structural obstacle to HAVA resettlement programs.

- **Taliban** (Malkasian): Islamic movement emerging from post-Soviet civil war chaos in Kandahar; drew on Pashtun identity, Islamic law, and anti-occupation sentiment. Malkasian's thesis: their key strength was ideological coherence — representing Islam and Afghan resistance in a way the US-backed government structurally could not match. Cross-reference: Cullather — Taliban movement began in the Helmand Valley in 1994; HAVA's alkaline soils proved ideal for opium poppies that became a primary Taliban revenue source.

- **"Islam + occupation" thesis** (Malkasian): The Taliban's alignment with Afghan national identity (religion + resistance to foreign invaders) was a *necessary* condition for US defeat — not the cause, but the condition without which other factors (corruption, Pakistani sanctuary) would not have been sufficient. Cross-reference: Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" (W1) — liberal occupation teaches the language of rights and generates opposition; here occupation generates opposition not through rights-language but through identity violation.

- **Durand Line** (Cullather + Malkasian): 1,200-mile boundary surveyed by Britain in 1893, bisecting the Pashtun people between Afghanistan and what became Pakistan. Source of the Pushtunistan dispute (Cullather) and, post-2001, of Pakistani sanctuary for the Taliban (Malkasian). Both readings show the Durand Line as a British-designed mechanism of strategic management that became the source of enduring instability. Cross-reference: connects to British imperial management techniques discussed across Weeks 1–2.

- **Annual lobotomy** (SIGAR): Institutional knowledge lost each year as staff rotated out; successors started from scratch and repeated predecessors' mistakes. Cross-reference: Montgomery's "new democratic leadership" element of artificial revolution (W10) — the rotation policy systematically prevented the development of leadership that actually understood the environment.

- **Elite capture** (SIGAR): When reconstruction funds are seized by local powerbrokers (warlords, corrupt officials) rather than intended beneficiaries. Cross-reference: Kim's Korean landlords using occupation's political alliance to block land reform (W7); Ladejinsky's "strange partnership" (W10) as the positive inversion — what elite capture prevented in Afghanistan was the indigenous reformer-occupier alliance that made Japan's land reform work.

- **Absorptive capacity** (SIGAR): Maximum aid a country can productively absorb (~15–45% of GDP). During the 2009–2011 surge, US spending exceeded 100% of Afghan GDP. Cross-reference: Cullather's HAVA — the same logic of flooding resources to signal commitment regardless of absorptive limits.

- **Off-budget assistance** (SIGAR): Aid channeled through NGOs/contractors rather than Afghan ministries; prevented the Afghan government from developing fiscal capacity. Cross-reference: the "bridge framing" (build now, sustain later) = HAVA's "integration" rhetoric — both built infrastructure that the recipient government could never maintain without external funding.

- **Mission creep** (SIGAR): Unplanned expansion from defeating al-Qaeda → defeating the Taliban → fighting corruption → building a legitimate state. Cross-reference: Latham's "tutelage without exit" (W11) — each expansion required more resources while the actual capacity remained unchanged.

- **"Pick-up game"** (SIGAR, Stephen Hadley): US has no post-conflict stabilization model; after Vietnam, dismantled irregular-warfare capacity, then repeated the same mistakes in Iraq and Afghanistan. Cross-reference: Dower's "uses of history" (W12) — the pattern of post-war amnesia is the institutional form of the same historical blindness; SIGAR's Gen. Keane: "We purged ourselves of everything that had to do with irregular warfare."

- **HAVA (Helmand and Arghandab Valley Authority)** (Cullather): US-Afghan joint development authority created 1952, controlling 1,800 sq. miles of river valley; responsible for dams, education, housing, medicine, agriculture, and industry. Modeled on TVA. Cullather's "controlled experiment" in modernization failure. Cross-reference: TVA as exported Cold War weapon (Truman/Schlesinger, p. 524).

- **Buffer state** (Cullather + Malkasian): A neutral state strategically maintained between competing powers. Afghanistan operated as a buffer first between British India and Russia, then between the US and USSR (Cullather), then as a strategic terrain for US counterterrorism with Pakistani "strategic depth" concerns as a structural complication (Malkasian). Cross-reference: Duara's client-state formation (W4) — the buffer state is the structural condition that makes genuine self-determination impossible; sovereignty is always hostage to the buffer function.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: Modernization Theory / Commitment/Leverage Paradox / Annual Lobotomy**
*(Cullather + Cullather/SIGAR + SIGAR — Week 14 internal)*

All three terms describe how the failure of American nation-building in Afghanistan was self-sustaining and self-regenerating. Modernization theory (Cullather's ideological foundation) absorbed each failure by generating new rationales — waterlogging became a reason for new drainage programs, not a reason to reconsider the project. The commitment/leverage paradox ensured that once U.S. credibility was invested, exit became more costly than continued failure — "an economic Vietnam" that consumed money and resources "all to avoid making failure obvious" (Cullather, p. 532). SIGAR's annual lobotomy is the institutional form of the same dynamic: each year's rotation destroyed the memory of previous failure, ensuring that the ideological certainty of modernization theory and the credibility trap of the commitment/leverage paradox reproduced themselves without anyone having to choose them. Three separate mechanisms — ideological, strategic, and institutional — converged to make the same failures permanent.

---

**Set 2: Point IV / "Strange Partnership" / Elite Capture**
*(Cullather/Latham W11 + Ladejinsky W10 + SIGAR — spanning Weeks 10, 11, and 14)*

Ladejinsky's "strange partnership" (Week 10) — indigenous reformer Hiroo Wada using MacArthur's authority to accomplish what Japanese domestic politics had blocked — is the positive model that Afghanistan systematically failed to achieve. Point IV's development logic (Cullather/Latham) arrived in Afghanistan in 1949 as a universal template, not as a response to indigenous Afghan demand; there was no Afghan equivalent of Wada who had conceptualized land and water reform in response to local conditions and was waiting for occupier authority to push it through. Instead, SIGAR's "elite capture" is what filled the same structural position: Afghan warlords and corrupt officials used reconstruction resources to entrench patronage networks rather than drive genuine reform. The contrast between Japan and Afghanistan is partly about conditions (Montgomery's prerequisites, Week 10) but also about which indigenous actors captured occupation power. "Strange partnership" requires an indigenous reformer; elite capture requires only indigenous powerbrokers. Afghanistan had the latter in abundance.

---

**Set 3: "Dangerous Symmetries" / Islam + Occupation / Durand Line**
*(Fradera W1 + Malkasian W14 + Cullather/Malkasian W14 — spanning Weeks 1 and 14)*

Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" (Week 1) predicted that liberal empires teach the language of rights and inevitably generate opposition from populations using that same language. In Afghanistan, the symmetry was not primarily about rights-language but about identity: Malkasian's "Islam + occupation" thesis shows that the US produced its own most effective enemy by occupying a country whose national identity had been forged — across centuries and across ethnic lines — by resistance to exactly what the US was doing. The Durand Line is the structural mechanism that locked this dynamic in place: by dividing the Pashtun people between Afghanistan and Pakistan, it created the cross-border networks that made the Taliban's sanctuary in Pakistan structurally immovable, regardless of US pressure. Cullather's Pushtunistan dispute (the Afghans' own use of Pashtun identity politics in the 1950s–60s) shows the same identity dynamic operating before 9/11: US modernization projects that tried to settle Pashtun nomads as a demographic wedge were already generating the resistance that would eventually fuel the Taliban. The dangerous symmetry in Afghanistan was not that Afghans used the occupier's language against it — it was that the occupier's presence summoned the deepest structures of Afghan identity in systematic opposition.

---

**Set 4: "Artificial Revolution" / "Pick-Up Game" / Buffer State**
*(Montgomery W10 + SIGAR/Hadley W14 + Cullather/Malkasian W14 — spanning Weeks 10 and 14)*

Montgomery's "artificial revolution" (Week 10) required a minimum set of preconditions: a defeated state whose unconditional surrender gave the occupier authority to overhaul institutions, a domestic reform current to empower, and a window before Cold War pressure distorted the program. Afghanistan met none of these. The "pick-up game" (Hadley) is what results when artificial revolution is attempted without those conditions by an institution that deliberately dismantled its irregular-warfare capacity after Vietnam: ad hoc improvisation that cannot accumulate learning because the personnel rotation cycle (annual lobotomy) destroys institutional memory. The buffer state logic (Cullather, Malkasian) adds the geopolitical dimension: Afghanistan was never the primary strategic object in its own right — it was a terrain for managing great-power competition, Pashtun nationalism, and post-9/11 counterterrorism, all simultaneously. Montgomery's artificial revolution requires an occupied state capable of being reconstructed; a buffer state is structurally incapable of developing the sovereign institutional coherence that reconstruction requires, because every neighboring power has strategic interests in keeping it divided and dependent.

---

**Set 5: "Independence Without Liberation" / Conditionality / "Any Afghan Government Was Imperiled"**
*(Chang W5 + SIGAR + Malkasian — spanning Weeks 5 and 14)*

Chang's "independence without liberation" described Korea's structural condition: formal sovereignty without economic, political, or security independence from the occupier. Malkasian's most provocative claim — that "any Afghan government, however good and however democratic, was going to be imperiled as long as it was aligned with the United States" (p. 6) — is "independence without liberation" taken to its logical extreme: in Afghanistan, nominal independence and actual occupation were not even in tension, because the Taliban's alignment with Afghan identity made foreign-backed governance structurally illegitimate regardless of its policy content. SIGAR's conditionality lesson connects the two: the US could not credibly withhold funds essential to the Afghan government's survival, because the Afghan government's collapse was exactly what the US was trying to prevent. "Independence without liberation" in Korea meant that formal sovereignty papered over structural dependency; in Afghanistan it meant that formal sovereignty was irrelevant because the government's survival required the very foreign presence that delegitimized it. Chang's Korea was caught in a structural dependency; Malkasian's Afghanistan was caught in a structural contradiction — sovereign enough to require independence, dependent enough to collapse without occupation, and delegitimized by the occupation it required.

## Essay Questions

1. Malkasian argues that "any Afghan government, however good and however democratic, was going to be imperiled as long as it was aligned with the United States" — making the alignment problem, not poor planning or insufficient resources, the *necessary* condition for US failure. SIGAR's report implies that better strategy, context-awareness, and institutional capacity could have produced better outcomes. Using Malkasian, SIGAR, and Cullather alongside at least two earlier weeks' frameworks (strong candidates: Latham W11 on modernization theory, Dower W12 on enabling conditions, Montgomery W10 on artificial revolution, or Chang W5 on structural dependency), assess whether Afghanistan's failure was primarily structural (built into the occupation from the start) or institutional (the product of correctable policy failures). What is at stake — analytically and politically — in which answer you accept?

2. Cullather argues that HAVA failed not for want of money, time, or imagination but because it "ran its full course" and "hit limits of physics, culture, and history." SIGAR's report covers a different era and a different intervention but identifies the same pattern — the same ideological self-sealing, the same commitment/leverage trap, the same contextual blindness. Using Cullather and SIGAR alongside Latham's analysis of modernization theory (W11), Dower's seven enabling conditions (W12), and at least one reading from the postwar occupation sequence (Weeks 5–7), analyze the continuity between Cold War and post-9/11 American nation-building in Afghanistan. What, if anything, changed between 1946 and 2001? What remained constant? Your answer should engage directly with SIGAR's finding that the US "purged itself" of irregular warfare capacity after Vietnam and then repeated the same mistakes — and with Cullather's observation that post-9/11 commentators called for nation-building centered on dams.

3. Malkasian documents that Afghanistan's national identity — shaped by resistance to Alexander, the Mughals, the British (three wars), and the Soviets — made US presence a systematic generator of Taliban legitimacy and government delegitimacy. This identity dimension has no precise parallel in the course's earlier case studies: Japan, Germany, and Korea were all occupied populations whose collaboration with the occupier was possible, even if contested. Using Malkasian's cultural-identity argument alongside Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" (W1), Dower's "not one instance of terror in seven years" as a prior condition (W12), and Kojima/Hurh's spectrum of occupied responses (W9), assess how thoroughly Afghan national identity's resistance tradition explains the occupation's failure — and how much remains explained by institutional and strategic factors (SIGAR, Cullather). What would it mean to say that an occupation's failure was "culturally overdetermined"? Does that conclusion exempt American policymakers from responsibility, or does it deepen their culpability?
