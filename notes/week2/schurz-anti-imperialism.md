## Summary (~200 words)

The Anti-Imperialist League, founded in Boston in 1898, brought together an eclectic coalition — Gilded Age liberals, labor organizers, old abolitionists, conservative Republicans, and Democratic populists — united by opposition to annexation of the Philippines. The League's platform, reprinted at the front of this pamphlet, is a concise statement of constitutional, moral, and political objections to empire. Its key move is to invoke the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution against expansion: "all men are created equal" and "just government derives its authority from the consent of the governed" — principles the League argues are violated by colonial rule over an unwilling Filipino population.

The pamphlet's combination of the League platform with Schurz's speech illuminates the tension within anti-imperialism: the League's platform emphasizes universal rights language, while Schurz's speech (see companion note) relies heavily on racial incapacity arguments. The two registers coexist uneasily — the League officially invokes consent and equality, but its leading intellectuals like Schurz undermine universalism by treating it as applicable only to racially "fit" peoples.

This pamphlet is also a window into the organizational moment of 1899: anti-imperialism was a mass movement with chapters, pamphlets, and a membership that included Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, and Samuel Gompers — a coalition broad enough to be politically significant but ultimately defeated.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Anti-Imperialist League**: Founded Boston, June 1898; peak membership over 500,000; included figures across the political spectrum (Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, Samuel Gompers, Jane Addams). Published pamphlets, organized lectures, lobbied Congress. Defeated when the Senate ratified the Treaty of Paris (February 1899) by a single vote.
- **Consent of the governed (as constitutional claim)**: The League platform's central legal argument — not just a moral principle but a constitutional requirement. Governing the Philippines without consent violates the foundational compact of American government. Contrast with Beveridge, who treats consent as a racial privilege.
- **Spirit of 1776 / anti-colonial precedent**: The League invokes the American Revolution as proof that colonial subjugation is wrong — since the US was itself a colony that resisted, it cannot justify doing to others what Britain did to it. This argument has rhetorical power but was ultimately overwhelmed by Beveridge's racial-commercial logic.
- **Militarism as domestic threat**: The League's argument that empire abroad requires standing armies and military culture at home — a danger to republican institutions. This is the Schurz-era version of Eisenhower's later "military-industrial complex" warning.
- **Coalition anti-imperialism**: The ideological heterogeneity of the League — conservatives worried about race mixing, labor organizers worried about cheap Filipino competition, liberals worried about constitutional principles, old abolitionists worried about betrayal of principles. The coalition's breadth was also its weakness: no single coherent alternative vision.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to schurz-policy-of-imperialism.md**: The companion file — together these two files cover the full intellectual content of both Schurz PDFs. The League platform represents the official movement position (more universalist), while Schurz's speech represents the intellectual position (more racially compromised). The gap between them is analytically important.
- **Connects to Beveridge**: The League platform and Beveridge's speech are the two poles of the 1900 debate. Beveridge won — the Treaty of Paris passed, the Philippines were annexed. Understanding why the anti-imperialists lost despite their broad coalition and principled arguments is a core course question.
- **Connects to Teller Amendment**: The Teller Amendment can be read as a legislative victory for the anti-imperialist position on Cuba — Congress built in a non-annexation pledge. But the Platt Amendment reversed this spirit five years later, showing the limits of legislative anti-imperialism.
- **Connects to Fradera**: The League's "consent of the governed" argument sits uneasily with Fradera's observation that the US had been governing people without their consent for a century (Native Americans, enslaved people, Jim Crow subjects). The League's historical amnesia about domestic empire is part of why Fradera's framework is important.
- **Connects to Chang ("Independence Without Liberation", Week 5)**: The anti-imperialist movement had analogues in later debates about Korea — the question of whether "liberation" from one colonial power simply meant subjection to another. Chang's analysis of Korean nationalism under American occupation echoes the League's warning that declared liberation can mask continued control.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*Set 1: Anti-Imperialist League / Teller Amendment / Platt Amendment*
"Consent" / "non-annexation" / "protectorate" — The League demanded consent as a constitutional requirement; the Teller Amendment built non-annexation into law for Cuba; but the Platt Amendment created a protectorate that achieved colonial control without annexation. Together these three documents show how anti-imperialist principles were formally acknowledged and then structurally circumvented.

*Set 2: League Platform / Fradera / Beveridge*
"Spirit of 1776" / "exceptionality" / "racial destiny" — The League invokes the American Revolution against empire, but Fradera shows the US had already been practicing exceptionality (governing without consent) domestically for a century. Beveridge's racial destiny argument won in 1900 precisely because it aligned with deeply embedded American practices that the League refused to acknowledge.

*Set 3: League Platform / Schurz / Kramer*
"Consent" / "racial fitness" / "imperial grammar" — The League platform invokes universal consent; Schurz qualifies consent with racial fitness; Kramer shows this qualification is where the anti-imperialist argument collapses into the imperial framework it claims to oppose. The gap between the platform's universalism and Schurz's racial argument is the movement's central intellectual contradiction.
