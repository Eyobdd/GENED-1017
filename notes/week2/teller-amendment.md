## Summary (~200 words)

The Teller Amendment, passed April 20, 1898 as part of the joint resolution authorizing US military intervention in Cuba, is a four-clause document with an outsized historical significance. Its core provision — Section IV — explicitly disclaims any US intention to exercise "sovereignty, jurisdiction, or control" over Cuba and pledges that the US will "leave the government and control of the island to its people" once pacification is complete.

The Amendment was sponsored by Senator Henry Teller of Colorado and passed with remarkable speed, inserted into the war resolution before the United States had even formally entered the conflict with Spain. Its motivations were mixed: genuine anti-annexationist sentiment among some senators, concern about Cuban sugar competing with Colorado's sugar beet industry (Teller's own commercial interest), and political pressure from the anti-imperialist movement.

The Amendment's historical importance lies less in what it achieved than in what it failed to prevent. Five years later, the Platt Amendment (1901, incorporated into the Cuban Constitution 1901, ratified as a treaty 1903) installed a quasi-colonial protectorate that effectively nullified the Teller promise — maintaining US control without formal annexation. The Teller-to-Platt sequence is the clearest illustration of how anti-imperialist sentiment was formally acknowledged and then structurally circumvented in American foreign policy.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Non-annexation pledge**: The Teller Amendment's core commitment — unusual in American legislative history for explicitly preemptively forswearing territorial acquisition from a war the US had not yet begun. Sets a precedent for "liberation without annexation" that will be structurally complicated by the Platt Amendment.
- **Pacification**: The word the Amendment uses for the condition that must be achieved before the US will "leave" Cuba. "Pacification" is a contested term — it implies an ongoing US presence until order is restored, but does not define when or how that condition is met. The Platt Amendment later defines "pacification" in ways that justify permanent intervention rights.
- **Liberation vs. annexation**: The conceptual framework the Teller Amendment establishes for the Spanish-American War — the US as liberator, not colonizer. This framing will be repeated (and contested) across later occupation cases: Korea ("liberation" from Japan), Germany and Japan (liberation from fascism). The gap between liberation rhetoric and occupation reality is a course theme.
- **Congressional self-limitation**: The Teller Amendment is a rare case of Congress preemptively constraining executive war powers — and shows the political strength of anti-annexationist sentiment in 1898. That the Platt Amendment later circumvented this constraint reveals the limits of legislative anti-imperialism.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Platt Amendment**: The direct legislative successor and effective reversal. The Teller-Platt sequence is the essential paired reading of Week 2 — together they show how the US moved from "we will not annex Cuba" to "we will permanently intervene in Cuba whenever we judge it necessary," while maintaining the fiction of Cuban sovereignty.
- **Connects to Schurz / Anti-Imperialist League**: The Teller Amendment is a legislative victory for the anti-imperialist coalition — Congress responded to anti-annexationist pressure by building in the non-annexation pledge. Reading it alongside the League platform shows how anti-imperialist sentiment could shape legislation without preventing imperial outcomes.
- **Connects to Beveridge**: Beveridge's speech does not address Cuba directly (he focuses on the Philippines), but his logic — commercial and strategic necessity — explains why the Teller promise could not be kept. The US could not simply "leave" Cuba without sacrificing the Pacific and Caribbean strategic position Beveridge describes.
- **Connects to Kramer**: The Teller Amendment's racial subtext — Cuba's population was majority nonwhite, and many senators who voted for non-annexation did so partly to avoid incorporating a nonwhite population — is consistent with Kramer's argument that race structured both imperialist and anti-imperialist positions.
- **Connects to Chang ("Independence Without Liberation", Week 5)**: The Teller Amendment's promise of independence that morphs into protectorate anticipates the pattern Chang analyzes in Korea — formal declarations of liberation that mask continued external control. "Independence without liberation" could describe Cuba under the Platt Amendment regime.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*Set 1: Teller Amendment / Platt Amendment / Schurz*
"Non-annexation" / "protectorate" / "consent" — The Teller Amendment enacted the anti-imperialists' demand for non-annexation, but the Platt Amendment replaced formal annexation with a protectorate that maintained US control without consent. Schurz's argument — that governing without consent violates republican principles — was formally honored in Teller and substantively violated in Platt.

*Set 2: Teller Amendment / Beveridge / Kramer*
"Liberation" / "racial fitness" / "pacification" — The Teller Amendment's "pacification" condition built in an indefinite US presence; Beveridge's racial argument explains why pacification could never be complete; Kramer shows that both the liberation framing and the pacification condition were structured by racial assumptions about Cuban (and Filipino) capacity for self-government.

*Set 3: Teller Amendment / Chang / Atlantic Charter (Week 4)*
"Self-determination" / "liberation rhetoric" / "occupation reality" — The Teller Amendment's promise of self-determination after liberation is a template for later American occupation rhetoric. Chang's analysis of Korea shows the same gap between liberation promise and occupation reality; the Atlantic Charter (Week 4) repeats the self-determination language at a global scale, with similarly complicated results.
