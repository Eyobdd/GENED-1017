## Summary (~200 words)

Carl Schurz's *Policy of Imperialism* is the most sustained intellectual attack on American annexation of the Philippines published in 1899. A German-born Civil War general and former senator, Schurz brought to the debate both moral passion and political experience. His central argument is that imperialism fundamentally corrupts the American republic — by acquiring non-contiguous territories populated by racially "alien" peoples, the United States must either grant those peoples equal citizenship (which he sees as ruinous) or govern them as colonial subjects (which contradicts the republic's founding principles). Both options are catastrophic; therefore, the Philippines must not be annexed.

Schurz's argument is sophisticated but, as Kramer observes, deeply compromised by its racial premises. He does not argue that Filipinos have a right to self-determination as a matter of universal principle; he argues that they are unassimilable and that incorporating them would degrade American democracy. His anti-imperialism is thus a defense of the racial integrity of the republic, not a challenge to racial hierarchy as such. This makes Schurz an invaluable document for understanding the ideological limits of the anti-imperialist position — and for the midterm, a perfect illustration of Kramer's claim that both sides operated within the same racial grammar.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Anti-imperialism (classical republican)**: The argument that colonial empire is structurally incompatible with republican self-government — governing others without their consent corrupts the governing power as well as the governed. Schurz's primary intellectual framework.
- **Assimilation problem**: The practical objection to empire: if the Philippines are annexed, Filipinos must eventually become citizens, which Schurz treats as racially and politically catastrophic. This argument accepts rather than challenges racial hierarchy.
- **Benevolent assimilation (critique)**: Schurz directly contests McKinley's framing — "civilizing" a people you are shooting is a contradiction in terms. Conquest dressed as uplift is still conquest.
- **Republic vs. empire**: Schurz's central binary — a republic governs by consent of the governed; an empire governs by force. These are mutually exclusive forms; you cannot be both simultaneously.
- **Racial heterogeneity as political argument**: Schurz uses the argument that Filipinos are racially unfit for democratic institutions not to defend Filipino rights but to protect American institutions from "contamination." This is anti-imperialism in the service of white republican purity.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Beveridge**: Forms the direct debate pair — both speeches were addressed to the Senate in 1899-1900. Beveridge says racial inferiority requires American governance; Schurz says racial inferiority makes governance impossible and ruinous. Kramer shows both share the racial premise.
- **Connects to Kramer**: Kramer's central analytical claim — that anti-imperialists operated within the same racial grammar as imperialists — is directly evidenced by Schurz's assimilation argument. Reading Schurz through Kramer reveals the ideological limits of the anti-imperial position.
- **Connects to Fradera**: Schurz's "republic vs. empire" binary implicitly rejects Fradera's argument that the US had already been an empire internally. If the republic has always already been an empire (dispossessing Native Americans, enslaving Africans), Schurz's claim that empire is a departure from republican principles is less coherent.
- **Connects to Teller Amendment**: The Teller Amendment's refusal to annex Cuba enacts the Schurz position in legislative form — the Senate built in a prohibition on annexation precisely to avoid the assimilation problem Schurz articulates.
- **Connects to Mabini (Week 3)**: Schurz argues that Filipinos are resisting American rule "with arms" — he acknowledges the Philippine-American War in ways Beveridge minimizes. Mabini's manifesto is the Filipino voice Schurz uses as evidence but does not engage on its own terms.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*Set 1: Schurz / Beveridge / Kramer*
"Anti-imperialism" / "racial grammar" / "consent" — Schurz and Beveridge share the premise that Filipinos are racially unfit for self-government; they differ only on whether this justifies annexation or prohibits it. Kramer's "racial grammar" concept explains why the anti-imperialist Schurz nonetheless operated within the ideological framework of empire.

*Set 2: Schurz / Fradera / Teller Amendment*
"Republic vs. empire" / "exceptionality" / "non-annexation" — Schurz insists republic and empire are mutually exclusive; Fradera shows the US had already built an internal empire through racial exceptionality. The Teller Amendment represents the legislative enactment of Schurz's position — but Fradera would argue the distinction between republic and empire had always been unstable.

*Set 3: Schurz / Platt Amendment / Mabini*
"Benevolent assimilation" / "resistance" / "sovereignty" — Schurz argues that governing an unwilling people is conquest, not civilization. Mabini's manifesto is the Filipino expression of this same point. The Platt Amendment represents the US response: maintain control through legal protectorate mechanisms that avoid the word "conquest" while achieving its substance.
