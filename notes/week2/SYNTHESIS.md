# Week 2 Synthesis: Occupying Others — The Great Debate over Empire
**Case Study:** Philippines, Cuba, Puerto Rico
**Lectures:** The Great Debate over Empire / Varieties of Occupation

---

## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 2 assembles the most ideologically rich cluster of primary sources in the course: Beveridge's Senate speech for empire, Schurz and the Anti-Imperialist League against it, and the Teller and Platt Amendments as the legislative outcome. Kramer's essay is the analytical frame that makes the primary sources intelligible — and troubling.

The central irony Kramer reveals is that the "Great Debate over Empire" was not really a debate about Filipino humanity or rights. Both Beveridge and Schurz shared a racial premise: Filipinos were inferior and incapable of self-government. They differed only in their conclusions. Beveridge concluded that racial incapacity required American governance ("God has made us master organizers of the world to establish system where chaos reigns," p. 706). Schurz concluded that racial incapacity made incorporation ruinous to the republic — Filipinos could not be assimilated, therefore they should not be annexed. Kramer names this dynamic the **imperial racial grammar**: Filipinos were the object, not the subject, of a debate conducted entirely within a framework of their inferiority. The primary sources confirm Kramer's analysis devastatingly: read side by side, Beveridge and Schurz are arguing about what America owes itself, not what America owes the Philippines.

The Anti-Imperialist League platform introduces a tension internal to the anti-imperialist position. The platform's official language invokes universal consent ("all men, of whatever race or color, are entitled to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness"), but Schurz's speeches undermine this universalism at every turn. The gap between the League's official universalism and Schurz's racial practicality is the movement's intellectual contradiction — and a key reason it lost.

The Teller and Platt Amendments together enact the full arc of this debate in legislative form. Teller (1898) was an anti-imperialist victory: Congress preemptively disclaimed sovereignty over Cuba. Platt (1901) was the reversal: having promised not to annex Cuba, the US constructed a protectorate that maintained all the substance of colonial control — intervention rights, fiscal veto, naval bases — without the form. Fradera's "special regime" concept describes this structure perfectly; the Platt Amendment is exceptionality written into treaty law. The Teller-Platt sequence demonstrates that anti-imperialism could win legislative battles while losing the strategic and structural war.

Kramer's methodological argument ties the week together: American colonial racial ideology was not invented in 1898. It was assembled transnationally from Spanish colonial archives, British imperial precedents, and domestic American racial formations (Jim Crow, Native American dispossession). When American administrators arrived in the Philippines, they brought a hybrid toolkit. This is why the debate over what to do with the Philippines so quickly became a debate about race rather than law or interest — race was already the language through which American governance operated.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 1 (Fradera, "Reading Imperial Transitions"):** Fradera's framework is the essential scaffolding for Week 2. Three specific connections:

1. **Exceptionality → Platt Amendment.** Fradera's defining concept — the formal construction of inferior legal and political rules for colonial populations within an otherwise liberal system — is precisely what the Platt Amendment instantiates. Cuba is not a US state; it is not a US territory; but it operates under a "special regime" of permanent intervention rights, fiscal limitations, and base leases that has no equivalent for any sovereign nation with which the US maintains normal relations. The Platt Amendment is Fradera's exceptionality written in treaty language.

2. **Continuity thesis → Beveridge.** Fradera insists that American overseas empire in 1898 was not a rupture but an externalization of already-developed internal colonial practices. Beveridge's "master organizers" rhetoric draws directly on the language the US had used for a century to justify governing Native Americans without their consent. The "wardship" logic — incapable peoples require benevolent external governance until they mature — was simply exported to the Pacific.

3. **Dangerous symmetries → Schurz.** Fradera's argument that liberal empires generate anticolonial opposition by teaching their subjects the language of rights is foreshadowed by Schurz's observation that governing people "who are resisting our rule with arms" contradicts the republic's liberatory self-presentation. Schurz sees the contradiction but draws the wrong lesson (non-annexation rather than non-intervention); he does not acknowledge that the US had already been practicing this contradiction internally for decades.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 5 (Race, civilization, and imperial ideology)** is overwhelmingly dominant. Kramer's framework, Beveridge's speech, Schurz's racial republicanism, and even the Teller Amendment's racial subtext all converge here. Race was not incidental to the 1898 debate — it structured the entire field of possible arguments. The week's most important analytical move is recognizing that the racial grammar was shared across the political spectrum.

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is enacted in the Teller-Platt sequence: Teller asserts Cuban self-determination; Platt redefines it as self-determination conditioned on US approval of "adequate government." The question "who decides when a people is ready for self-government?" — answered always by the occupier — is the course's deepest running thread.

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy and planning — punitive vs. reformist)** appears in the contrast between Beveridge's bellicose "master organizers" language and McKinley's "benevolent assimilation" framing. The Philippines debate anticipates the punitive-vs.-reformist tension that will define occupation governance in later weeks.

**Theme 8 (Collaboration and resistance)** is introduced by Kramer's insistence on Filipino agency: Filipino elites navigated racial categories strategically, appropriating some American frameworks while contesting others. The Philippine-American War (mentioned in both Kramer and Schurz) is the moment of open resistance that forced ideological crisis on the "benevolent" civilizing mission.

## Combined Key Terms

- **Imperial racial grammar** (Kramer, p. 204): The shared framework within which both imperialists and anti-imperialists operated — Filipinos as racially inferior, incapable of self-government, objects rather than subjects of the debate. Neither Beveridge nor Schurz challenges this premise; they draw opposite policy conclusions from it.
- **Imperial formation** (Kramer, p. 201): Kramer's term for a colonial racial and governing structure that is contingent, unstable, assembled from multiple sources (Spanish, British, domestic US racial regimes), and requires constant maintenance. Not a coherent "system" but a contested assemblage.
- **Transnational history** (Kramer, p. 202): A methodological approach tracing ideas, people, and structures across colonial/national boundaries. Necessary because American colonial racial ideology was produced at the intersection of prior colonial formations, not invented domestically.
- **Civilizationist ideology** (Beveridge, pp. 706-708; Kramer, pp. 203-205): The belief that world's peoples occupy a racial hierarchy of "civilization," with Anglo-Saxons at the apex, obligating them to govern "inferior" peoples. Beveridge's speech is its purest senatorial expression in 1900.
- **Benevolent assimilation** (Beveridge, p. 705; Schurz, p. 8): McKinley's and Beveridge's framing of Philippine colonialism as a gift of civilization. Schurz demolishes this rhetorical move directly: "To govern people who have not consented to be governed, and who are resisting our rule with arms, is not to 'civilize' them but to conquer them."
- **Consent of the governed (racial limitation)** (Beveridge, p. 710; League Platform, p. 2): Beveridge argues the Jeffersonian principle of consent applies only to those "capable of self-government" — a racial limitation that turns liberal political theory into an instrument of colonial exclusion. The League invokes consent as universal; Schurz implicitly qualifies it racially.
- **Assimilation problem** (Schurz, pp. 4-6): Schurz's practical objection — if the US annexes the Philippines, Filipinos must eventually become citizens, which he treats as racially catastrophic. This is not a defense of Filipino rights but a defense of racial republican purity.
- **Non-annexation pledge / Teller Amendment** (Teller, Sec. IV): Congress's preemptive disclaimer of sovereignty over Cuba (1898) — a legislative anti-imperialist victory. Its significance is measured by what followed.
- **Pacification** (Teller, Sec. IV): The condition the Teller Amendment set for US withdrawal from Cuba — never defined, and later redefined by the Platt Amendment to justify permanent intervention rights. The word recurs across US occupation documents as a placeholder for open-ended control.
- **Informal empire / protectorate** (Platt Amendment; Fradera): Cuba under the Platt Amendment had nominal sovereignty but US intervention rights, fiscal veto, and permanent military bases. Fradera's "special regime" formalized in treaty law. Sovereignty in name, protectorate in practice.
- **Intervention clause** (Platt, Art. III): The US may intervene to preserve "adequate government adequate for protecting life, property, and individual liberty" — where the US defines "adequate." Invoked multiple times (1906, 1912, 1917, 1920) before abrogation in 1934. Guantanamo Bay lease (Art. VII) persists in 2026.
- **Forced constitutionalization** (Platt Amendment; see also Week 6): The US made military withdrawal from Cuba conditional on the Cuban Constitutional Convention accepting the Platt provisions. The Convention protested but had no choice. This precedent — occupation forces dictating constitutional content — reappears in Japan and Germany in Week 6.
- **Filipino agency** (Kramer, p. 206): Kramer's insistence that colonized Filipinos shaped, appropriated, and contested colonial racial structures rather than merely receiving them. The Philippine-American War is the moment of armed resistance that forced the civilizationist narrative to confront its own contradiction.
- **Anti-Imperialist League** (schurz-anti-imperialism.md): Founded Boston 1898; 500,000+ members at peak; included Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, Samuel Gompers, Jane Addams. Defeated when Senate ratified Treaty of Paris (February 1899) by one vote. Its coalition's breadth was also its weakness — no single coherent alternative vision.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: Civilizationist Ideology / Imperial Racial Grammar / Exceptionality** *(Beveridge + Kramer + Fradera)*
Beveridge's "God has made us master organizers" speech is the American popular expression of civilizationist ideology — the racial belief that Anglo-Saxon peoples are divinely obligated to govern inferior races. Kramer names this the "imperial racial grammar": both pro- and anti-imperialists shared the premise of Filipino racial inferiority and differed only in their conclusions. Fradera shows the structural form this ideology took — "exceptionality," the formal construction of inferior legal rules for colonial populations. Together, the three concepts describe American colonial governance as simultaneously an ideology (civilizationist), a discursive structure (racial grammar), and a legal architecture (exceptionality).

**Set 2: Teller Amendment / Platt Amendment / Special Regime** *(Teller + Platt + Fradera)*
The Teller-Platt sequence is the cleanest illustration of how anti-imperialist principles were formally acknowledged and then structurally circumvented. Teller (1898) disclaimed US sovereignty over Cuba; Platt (1901) created a protectorate that maintained all the substance of sovereignty — intervention rights, fiscal veto, naval bases — without the form. Fradera's "special regime" names exactly this structure: governing a population through exceptional rules that have no equivalent for territories with full constitutional standing. The Platt Amendment is exceptionality written into bilateral treaty law, permanent until 1934 (and Guantanamo still today).

**Set 3: Consent of the Governed / Anti-Imperialist League / Assimilation Problem** *(League Platform + Schurz + Beveridge)*
The League platform invokes universal consent as a constitutional requirement — "governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed." But Schurz, the League's leading intellectual, collapses this universalism with his "assimilation problem": Filipinos are racially unfit for democratic institutions, therefore incorporation would degrade the republic. Beveridge makes the same racial move but draws the opposite conclusion: racial incapacity justifies governance, not non-annexation. Kramer's most powerful observation is that neither side questioned the premise — consent was always already racially limited in the 1900 debate.

**Set 4: Benevolent Assimilation / Philippine-American War / Filipino Agency** *(Beveridge + Kramer + Schurz)*
McKinley's and Beveridge's framing of Philippine colonialism as the "benevolent assimilation" of a child race runs directly into the Philippine-American War — armed Filipino resistance that Schurz himself acknowledged ("resisting our rule with arms"). Kramer insists this resistance represented Filipino agency, not mere reaction: Filipino elites navigated colonial racial categories strategically, appropriating some American frameworks while contesting others. The war did not just kill people; it forced the ideological crisis of the civilizing mission, revealing conquest beneath the language of uplift. The gap between benevolent assimilation rhetoric and colonial war reality is one the course will track all the way to Korea and Japan.

**Set 5: Imperial Racial Grammar / Dangerous Symmetries / Liberation vs. Control** *(Kramer + Fradera + Teller/Platt)*
Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" — liberal empires propagate rights-language that colonized peoples deploy against them — connects directly to Week 2's debate. The Anti-Imperialist League's invocation of "consent of the governed" and the "spirit of 1776" against Philippine annexation is one instantiation of this dynamic; but Kramer shows that the racial grammar constrained even the anti-imperialists. The Teller Amendment's "liberation" promise and the Platt Amendment's "protectorate" outcome together demonstrate that liberation rhetoric can be both genuine (some senators really did oppose annexation on principle) and structurally insufficient (the imperial formation absorbed the anti-imperialist impulse and reproduced control in a new form).

## Essay Questions

1. Kramer argues that "both supporters and opponents of annexation often shared the premise of Filipino racial inferiority" and that "Filipinos were the object, not the subject, of the debate." Using Beveridge, Schurz, the Anti-Imperialist League platform, and Kramer's analytical framework, evaluate this claim. What are its limits? Was there any argument available in 1900 that genuinely challenged the imperial racial grammar rather than operating within it?

2. The Teller Amendment passed in 1898; the Platt Amendment passed in 1901. The first disclaimed US sovereignty over Cuba; the second created a permanent protectorate. Using Beveridge, Kramer, Fradera, and both amendments, explain the political and ideological logic by which anti-imperialist sentiment was formally acknowledged and then structurally circumvented. Is this pattern unique to Cuba, or does it describe a recurring structure in American occupation policy?

3. The acquisition of the Philippines in 1898 is sometimes described as a rupture — America's sudden entry into overseas empire. Using Fradera (Week 1), Kramer (Week 2), and at least two primary sources from Week 2, argue for or against the "continuity thesis": that 1898 was not a rupture but an externalization of domestic imperial practices already well-established. What does your answer imply about the "driving force" behind US expansion in 1898?
