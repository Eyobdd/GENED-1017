## Summary (~200 words)

The Platt Amendment is the legal instrument by which the United States converted its promise not to annex Cuba (Teller Amendment, 1898) into a permanent quasi-colonial protectorate. Originally attached by Congress as a rider to the Army Appropriations Act of 1901, it was then demanded as a condition for American military withdrawal and forced into the Cuban Constitution — over the objections of the Cuban Constitutional Convention, which accepted it only after being told the US would not withdraw its forces otherwise. It was later incorporated into a bilateral treaty in 1903.

The Amendment's eight provisions give the United States sweeping permanent rights: the right to intervene to "maintain Cuban independence" and protect life, property, and individual liberty (Article III); prohibition on Cuban treaties or foreign debts that might impair independence or US intervention rights (Articles I-II); validation of all US military acts during the occupation (Article VI); and, critically, the right to purchase or lease Cuban lands for coaling and naval stations (Article VII — the basis for the Guantanamo lease, still in force today).

The Amendment was abrogated in 1934 under FDR's Good Neighbor Policy — but the Guantanamo lease survived. The Platt Amendment is the definitive document of American informal empire: sovereignty in name, protectorate in practice, with permanent military presence as the enforcement mechanism.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Informal empire / protectorate**: The governing concept the Platt Amendment instantiates. Cuba has nominal sovereignty and is not formally annexed — but the US retains the right to intervene militarily, control its foreign and fiscal policy, and maintain permanent military bases. This is the model of control without annexation that Schurz said was impossible; Platt shows it was entirely feasible.
- **Intervention clause (Article III)**: The most consequential provision — the US can intervene whenever it judges that Cuban independence, or life, property, and liberty are at risk. "Adequate government" is defined by the US. This clause was invoked multiple times (1906, 1912, 1917, 1920) before the Amendment's abrogation in 1934.
- **Guantanamo Bay Naval Station**: The direct product of Article VII — leased in 1903, still operated by the US as of 2026. The Platt Amendment's abrogation in 1934 explicitly preserved the Guantanamo lease. A living legacy of the Amendment in the most literal sense.
- **Forced constitutionalization**: The method by which the Platt Amendment was inserted into Cuban law — the US made military withdrawal conditional on Cuban Constitutional Convention acceptance. The Convention resisted and protested but ultimately had no choice. This precedent (occupation forces dictating constitutional content) reappears in Japan (Week 6) and Germany (Week 6).
- **Good Neighbor Policy (1934)**: FDR's abrogation of the Platt Amendment as part of a broader renunciation of the right of unilateral intervention in Latin America — but the Guantanamo lease and the fundamental economic relationships it established persisted.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Teller Amendment**: The essential paired document. Teller (1898) promises non-annexation and self-government; Platt (1901) delivers protectorate and permanent intervention rights. The sequence reveals how anti-imperialist legislative commitments were honored in form while being reversed in substance.
- **Connects to Beveridge**: Beveridge's commercial and strategic arguments explain the Platt Amendment's logic — the US needed Cuba as a naval base and economic dependency for Caribbean and Pacific strategy. The Amendment is the legal architecture for the strategic position Beveridge demanded.
- **Connects to Kramer**: The Platt Amendment is what Kramer would call an "imperial formation" — it governs Cuba through racial exceptionality (nonwhite Cuba needs American guidance to maintain "adequate government") without formal colonial annexation. The racial premise is embedded in the intervention clause.
- **Connects to Fradera**: Fradera's "special regime" concept describes exactly the Platt Amendment's structure — Cuba is governed by rules (intervention rights, fiscal limitations, base leases) that have no counterpart in the US Constitution's treatment of states. This is exceptionality formalized in treaty law.
- **Connects to SWNCC documents (Week 5) and occupation planning**: The Platt Amendment's "adequate government" standard — where the occupying power defines adequacy — is structurally similar to the conditions the US set for ending occupations in Japan and Germany after WWII. The question "when is the occupied country ready for self-government?" is answered by the occupier in all cases.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*Set 1: Platt Amendment / Teller Amendment / Fradera*
"Protectorate" / "non-annexation" / "special regime" — The Platt Amendment fulfills Fradera's prediction: once the US decided not to annex Cuba (Teller), it needed a "special regime" to maintain control through exceptional rules rather than territorial incorporation. The Platt-Teller sequence is the Cuban instantiation of the general pattern Fradera describes for all modern empires.

*Set 2: Platt Amendment / Beveridge / Kramer*
"Intervention rights" / "adequate government" / "racial governance" — Beveridge's argument that the US must ensure "adequate government" among racially inferior peoples is encoded in the Platt Amendment's intervention clause. Kramer would identify this as racial governance without racial naming — the racial premise is structural, embedded in the definition of what constitutes adequate self-rule.

*Set 3: Platt Amendment / SWNCC 150 (Week 5) / Chang (Week 5)*
"Occupation conditions" / "self-government readiness" / "liberation without liberation" — All three documents involve an occupying power setting the conditions under which it will withdraw. The Platt Amendment defines "adequate government" as the withdrawal condition for Cuba; SWNCC 150 defines acceptable Japanese governance for the post-WWII occupation; Chang shows that Korea's "liberation" from Japan similarly failed to produce genuine self-determination. The pattern of conditioned liberation is consistent across six decades of American occupation.
