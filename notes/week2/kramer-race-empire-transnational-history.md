## Summary (~500 words)

Paul Kramer's essay is a methodological and historiographical intervention as much as a substantive argument. Writing in the context of the "new imperial history," Kramer asks how historians should write about American empire—and insists that the answer must be *transnational* and *racial*. His central claim is that **race was not merely an ideology that accompanied American empire but the organizing logic through which empire was constructed, justified, contested, and ultimately transformed**. Moreover, the racial formations that shaped American colonialism cannot be understood by looking at the United States alone; they were produced through encounters, borrowings, and conflicts that crossed colonial boundaries.

Kramer begins by situating the American empire of 1898 within a larger comparative context. The United States did not invent colonial racial governance—it inherited and adapted it from Spanish colonialism in Cuba and the Philippines and from British imperial practices in India and elsewhere. When American officers, administrators, and anthropologists arrived in the Philippines, they brought with them racial assumptions shaped by domestic US experience (particularly the treatment of African Americans and Native Americans) but also imported frameworks from British India and Spanish colonial archives. The result was a hybrid colonial racial formation—what Kramer calls an **imperial formation** characterized by its contingency and its multiple genealogies.

One of Kramer's key arguments is that the question of Filipino *capacity for self-government* was central to the pro- and anti-imperialist debates. Both sides invoked race, but in different registers. Imperialists like Beveridge argued that Filipinos were a child race requiring American tutelage—racially incapable of self-government and thus requiring benevolent despotism. Anti-imperialists like Schurz often agreed on racial inferiority but drew the opposite conclusion: if Filipinos could never be assimilated, incorporating them into the American polity would degrade the republic. Race thus structured the entire debate—even critics of empire often operated within, rather than against, its racial premises.

Kramer also emphasizes the role of **Filipino agency** in shaping colonial formations. Colonial racial ideology was not simply imposed from above; it was negotiated, contested, and partially appropriated by Filipino elites who sometimes used American racial frameworks strategically. The Philippine-American War itself forced Americans to confront Filipino resistance in ways that destabilized simple civilizationist narratives—fighting an insurgency required different ideological work than a simple "civilizing mission."

The essay's methodological contribution connects directly to the course's comparative structure. Kramer argues for a "transnational" history that follows ideas, people, and practices across colonial borders—tracking how American administrators in the Philippines borrowed from Spanish governors, how Philippine-American racial debates echoed Caribbean ones, and how anti-imperialist movements in the US drew strength from Filipino nationalist arguments. This framework is essential for understanding why the course pairs Week 2's Philippine case with later cases in Germany, Japan, and Korea: occupation is always a comparative and globally entangled phenomenon.

For the three course questions: Kramer addresses WHY Americans occupied (racial ideology, civilization mission, borrowed imperial logic) and WHAT they did (constructed racialized governing structures), while gesturing toward the HOW of eventual Filipino independence as an exhaustion of racial exceptionality under international pressure.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Imperial formation**: Kramer's term for a colonial racial and governing structure that is contingent, unstable, and assembled from multiple sources — not a coherent "system" but a contested assemblage of practices, ideologies, and institutions.
- **Transnational history**: A methodological approach that traces ideas, people, and structures across national/colonial boundaries rather than treating each empire or colony in isolation. Kramer argues this is necessary because colonial racial knowledge was produced comparatively.
- **Racial formation (colonial)**: The process by which race is constructed, naturalized, and institutionalized within a specific colonial context — shaped by prior colonial racial regimes (Spanish, British) and by domestic American racial politics (Jim Crow, Native American dispossession).
- **Civilizationist ideology**: The belief that non-European peoples exist at earlier "stages" of civilization and require tutelage or governance by more "advanced" races — the dominant justificatory framework for American empire in the Philippines.
- **Benevolent assimilation**: McKinley's official framing of American colonialism in the Philippines — presenting conquest as a gift of civilization, order, and eventual self-government. Kramer shows this was racial paternalism encoded as humanitarian policy.
- **Filipino agency**: The capacity of colonized Filipinos to shape, contest, appropriate, and resist colonial racial structures — not merely passive objects of American policy. Kramer insists on this against purely top-down accounts of empire.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Beveridge ("In Support of an American Empire")**: Kramer's analysis of civilizationist ideology and racial paternalism is directly illustrated by Beveridge's Senate speech. Beveridge is a primary source embodiment of the racial grammar Kramer describes — "God has not been preparing the English-speaking and Teutonic peoples for a thousand years for nothing."
- **Connects to Schurz ("Policy of Imperialism")**: Kramer's argument that anti-imperialists shared the racial premises of imperialists is key to reading Schurz critically. Schurz opposes annexation partly on racial grounds — Filipinos cannot be assimilated — which Kramer would identify as racial exceptionality in anti-imperial dress.
- **Connects to Fradera ("Reading Imperial Transitions")**: Both authors argue that American empire was not a sudden rupture in 1898 but drew on prior imperial formations (Spanish, British). Fradera focuses on legal/structural exceptionality; Kramer focuses on racial ideology. Together they provide a full picture of the borrowed toolkit of American colonialism.
- **Connects to Teller and Platt Amendments**: The amendments enact legally the tension Kramer describes — Teller prohibits formal annexation of Cuba (anti-imperialist impulse), while Platt installs a quasi-colonial protectorate (imperial practice). Both are comprehensible as negotiations of the racial question: how to govern without formally incorporating nonwhite populations.
- **Connects to May ("Business of Education in the Colonial Philippines")**: Kramer's argument about civilizationist ideology is institutionalized in the education system May analyzes. American schools in the Philippines were racial projects — teaching English, American history, and "civilization" as a means of colonial governance.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*Set 1: Kramer / Beveridge / Fradera*
"Racial ideology" / "civilizing mission" / "exceptionality" — Kramer argues that racial ideology was the *mechanism* of American empire, not merely its justification. Beveridge's civilizing-mission rhetoric is the popular expression of this ideology, while Fradera shows how exceptionality — the legal codification of racial difference — was the structural form it took in governing the Philippines and other new territories.

*Set 2: Kramer / Schurz / Teller Amendment*
"Anti-imperialism" / "racial grammar" / "non-annexation" — Kramer's insight that anti-imperialists shared the racial premises of imperialists is perfectly illustrated by pairing Schurz with the Teller Amendment. Schurz opposed Philippine annexation partly because Filipinos could not be assimilated; the Teller Amendment refused Cuban annexation for similar reasons. Both are anti-colonial in form but operate within an imperial racial logic.

*Set 3: Kramer / Fradera / Platt Amendment*
"Transnational race" / "exceptionality" / "protectorate" — Kramer and Fradera both show that American empire was constructed from borrowed colonial tools; the Platt Amendment is a direct example — modeled on British protectorate arrangements — of how the United States built a racial colonial order without formal annexation, maintaining control through legal exceptionality rather than direct territorial incorporation.
