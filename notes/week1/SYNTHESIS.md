**Case Study:** General / Comparative (Spain, Britain, United States)
**Lectures:** The US as Empire / Toward 1898

## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 1 has a single assigned reading — Fradera's "Reading Imperial Transitions" — which functions as the theoretical spine for the entire course. Rather than a dialogue between texts, the synthesis here is between Fradera's comparative framework and the two lectures ("The US as Empire" and "Toward 1898"), which translate his structural argument into American chronology.

Fradera's core move is to refuse American exceptionalism about empire. He shows that the US in 1898 was not a reluctant or accidental empire stumbling into overseas adventure — it was an already-mature colonial power whose internal practices (Native American dispossession, slavery, Jim Crow) were structurally identical to European colonial "special regimes." The "Toward 1898" lecture presumably thickens this story, showing how continental expansion created the economic surpluses, naval doctrine (Mahan), and racial ideologies that made overseas empire feel natural by the 1890s.

The key analytical contribution Fradera offers is the concept of **exceptionality**: modern empires are defined not by conquest per se but by the *formal construction of inferior rules* for colonial populations within otherwise liberal political systems. This is what allows liberal states to hold colonies without logical contradiction — they simply carve out legal exceptions. The insular cases (1901) are the American legal expression of a structure Spain, Britain, and others had already perfected.

This week thus equips students with a comparative vocabulary (exceptionality, dangerous symmetries, incorporated/unincorporated) before the course dives into case-specific material in weeks 2-7.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 5 (Race, civilization, and imperial ideology)** is the most active: Fradera's entire argument turns on how racial "heterogeneity" was invoked to justify exclusion from constitutional rights — *castas pardas* in Spain, nonwhite Crown colonies in Britain, Native Americans and then Filipinos in the US. The racial line is not incidental but structural to modern liberal empire.

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is introduced via "dangerous symmetries": the claim that liberal universalism generates its own anticolonial opposition. Every empire teaches the language of rights, then faces populations who use it. This tension will drive material in weeks 2-7.

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy & planning — punitive vs. reformist)** is set up implicitly: Fradera shows that "exceptional" colonial governance took many forms — from Spain's quasi-military captain-generals to Britain's sophisticated racial constitutionalism to the US insular cases. Later weeks will trace how these inherited repertoires shaped actual occupation policy.

## Combined Key Terms

- **Exceptionality / Special regime** (Fradera, p. 35): The formal construction of inferior political, legal, and fiscal rules for colonial possessions relative to the metropole; Fradera's defining feature of modern empire.
- **"Exception as the rule"** (Fradera, p. 45): The structural normalization of colonial exception — not a temporary emergency but the permanent operating logic of empire. Central to Discussion Question 1.
- **Dangerous symmetries** (Fradera, pp. 59-62): The inherent contradiction between liberal universalism and colonial exceptionality; colonized peoples use the metropole's own rights-language to demand equality, exhausting empires from within.
- **Incorporated vs. unincorporated territories** (Fradera, p. 55): Post-1901 American legal distinction; "unincorporated" territories (Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam) were owned by but not constitutionally part of the US, enabling indefinite colonial governance without extending full rights.
- **Insular cases (1901)** (Fradera, p. 55): Supreme Court decisions (*Downes v. Bidwell* et al.) establishing the unincorporated territory doctrine — the American legal architecture of empire.
- **Imperial transition** (Fradera, pp. 34-38): Overlapping, mutually influencing power structures rather than simple succession; empires coexist, borrow from, and compete with one another.
- **Feitoria** (Fradera, p. 37): Portuguese trading enclave model, earliest form of colonial commercial control; illustrates the deep trading-network roots of empire.
- **Castas pardas** (Fradera, p. 43): Free descendants of African slaves (~one-third of Spanish American population), excluded from Spanish constitutional citizenship in 1812 via the rhetoric of racial "heterogeneity" — paradigmatic case of exceptionality encoded in liberal law.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: Exceptionality / Insular Cases / "Exception as the Rule"**
Fradera argues that modern empires are defined by the construction of "special regimes" — formal inferiority of rights in colonial possessions relative to the metropole. The insular cases (1901) were the American legal expression of this logic: the Supreme Court ruled that the Philippines and Puerto Rico were "unincorporated" territories where Congress held "plenary powers" and the full Constitution did not apply. Far from being an improvised response to 1898, this was exceptionality becoming, as Fradera's section heading puts it, "the rule" — the normal operating mode of liberal empire.

**Set 2: Westward Expansion / Cherokee Nation / Philippines**
Fradera's "continuity thesis" insists that American overseas imperialism in 1898 was not a rupture but an exteriorization of already-developed internal colonial practices. The legal "wardship" doctrine established in *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) — treating Indigenous peoples as dependent nations incapable of full sovereignty — was recycled wholesale when the US governed the Philippines. Taft's paternalistic language about "wards" destined for eventual self-government reproduced a framework the American state had already perfected against Native peoples across the continent.

**Set 3: Dangerous Symmetries / Self-Determination / Anticolonial Nationalism**
Fradera's most powerful contribution to the course is the concept of "dangerous symmetries": liberal empires propagate the universalist language of rights and equality, which colonized peoples then deploy against the empires themselves. The contradiction is structural and irresolvable — you cannot simultaneously claim all men are born equal and maintain a formal regime of exclusion. This tension is the motor of anticolonial nationalism everywhere from the Philippines to Korea, and it explains why, as Fradera concludes, "all the empires became exhausted, sooner or later, in their struggle against the unstoppable leveling tide" (p. 62).

**Set 4: Racial Heterogeneity / Jim Crow / Castas Pardas**
All three represent the same structural move: invoking racial or social "heterogeneity" to exclude populations from rights formally guaranteed by liberal constitutions. Spanish liberals excluded *castas pardas* from the 1812 Constitution by arguing they were too socially mixed for citizenship. The US South institutionalized Jim Crow after Reconstruction using analogous reasoning. When the US acquired overseas territories, it imported this domestic repertoire — the Philippines were simply added to a long list of populations deemed not yet ready for full rights.

**Set 5: Imperial Transition / British Racial Calculus / American Irruption**
Fradera shows that 1898 was not America's sudden entry into a finished imperial world but its "irruption" into a system of competing, overlapping empires already in motion. Britain had refined its racial calculus — self-government for white dominions, exclusion for nonwhite Crown colonies — over decades. When the US acquired Spain's remnant empire, it drew on British precedents as well as its own. The insular cases' distinction between "incorporated" and "unincorporated" territories closely mirrors the British distinction between self-governing dominions and Crown colonies. American empire was not invented from scratch in 1898.

## Essay Questions

1. Fradera argues that American overseas imperialism after 1898 represented continuity with — not a departure from — the internal colonial practices of the preceding century. What is the strongest evidence for this "continuity thesis"? What are its limits? What might an emphasis on continuity obscure about the genuinely new challenges and choices Americans faced in governing overseas populations?

2. Fradera concludes that all modern liberal empires were ultimately undone by "dangerous symmetries" — the contradiction between universalist rhetoric and colonial exceptionality. Is this a satisfying explanation for why empires end? Drawing on Fradera's comparative framework, what additional factors — economic, military, geopolitical — might complement or complicate his argument?

3. Fradera treats "exceptionality" as the structural core of modern empire. How useful is this concept as a framework for analyzing the cases studied later in the course (Philippines, Germany, Japan, Korea)? Where does it illuminate American occupation policy, and where does it fall short?
