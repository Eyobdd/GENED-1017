## Imperial Structure & Theory

**Imperial transition** *(Fradera)*
The overlapping coexistence, rivalry, and exchange between successive empires — not a simple linear succession but a long period of mutual borrowing and competition. Fradera uses it to frame the Spanish–British–American sequence.

**Exceptionality / Special regime** *(Fradera)*
The systematic construction of inferior political, legal, and fiscal rules for colonial possessions relative to the metropole. Fradera's central argument: this is the *defining* feature of modern empire. Every subsequent week's readings show a specific form of it.

**"Exception as the Rule"** *(Fradera)*
Exceptionality was not a temporary emergency but the permanent, structurally normal operating logic of empire. All modern empires governed overseas territories under rules they would never apply at home.

**Dangerous symmetries** *(Fradera)*
The inherent contradiction between liberal universalism ("all men are born free and equal") and colonial exceptionality. As liberal values spread, colonized peoples used the metropole's own language of rights to demand equality — ultimately exhausting all empires.

**Informal empire** *(Fradera)*
British commercial and diplomatic dominance over nominally independent territories (e.g., Latin America after Spanish collapse) without formal political control. Fradera notes the scholarly debate about this form of dominance was largely exhausted by the 1970s.

## American Imperial Concepts

**Insular cases (1901)** *(Fradera)*
Supreme Court decisions (*Downes v. Bidwell*, etc.) establishing the constitutional status of territories acquired from Spain. Created the "incorporated" vs. "unincorporated" territory distinction — the latter "destined never to be a part of" the Union, allowing indefinite colonial governance without full constitutional rights.

**Incorporated vs. unincorporated territories** *(Fradera)*
Post-1901 legal distinction: "incorporated" = on path to statehood with full rights; "unincorporated" = owned by US but subject only to Congress's "plenary powers." Applied to Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam.

**Plenary powers** *(Fradera)*
Congress's near-absolute authority over "unincorporated" territories, derived from the territorial clause of the Constitution (Article IV, Section 3). Enabled racial exceptionality in colonial governance without violating the Constitution's letter.

**Wardship / Pupilage doctrine** *(Fradera)*
Legal doctrine from *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831): Native American nations were "domestic dependent nations" in a "state of pupilage" — incapable of full sovereignty, subject to federal tutelage. Applied directly to the Philippines after 1898 (Henry Cabot Lodge explicitly invoked Marshall's ruling).

## Race & Colonial Hierarchy

**Castas pardas** *(Fradera)*
Free descendants of African slaves, approximately one-third of the Spanish American population. Excluded from Spanish citizenship in the liberal Constitution of 1812 by invoking colonial "heterogeneity" — key example of racial exceptionality encoded in liberal law.

**"Racial balance"** *(Fradera)*
Term used by Spanish colonial authorities in mid-19th-century Cuba to describe their system of racial management — maintaining social order through calibrated racial hierarchy rather than formal legal equality.

**Divide et impera** *(Fradera)*
Colonial governing logic (used explicitly in British India): exploit existing social divisions (castes, religious communities, ethnic groups) to maintain control. Fradera identifies it as a general feature of imperial governance.

## Economic & Labor Structures

**Feitoria** *(Fradera)*
Portuguese trading enclave model — an offshore marketplace from which to trade, giving birth to the earliest model of colonial commercial expansion. Fradera traces modern empire's origins to this Phoenician/Greek commercial model.

**Fazenda** *(Fradera)*
Large Brazilian slave-based agricultural units producing sugar; the model spread to Dutch, English, and French Caribbean possessions and to North American plantation agriculture. Central to the second British empire's economic base.

**Situados** *(Fradera)*
Silver subsidies from New Spain's public finances that funded fortification and colonial defense across the Spanish empire (Havana, Puerto Rico, Philippines, Pensacola). Illustrates the integrated fiscal architecture of the Spanish imperial system.

## Key Legal & Constitutional Moments

**Northwest Ordinance (1787)** *(Fradera)*
Ambiguous US legislation on the extension of rights to western territories aspiring to become states — a key moment in American continental imperial expansion.

**Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831)** *(Fradera)*
Supreme Court decision defining Native American nations as "domestic dependent nations" in federal tutelage — the foundational precedent for treating colonized peoples as incapable of self-government, later applied to the Philippines.

**Foraker Act (1900)** *(Fradera)*
Organic law for Puerto Rico establishing US presence and "tutelage" — represented a *regression* in Puerto Rican rights relative to what they had gained under Spanish colonial policy between 1873 and 1897.

**Philippine Organic Act (1902)** *(Fradera)*
Established William Howard Taft as "governor-general" — terminology borrowed directly from British and Spanish colonial administration, institutionalizing American colonial governance in the Philippines.
