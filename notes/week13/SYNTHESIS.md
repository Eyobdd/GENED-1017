## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 13's readings form a nested temporal structure spanning fifteen years of Iraq policy: pre-war planning (Haass, 2002), the founding occupation decisions (Bremer memoir; CPA Orders 1 & 2, May 2003), mid-occupation assessment (Boot & Luttwak, 2005; Biddle, 2006; Crocker cable, 2007), and long-arc retrospective (Sky, 2017). Read together, they constitute a complete anatomy of how an occupation was planned, how that planning was ignored, how the decisions made in the occupation's first two weeks set the trajectory for the next fourteen years, and what that trajectory ultimately cost.

The week's organizing tension is between **planners who drew careful lessons from history** and **actors who invoked history selectively**. Haass's September 2002 State Department memo is the week's prophetic document: written five months before the invasion, it predicts virtually every subsequent failure. Avoid a security vacuum (CPA Order 2 created one by disbanding 400,000 armed men with no transition plan). Do not do full de-Baathification (CPA Order 1 did exactly this, purging the administrative class that ran every ministry, hospital, and university). Do not use the "MacArthur model" of unilateral proconsular governance (the CPA became that model). In post-conflict reconstruction, "there is no such thing as a clean slate." The Bush administration ignored every one of these warnings.

Bremer's memoir shows those lessons being overridden in real time — and the mechanism of overriding: Rumsfeld's directive ("actively oppose Saddam Hussein's old enforcers") arrived in Baghdad before Bremer did; de-Baathification was a Washington decision handed to a new administrator who enforced it within his first days. Bremer explicitly cites Germany and Japan as his historical templates — "Democracies don't work unless the political structure rests on a solid civil society" — while issuing the very order that destroyed the existing civil-society infrastructure. The invocation of WWII precedents here is not analogy; it is alibi. Haass had already warned that Iraq's "hardware" and "software" were different from Japan's, that no Hirohito figure existed to provide a unifying symbol, and that the multilateral approach was essential. Bremer did the opposite of all of this and described himself as following the right historical lessons.

The two CPA orders are the week's primary-source center of gravity, and the contrast between them and their WWII analogs illuminates the week's central theme. CPA Order #1 (De-Baathification, May 16) purged the top four party ranks across every institution; CPA Order #2 (Dissolution of Entities, May 23) disbanded the entire Iraqi military, intelligence apparatus, and security forces — approximately 400,000 suddenly unemployed, armed, and humiliated men, with officers of Colonel rank or above barred even from severance. Together these orders embody "tabula rasa" state-building: the theory that total demolition of existing institutions is the precondition for democratic reconstruction. This is the opposite of what WWII occupiers did in Japan and Germany, where significant bureaucratic infrastructure was retained while leadership was purged. The orders also demonstrate occupation-by-personal-decree: Bremer signed both within his first two weeks, under self-declared CPA authority, with no Iraqi approval mechanism.

The Boot-Luttwak symposium (2005) and Biddle's *Foreign Affairs* essay (2006) offer three distinct diagnoses of what went wrong — and none of them agrees with the others. Boot defends the strategic vision (Bush Doctrine as forward defense, democracy promotion as counterterrorism) while blaming implementation; Iraq is not different from WWII in its early-stage blundering. Luttwak attacks the premise using the Sicily analogy: when occupied peoples do not liberate themselves, they define post-liberation identity by rejecting everything the liberators stood for; Iraq is "the same but much worse" because religious distance, conspiratorial resentment, and mobilized Islamic identity compound the structural mechanism. Biddle cuts across both positions with an empirical move: the entire Washington debate — proponents and critics alike — assumes Iraq is Vietnam (a Maoist people's war), and that misdiagnosis is the root cause of bad strategy. Iraq is a *communal civil war*, in which hearts-and-minds is irrelevant (no uncommitted population exists), rapid democratization is actively harmful (it hardens sectarian identity rather than resolving it), and Iraqization — the bipartisan consensus "exit strategy" — accelerates conflict by giving Sunnis a Shiite-Kurdish military machine to fear. Biddle's prescription: delay Iraqization, use military leverage to coerce a communal power-sharing agreement, broaden amnesty for ex-Baathists. The Crocker cable, written just one year later, records Iraqi officials on the ground independently reaching the same conclusion about de-Baathification that Biddle reached theoretically.

Sky's 2017 retrospective completes the arc. The causation chain she traces — CPA Orders → state collapse → al-Qaeda in Iraq foothold → surge & Sunni Awakening reversal → Obama's 2010 election betrayal of Allawi → Maliki's Sunni exclusion → ISIS — shows how the founding decisions of May 2003 ramified for over a decade. Her central claim, that "ISIS is not the cause of Iraq's problems but a symptom of failed governance," is the long-run confirmation of Haass's 2002 argument: the quality of post-conflict governance, not the military outcome of invasion, determines whether occupation produces stability or chaos. Sky also identifies the one partial success — the Counter Terrorism Service, cross-sectarian, battle-proven, US-trained — as the model for what occupation institution-building should have looked like from the start.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 12 (Dower, "Lessons from Japan"/"The Other Japanese Occupation"):** The connection is direct and constitutes the week's analytical spine. Dower's seven conditions for Japan's occupation success function as a negative checklist for Iraq: no international legitimacy (US invaded over Security Council opposition); no unconditional surrender giving unchecked authority; a deeply fragmented occupied population with immediate insurgency; no prewar democratic traditions or intact bureaucracy to work through; no genuine reformist agenda with ideological commitment (Boot concedes Iraq had no "New Deal" equivalent); no calm 1945–47 window before Cold War distortion; no long planning period. Bremer invokes Japan explicitly; Haass had already warned against the Japan-as-model analogy for precisely the reasons Dower later documented. The "new zaibatsu" parallel Dower drew in Essay 2 (defense contractors replacing decartelization) is visible in the CPA's early economic orders — the reconstruction contracts went to connected US firms, not to Iraqi institution-builders. Luttwak's argument that Germany and Japan were "exceptions, not the rule" in occupation history — because they had prewar democratic traditions, no Islam to mobilize against occupiers, and total ideological defeat — independently reaches the same conclusion Dower's checklist produces.

**Week 10 (Montgomery, *Forced to be Free*; Ladejinsky retrospective):** Montgomery's "artificial revolution" framework maps onto Iraq with grim precision, but inverted. In Japan and Germany, the artificial revolution at least executed its first element (elite removal via denazification/purge) before the reverse course cut short the second and third (new democratic leadership; institutional guarantees). In Iraq, CPA Orders 1 and 2 executed elite removal without any parallel construction: no "strange partnership" of the Ladejinsky type emerged, no Hiroo Wada figure drove indigenous reform through the occupation's authority. The New Iraqi Corps sketched in Order #2's §5 — one paragraph, no timeline, no implementation — is the negative image of Ladejinsky's land reform program: the promise of construction without the substance. Montgomery's warning that "ambition outran performance" and that the negative phase (elite removal) was the easiest element to execute but the least sufficient for durable change describes Iraq perfectly.

**Week 5 (Dower Ch. 6 "Neocolonial Revolution"; SWNCC 150; JCS 1067; Chang "Independence Without Liberation"):** SWNCC 150 retained Japanese bureaucratic infrastructure while purging militarist leadership; JCS 1067 removed Nazi party members from public positions but ultimately required a pragmatic reverse course that rehabilitated enough administrators to keep Germany functioning. CPA Order #1 exceeded both by sweeping out not just senior political appointees but the entire professional class in every institution. Chang's "independence without liberation" for Korea — occupation that granted formal sovereignty without substantive self-determination — finds its Iraqi parallel in Bremer's "incremental" path to representative government, where "responsible" Iraqi leaders were defined by the CPA administrator, not elected. The exile/internal leader tension Bremer identifies in his ILC critique (he distrusts the exile-dominated council for the same reason he distrusted WWII-era governments-in-exile) mirrors Chang's analysis of how the US prioritized external actors with no popular mandate over internal political forces in Korea.

**Week 11 (Latham, *Modernization and Culture*; Eisenhower; Ho Chi Minh):** Biddle's central argument — that Washington's Iraq debate is imprisoned by the Vietnam analogy even when it tries to repudiate it — is the Week 11 connection in its deepest form. Vietnam so thoroughly structured US counterinsurgency doctrine (hearts-and-minds, Vietnamization, Oil-Spot strategy) that even critics of "Vietnamization" could not escape its framework when designing "Iraqization." Latham's argument that modernization theory was not a Cold War invention but a crystallization of imperial assumptions dating to 1898 applies equally to counterinsurgency doctrine: COIN theory carries the same tutelage logic. Boot's "forward defense" as the Bush Doctrine's strategic rationale — democracy promotion as counterterrorism — is Latham's modernization theory in its post-9/11 military idiom: the same belief that implanting liberal institutions in "underdeveloped" polities eliminates the conditions for anti-American violence.

**Weeks 5–7 (Japan/Germany/Korea occupation sequence):** The de-Baathification/de-Nazification comparison is the week's most explicit historical analogy and its most instructive failure. In Germany, JCS 1067 was ultimately walked back when it became clear that Germany needed its trained administrators to function; the "reverse course" that rehabilitated ex-Nazis into West German state-building was the pragmatic concession to institutional necessity. In Iraq, the CPA drew no such lesson. The Diyala provincial officials in the Crocker cable make precisely this point — that Ba'ath Party membership was coerced, not chosen (membership was required for career advancement, just as Nazi party membership was for many German professionals), and that a "bloody hands" standard distinguishing coerced members from criminals was both feasible and necessary. The Germany case provides the exact precedent for this: denazification's ultimate pragmatism, which rehabilitated technocrats into the West German state, was what the CPA should have replicated and did not.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy & planning — punitive vs. reformist)** reaches its most dramatic expression in Week 13. CPA Orders 1 and 2 together constitute the most punitive opening acts of any occupation the course has examined — more sweeping than JCS 1067 for Germany (which was eventually revised), more comprehensive than any Japanese purge (which retained the emperor and the bureaucracy). They were also the least planned: Order #2's promise of a "New Iraqi Corps" consists of one paragraph with no timeline, no staffing plan, no budget. The contrast with Haass's detailed pre-war memo, which anticipated nearly every requirement and failure mode, illustrates a recurring course theme: planning capacity existed, and the decision to ignore it was itself a policy choice. Punitive occupation is often not chosen consciously — it is what reformist occupation degrades into when planning is abandoned.

**Theme 2 (Democratic reform & constitutionalism)** is complicated by Biddle's argument in its most direct form yet. The earlier weeks' readings assumed that democratic institutions — constitutions, elections, parties — were the goal of occupation and that the difficulty was implementation. Biddle inverts this: in a communal civil war, imposing elections before a communal security compromise hardens sectarian divisions and makes democratic governance less likely, not more. Rapid democratization is the wrong sequence, not just the wrong implementation. Sky's account of the 2010 election — where the US backed the wrong candidate for strategic reasons, undermining the democratic process it had invested in building — shows that democratic constitutionalism is not just a matter of drafting good texts; it requires the occupier to honor electoral outcomes even when they produce inconvenient results.

**Theme 4 (Cold War reversal / "reverse course")** operates in Week 13 with a distinctive twist: Iraq's "reverse course" ran in the opposite direction from Japan and Germany. Those occupations began with genuine reformist agendas that the Cold War then truncated toward strategic stabilization. Iraq began with no reformist agenda — the CPA had no land reform, no labor rights program, no Sirota drafting democratic constitutional guarantees — and then attempted reform (the 2007 surge, the Sunni Awakening incorporation) only after strategic failure forced a reassessment. Sky's arc makes this inversion visible: the surge was the belated introduction of political sensitivity (engaging Sunni communities) that the initial occupation should have prioritized. The Sunni Awakening that the surge enabled, and Maliki's subsequent betrayal of it, shows that even belated reformist impulses can be reversed by the same Cold War-style calculation (backing a "stable" strongman over a democratic process) that distorted the WWII occupations.

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is at the center of the week's political argument. Bremer's "incremental" formula for sovereignty transfer is the most explicit statement in any course reading of what self-determination means on the occupier's terms: "we are prepared to cede increasing responsibility to *responsible* Iraqi leaders" — where "responsible" is defined by the CPA administrator, not by Iraqis. The Diyala officials' cross-sectarian consensus in the Crocker cable — that the de-Baathification policy failed, that coerced membership should not equal permanent exclusion — is the most vivid example of occupied people's judgment being systematically overridden even when it was correct and even when it was expressed by the officials the CPA itself had empowered. Sky's account of the 2010 election and its manipulation is the endpoint: the formal apparatus of self-determination (elections, ballots, results) was in place, and the US chose not to honor it.

**Theme 8 (Collaboration & resistance by occupied peoples)** generates Week 13's most significant case studies. The Sunni Awakening is the occupation's most successful collaboration success: Sunni tribal leaders switched sides and fought al-Qaeda in exchange for political incorporation promises — a genuine strategic partnership that reversed the occupation's trajectory. Its betrayal by Maliki (reneging on promises, labeling Awakening leaders "terrorists") is the collaboration-turned-resistance case: the same actors who collaborated when given a credible stake became the constituency ISIS exploited when that stake was withdrawn. The Diyala provincial officials in the Crocker cable represent a subtler collaboration/resistance dynamic — they accepted positions in an occupier-structured system while speaking honestly about the system's failures. Bremer's ILC encounters illustrate the exile/internal tension that Chang diagnosed for Korea: actors with no popular mandate cannot function as genuine intermediaries between occupier and occupied.

## Combined Key Terms

*Deduplicated across all Week 13 readings, with cross-references.*

- **CPA Order No. 1 (De-Baathification, May 16, 2003):** Coalition Provisional Authority decree signed by Bremer removing all Ba'ath Party members from public employment; purged top four party ranks and the top three management layers of every ministry, university, and hospital. Explicitly modeled on denazification (JCS 1067, W5) but far more sweeping — no pragmatic revision followed. Cross-reference: JCS 1067 (W5); Haass's warning against full de-Baathification (W13); Dower's reverse-course-in-Germany as the missing analog (W12).

- **CPA Order No. 2 (Dissolution of Entities, May 23, 2003):** Disbanded the entire Iraqi military, intelligence services, and security apparatus; dismissed ~400,000+ personnel; barred officers (Colonel and above) from severance or pension; dissolved Republican Guard, intelligence services, and key regime organs. Created the security vacuum Haass had predicted and the armed, aggrieved Sunni insurgent class Sky documents. Cross-reference: SWNCC 150's retention of Japanese bureaucratic infrastructure (W5); Haass's "security vacuum" warning (W13); Dower's emphasis on non-insurgent occupied population as a precondition of Japan's success (W12).

- **Tabula rasa state-building:** The theory implicit in CPA Orders 1 and 2 — that total erasure of existing state institutions is the precondition for democratic reconstruction. The opposite of what WWII occupiers did in Japan and Germany, where significant bureaucratic capacity was retained while political leadership was purged. Cross-reference: Ladejinsky's "strange partnership" (W10); Montgomery's artificial revolution's three-part program (W10); JCS 1067's pragmatic revision (W5/W12).

- **"MacArthur model":** Haass's term for unilateral proconsular occupation — a single supreme US authority governing by decree without multilateral legitimacy or Iraqi approval mechanisms. Haass explicitly rejected this for Iraq; the CPA became exactly this. Cross-reference: MacArthur's actual Japan occupation (W5–W7); Dower's seven conditions for Japan's success, including international legitimacy (W12).

- **"Bloody hands" standard:** Criterion proposed by Diyala provincial officials (Crocker cable, 2007) for distinguishing rehabilitable Ba'athists (those who joined for career necessity) from those who committed crimes under Saddam. The pragmatic standard that Germany's reverse-course eventually applied to ex-Nazis; the CPA refused to apply it to ex-Ba'athists. Cross-reference: JCS 1067's eventual rehabilitation of German technocrats (W5/W12); Haass's recommendation to "preclude only a small number" (W13).

- **Communal civil war (Biddle):** Biddle's diagnostic category for Iraq — conflict between ethnic and sectarian groups (Sunni, Shiite, Kurd) driven by mutual existential fear, not ideology or class. Distinguished from a Maoist "people's war" (Vietnam), in which an uncommitted population can be won through good governance. Hearts-and-minds COIN strategy is inapplicable; rapid democratization is actively harmful; Iraqization accelerates conflict. Cross-reference: Domino theory and Vietnam analogy (W11); SWNCC 150's assumption that Japan's population was winnable through democratic governance (W5).

- **People's war (Maoist):** Class-based insurgency competing for an uncommitted population's allegiance through ideology, nationalism, and promises of better governance. Vietnam paradigm. Hearts-and-minds logic applies here but not to communal civil war. Cross-reference: NLF strategy (W11); Eisenhower's strategic rationale (W11).

- **Iraqization:** Bush administration policy of building Iraqi national security forces so coalition forces could "stand down" — analog of Nixon's Vietnamization. Biddle's target: in a communal war, "national" forces are perceived by Sunnis as a Shiite-Kurdish militia, inflaming tensions and foreclosing constitutional compromise before it can take root. Cross-reference: Vietnamization (W11); Wada's "strange partnership" as the positive counter-model of enabling indigenous capacity (W10).

- **Security dilemma (communal):** Dynamic in which each communal group fears mass violence at rivals' hands if it cedes any advantage, making compromise seem worse than ongoing low-intensity war. The structural driver of Iraq's communal civil war in Biddle's analysis; the reason economic reconstruction ("fixing the sewers") cannot substitute for communal security guarantees. Cross-reference: Biddle's empirical evidence — 85% of insurgent attacks in four Sunni provinces (W13).

- **"Sicily analogy" (Luttwak):** Luttwak's comparative claim: Sicily, liberated by outside Piedmontese forces in 1860, still suffers systemic misgovernance 145 years later because Sicilians define identity by rejecting what the liberators imposed. Iraq repeats this "but much worse" — religious and cultural distance, conspiratorial resentment, and mobilized Islamic identity compound the mechanism. Cross-reference: Dower's seven conditions (W12); Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" — liberal empire teaching the language of rights generates populations who turn it against the occupier (W1).

- **"Forward defense" (Boot):** Post-9/11 strategy of attacking terrorism proactively — killing or detaining terrorists before they strike, denying sanctuaries, promoting liberal democracy as an alternative to radicalism. Boot's defense of the Bush Doctrine; the geopolitical equivalent of Latham's modernization theory. Cross-reference: Latham's "Point Four" program and its continuity with colonial tutelage logic (W11); Eisenhower's domino theory as the Cold War version of the same forward-engagement rationale (W11).

- **Sunni Awakening (Sahwa):** 2006–2007 movement of Sunni tribal leaders who allied with US forces against al-Qaeda in Iraq in exchange for political incorporation. The surge's success depended on this collaboration. Maliki's subsequent betrayal — labeling Awakening leaders "terrorists," excluding them from political power — is Sky's proximate cause of ISIS's rise. Cross-reference: Collaboration/resistance typology across course cases; the KDP's land reform obstruction in Korea (Kim, W7) as a counter-case of indigenous collaboration-as-obstruction.

- **Popular Mobilization Units (PMUs / Hashd al-Shaabi):** Iranian-backed Shiite militias formed after Sistani's 2014 fatwa; militarily effective against ISIS but politically corrosive — they undermine state legitimacy, spread Sunni fear, and give Iran structural leverage in Iraqi politics. Sky's model of the double-edged collaborator: necessary to the occupation's security but destructive to the democratic goal. Cross-reference: Peshmerga Kurdish militias as a parallel (W13/Bremer); Sunni insurgency as the mirror dynamic.

- **Counter Terrorism Service (CTS):** Elite, cross-sectarian US-trained Iraqi force; bore the brunt of the Mosul campaign; Sky calls it the "most successful U.S. initiative in Iraq since 2003." Model for what successful occupation institution-building looks like: cross-sectarian, combat-proven, loyal to the state rather than to a communal faction. Cross-reference: Ladejinsky's "strange partnership" as the WWII precedent for enabling indigenous institution (W10).

- **De-Baathification vs. denazification:** The week's most explicit comparative pairing. Both policies purged ruling-party members from public life; Germany's was eventually walked back pragmatically when US realized trained administrators were needed; Iraq's was not, producing sustained governance collapse. Haass predicted this; Bremer invoked the analogy to justify the policy. Cross-reference: JCS 1067 (W5); Dower on the "reverse course" rehabilitating ex-Nazis (W12); Crocker cable's "bloody hands" standard as the missing pragmatic revision (W13).

- **"Inflection points" (Sky):** Sky's recurring analytical term for moments when US policy choices determined Iraq's trajectory — 2003 (CPA orders), 2007 (surge), 2010 (election manipulation), 2014 (ISIS), 2017 (post-Mosul). The concept formalizes what Haass's memo predicted: that policy choices at critical junctures compound, making early decisions vastly more consequential than later corrections. Cross-reference: Montgomery's "artificial revolution cut short" (W10) as the WWII equivalent of a missed inflection point.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: De-Baathification / Denazification / Reverse Course**

Bremer invoked denazification as the legitimating precedent for de-Baathification, but the comparison collapses on inspection. Germany's JCS 1067 was eventually revised — a pragmatic reverse course that rehabilitated ex-Nazis into West German state-building when the alternative was administrative paralysis. De-Baathification had no equivalent revision. Iraq's "reverse course" ran in the opposite direction from WWII precedent: it started punitive and stayed punitive, while Japan and Germany began reformist and then reversed toward stability. The lesson the CPA should have drawn — that effective purges require distinguishing coerced membership from criminal complicity — was the lesson Germany eventually learned and Iraq never applied. Haass's memo contained this warning in 2002; the Diyala officials in the Crocker cable were still making the same argument in 2007.

**Set 2: Tabula Rasa / Security Vacuum / Sunni Insurgency**

CPA Orders 1 and 2 embody tabula rasa state-building: the theory that total demolition of existing institutions precedes democratic reconstruction. The immediate result was a security vacuum — no police, no military, no administrative class — that Haass had specifically warned was the most dangerous moment in any post-conflict reconstruction. Warlords, crime syndicates, and eventually sectarian militias fill voids rapidly; once entrenched, they become impossible to dislodge without the political violence that destroys the stability the reconstruction aimed to create. In Iraq, the vacuum was filled most decisively by the Sunni insurgency — 400,000 suddenly unemployed, armed, and humiliated men whose organizational skill, military training, and accumulated grievances the CPA had created in a single week of decrees.

**Set 3: Communal Civil War / Iraqization / Constitutional Compromise**

Biddle's diagnostic argument is the week's most consequential theoretical intervention: Iraq is a communal civil war, not a people's war, and this distinction determines which strategies can succeed. Iraqization — building Iraqi national forces before a communal compromise — is dangerous precisely because "national" institutions are instruments of communal dominance in a war fought along ethnic and sectarian lines. Sunnis perceive the Iraqi Army as a Shiite-Kurdish militia regardless of its nominal nationality. The correct sequence, Biddle argues, is the inverse: use military leverage to coerce a constitutional power-sharing agreement, then build security forces to defend the resulting order. Sequence matters — compromise first, institutions second. The failure to follow this sequence produced the political conditions Sky documents: a Shiite-dominated government whose exclusion of Sunnis created the ISIS recruitment base.

**Set 4: Sicily Analogy / Seven Conditions / Artificial Revolution**

Three frameworks from different weeks converge on the same diagnostic conclusion about Iraq. Luttwak's Sicily analogy argues that liberation from outside generates identity-based resistance — Iraqis construct their sense of self by rejecting what American liberators stood for. Dower's seven conditions checklist (Week 12) establishes that Japan's occupation success required enabling factors Iraq uniformly lacked. Montgomery's "artificial revolution" framework (Week 10) shows that even the WWII occupations, which possessed several of these conditions, only executed the first element (elite removal) durably; the second and third elements (new democratic leadership; institutional guarantees) were cut short by Cold War pressure. Iraq failed on all three frameworks simultaneously: the structural mechanism of external liberation worked against it (Luttwak); it lacked the specific enabling conditions (Dower); and the occupation never even attempted the constructive elements of artificial revolution (Montgomery).

**Set 5: Haass Warning / MacArthur Model / Potters Barn Rule**

Haass's 2002 memo explicitly rejected the "MacArthur model" of unilateral proconsular occupation, predicted that it would be perceived as an imperial oil grab, and recommended a multilateral "national coalition" under UN auspices. The Bush administration chose the MacArthur model anyway — the CPA was precisely the structure Haass warned against. The memo's deepest insight is its "Pottery Barn rule" logic: "once we cross the Rubicon by entering Iraq and ousting Saddam ourselves, we will have much greater responsibility for Iraq's future." This is the occupation logic that runs from the Philippines (we cannot abandon the Filipinos we liberated) through Korea (we cannot withdraw and leave the south vulnerable) through Iraq (we own the aftermath of what we started). Haass understood that ownership of military outcomes entails ownership of political consequences — and that the MacArthur model, by concentrating that ownership in a single American authority, foreclosed the multilateral burden-sharing that might have made the responsibility bearable.

## Essay Questions

**1.** "There is no such thing as a clean slate," Haass warned in 2002. CPA Orders 1 and 2, issued in May 2003, operated on precisely the opposite premise. Using the Iraq readings alongside at least two prior case studies (Germany, Japan, Korea, Philippines), evaluate: when is it possible to "start over" in occupation, and what conditions determine whether institutional demolition serves or undermines the occupation's democratic goals?

**2.** Both Biddle and Luttwak argue that the Washington policy consensus about Iraq is built on a wrong historical analogy — but they identify *different* wrong analogies (Vietnam vs. Germany/Japan). Using evidence from Weeks 11–13, analyze how historical analogies function in occupation policy debates: what do they illuminate, what do they obscure, and what are the political stakes of choosing one comparison over another?

**3.** Sky argues that the 2010 Iraqi election — when the US backed Maliki over Allawi despite Allawi's electoral victory — was the single most consequential inflection point in Iraq's post-invasion trajectory. Compare this to at least two earlier moments in course history when occupiers overrode electoral or political processes in the name of strategic stability. What does this recurring pattern reveal about the relationship between self-determination and imperial control in US occupation policy?
