# Defending and Advancing Freedom: A Symposium (Boot & Luttwak contributions)
**Author:** Max Boot; Edward N. Luttwak | **Week:** 13 | **Type:** PS
**Case Study:** Iraq / Afghanistan (with explicit comparisons to Germany and Japan)
**Pages:** 23-24 (Boot), 47-48 (Luttwak) | *Commentary*, November 2005, Vol. 120, No. 4

## Summary

Two symposium contributors offer sharply opposed verdicts on the Bush Doctrine in November 2005, two and a half years into the Iraq War — a debate that doubles as a reckoning with the entire American tradition of occupation and nation-building.

**Max Boot (pp. 23-24)** is an unqualified defender of the Bush Doctrine as the necessary successor to Cold War containment. He catalogues early victories — the fall of the Taliban and Baathists, nascent democracies, Libya's WMD renunciation, the A.Q. Khan network's breakup — as proof that "forward defense" through regime change and democracy promotion works. His critique is entirely of implementation: Pakistan, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia have faced no consequences for harboring extremism; post-invasion planning in Iraq was catastrophically inadequate. But Boot insists the strategic vision is sound, likens Bush to Truman (reviled in office, vindicated by history), and dismisses calls for withdrawal. His argument is continuous with classical empire-boosterism: American hegemony produces liberal order, and failure reflects incompetence rather than hubris.

**Edward Luttwak (pp. 47-48)** rejects the entire premise using a striking historical analogy: Sicily, liberated from Bourbon rule by Piedmontese northerners in 1860, still suffers systemic misgovernance 145 years later because the Sicilians "did not liberate themselves but were instead liberated by northerners." When liberation comes from outside, the occupied define their identity by rejecting everything the liberators stand for. Iraq is "the same but much worse": religiously and ethnically distinct liberators, a ubiquitous Islam the occupiers are seen as subverting, and conspiracy theories in which Americans are secretly fomenting Sunni-Shia civil war. Luttwak's devastating comparative move: Germany and Japan in 1945 had "aborted prewar democracies only had to be rehabilitated… no Islam standing in the way… years of bloody warfare had… utterly discredited their ideologies." Iraq has none of these preconditions. The occupation will add "generations if not centuries" to the process of self-governance.

Together the two pieces bracket the central course debate: when does external imposition of democracy succeed, and when does it produce the opposite?

## Key Terms
- **Bush Doctrine**: Post-9/11 foreign policy combining pre-emptive military action, regime change, and democracy promotion as a counter-terrorism strategy; Boot defends it as the natural heir to Cold War containment
- **Forward defense**: Boot's term for the strategy of attacking terrorism at its source rather than relying on homeland security; the rationale for both Afghanistan and Iraq invasions
- **Liberation from outside / passive liberation**: Luttwak's framework — when occupied peoples play no active role in their own liberation, they define post-liberation identity against the liberator rather than through positive nation-building
- **Sicily syndrome**: Luttwak's analogy for structural misgovernance produced by external liberation; extends his thesis beyond Iraq to a general claim about occupations that lack indigenous buy-in
- **Neoconservatism**: The ideological tradition Boot represents; belief in democracy promotion through military power as both morally correct and strategically sound
- **Sectarian civil war / Sunni-Shia division**: The specific form Iraqi resistance took by 2005; Luttwak cites Iraqis' conspiracy theories about American instigation as evidence of the identity-rejection dynamic

FRONT: What is Luttwak's "Sicily analogy" and how does it apply to Iraq?
BACK: Sicily was liberated from Bourbon rule by outside (Piedmontese) forces in 1860; 145 years later it remains mismanaged because Sicilians define identity by rejecting what liberators imposed. Iraq repeats this but worse: religious and cultural distance, conspiratorial resentment, and Islamic identity mobilized against occupiers mean liberation adds generations to self-governance. | Boot & Luttwak symposium, pp. 47-48 | Core critique of the Germany/Japan analogy

FRONT: How does Boot justify the Iraq War despite admitting "the war has been bungled in many respects"?
BACK: Boot separates strategic vision (correct) from implementation (flawed). He argues all wars involve blunders and that final victory will retroactively vindicate mistakes; invokes Truman precedent. The Bush Doctrine — kill terrorists, deny sanctuary, promote liberal democracy — remains sound regardless of execution failures. | pp. 23-24 | Classic imperial logic: failures are about method, not mission

FRONT: What makes Germany and Japan the exception rather than the rule for successful occupation, according to Luttwak?
BACK: Three factors absent in Iraq: (1) prewar democratic traditions that could be "rehabilitated" rather than built from scratch; (2) no Islam to mobilize religious identity against occupiers; (3) total military defeat that "utterly discredited" fascist ideologies and their leaders. Iraq shares none of these preconditions. | p. 48 | Crucial counter to pro-intervention analogies

FRONT: What does Boot mean by "forward defense"?
BACK: A strategy of attacking terrorism proactively — killing or detaining terrorists before they strike, denying them sanctuaries, and undermining recruitment by promoting liberal democracy as an alternative. Boot presents it as the only rational response to 9/11, contrasting it with the "law-enforcement approach" he blames for the attacks. | p. 23 | Ideological rationale behind both Afghanistan and Iraq invasions
