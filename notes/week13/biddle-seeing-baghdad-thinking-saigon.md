# Seeing Baghdad, Thinking Saigon
**Author:** Stephen Biddle | **Week:** 13 | **Type:** RR
**Case Study:** Iraq (with Vietnam as comparative foil)
**Pages:** 2-14 (*Foreign Affairs*, Vol. 85, No. 2, Mar/Apr 2006)

## Summary

Biddle's central argument is a diagnostic intervention: the entire Washington debate about Iraq — pro-war, antiwar, left, and right — shares one fatal assumption: that Iraq is Vietnam, and the task is to learn the right lessons from that war. Biddle argues this assumption is wrong, and that misdiagnosing the conflict type is the root cause of bad strategy.

**The misdiagnosis:** Vietnam was a Maoist *people's war* — a class-based, ideological insurgency competing for the allegiance of an uncommitted population. The strategic logic of a people's war centers on hearts and minds: whichever side better governs and wins popular loyalty wins. This framework underlies both "Vietnamization" (Nixon's strategy of handing off ground combat to South Vietnamese forces) and its Iraq analog, "Iraqization" (Bush's policy of standing up Iraqi security forces so coalition forces can "stand down"). It also underlies the hearts-and-minds COIN approach favored by reformist critics of the war, and the withdrawal argument of opponents.

**The correct diagnosis:** Iraq is a *communal civil war* — conflict between ethnic and sectarian groups (Sunni, Shiite, Kurd) driven not by ideology or class but by existential fear. In communal wars, there is no pool of uncommitted citizens to win over through good governance. The government is itself an instrument of one communal faction. The underlying dynamic is a *security dilemma*: each group fears that if rivals gain power, mass violence or genocide will follow. The evidence is stark: 85% of insurgent attacks occur in the four Sunni-majority provinces; violence maps onto communal affiliation, not ideology.

**Why Vietnam-era remedies fail in Iraq:**
1. *Hearts and minds* — irrelevant when survival trumps prosperity and no uncommitted population exists to persuade.
2. *Rapid democratization* — actively harmful; in an immature polity, electoral competition mobilizes communal fear and hardens sectarian divisions rather than resolving them.
3. *Iraqization* — the most dangerous policy. Building Iraqi national forces before a communal compromise hands military power to a Shiite-Kurdish coalition that Sunnis rationally perceive as a sectarian militia. This inflames the conflict and forfeits Washington's primary leverage. Integrating Sunnis into mixed units creates infiltration vulnerabilities; segregating them creates parallel armed factions.

**Biddle's prescription:** Washington must delay Iraqization and instead use *military leverage* — the credible threat to shift the balance of power among factions — to coerce all parties into a constitutional power-sharing agreement. Only after a stable communal compromise is reached should indigenous security forces be built up. Concurrently, Washington should broaden amnesty for ex-Baathists and insurgent leaders to create credible Sunni negotiating partners, and stop imposing democratization deadlines that polarize factional politics before compromises can take root.

The stakes are high: without a communal compromise, Iraqization will produce either an exclusionary Shiite-Kurdish force (effective but divisive) or an integrated force penetrated by insurgents (inclusive but ineffective). Either path forecloses the only durable exit — a constitutional deal protecting all parties. Biddle's closing is pointed: understanding the war correctly cannot guarantee success, but misunderstanding it as Vietnam guarantees failure.

**Course themes activated:** Occupation policy & planning (punitive vs. reformist); democratic reform & constitutionalism; self-determination vs. imperial control; collaboration & resistance.
**Guiding question:** HOW does the occupation end? What conditions must precede withdrawal?

## Key Terms

- **People's war (Maoist):** Class-based insurgency competing for the allegiance of an uncommitted population through ideology, nationalism, and promises of better governance. Vietnam paradigm. Hearts-and-minds logic applies.
- **Communal civil war:** Conflict between ethnic or sectarian groups driven by mutual existential fear, not ideology. No uncommitted population; government is a factional instrument. Iraq paradigm.
- **Vietnamization:** Nixon's 1969 policy of transferring ground combat to South Vietnamese forces on an "orderly scheduled timetable" while withdrawing U.S. troops. Model for Iraqization.
- **Iraqization:** Bush administration's policy of building Iraqi national military and police so coalition forces can "stand down." Biddle's target — argues it is inappropriate for a communal war.
- **Security dilemma (communal):** Dynamic where each communal group fears mass violence at rivals' hands if it cedes any advantage, making compromise seem worse than ongoing low-intensity war.
- **Hearts and minds:** COIN strategy of winning popular loyalty through good governance and economic development. Biddle argues it is logically inapplicable when the conflict is not ideological.
- **Oil-spot strategy:** Krepinevich's proposed COIN alternative — concentrate on securing civilians in small areas and expand outward. Biddle argues this faces the same fatal flaw: it requires Iraqi forces who will be either integrated-and-weak or segregated-and-divisive.
- **Constitutional power-sharing compromise:** Biddle's preferred solution — an ethnically inclusive constitutional deal with ironclad protections for all factions, negotiated *before* military devolution.
- **Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA):** U.S. occupation authority in Iraq, May 2003–June 2004, headed by L. Paul Bremer. Biddle criticizes its passive approach to interethnic negotiations.
- **Communal security problem:** The underlying driver of communal civil war — groups need credible guarantees against mass violence before they can afford to compromise politically.
