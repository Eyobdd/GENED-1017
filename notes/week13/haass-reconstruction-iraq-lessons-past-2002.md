# Haass, "Reconstruction in Iraq: Lessons of the Past" (2002)
**Author:** Richard N. Haass (Director, Policy Planning Staff, S/P) | **Week:** 13 | **Type:** PS
**Case Study:** Iraq (draws comparisons across Philippines, Germany, Japan, Korea, Somalia, Kosovo, Bosnia, Haiti, Afghanistan, and others)
**Pages:** State Dept. Information Memorandum, pp. 1–15 (memo pagination); declassified 2006/2012

---

## Summary

A September 2002 SECRET memorandum from State Department Policy Planning Director Richard Haass to Secretary Powell, written *before* the Iraq War began. Haass argues the US must prepare to "win the ensuing peace" and draws on 20+ historical reconstruction cases to identify what Iraq will require. The memo is remarkable as evidence that serious planning *did* occur — and that its conclusions were largely ignored.

Haass frames reconstruction around four questions: (1) What is inherited? Iraq falls in the *middle* of the development spectrum — it has oil wealth, an educated middle class, and an existing bureaucracy, but lacks democratic experience, is riven by ethnic divisions, and carries a decade of sanctions damage. (2) How ambitious should we be? The more we own the ouster, the more we own the reconstruction; full-scale nation building comparable to South Korea, Greece, or Japan will be required. (3) What levers work? Five core elements: physical security (first and foremost), humanitarian relief, economic reconstruction, legitimate governance, and regional stabilization. (4) How to organize the international effort? Haass rejects the unilateral "MacArthur model" as too costly and imperially optic; recommends a "national coalition" with firm U.S. leadership under UN auspices.

The seven specific lessons for Iraq predict most of the post-2003 failures: prevent a security vacuum, avoid full de-Baathification, promote Iraqi self-government rather than imposing a ruler, and contain neighbor meddling. Its prescience makes it a damning indictment of the CPA's subsequent choices.

**Relates to:** HOW does occupation end? / WHAT did Americans plan to do? It also shows the *gap* between planning and implementation — a key theme when read alongside Bremer, CPA Orders 1 & 2.

## Key Terms

- **"Nation building lite"**: Haass's term for minimal-ambition post-conflict intervention — restoring basic order and humanitarian relief without political transformation. Applies to early Caribbean interventions (Cuba, Dominican Republic, Haiti 1994). Contrasted with full-scale nation building.
- **"MacArthur model"**: Unilateral occupation with a single supreme authority (as MacArthur exercised in Japan). Haass explicitly warns against applying this to Iraq — it worked in Japan because the US bore all costs alone; in Iraq it would read as imperial grab.
- **"National coalition" approach**: Mixed international actors (countries, IOs, NGOs) under a single UN mandate and de facto U.S. leadership. Haass's recommendation for Iraq, modeled on Kosovo and Afghanistan.
- **Hardware vs. software (development)**: Haass's distinction between a country's physical/institutional "hardware" (state apparatus, infrastructure, bureaucracy) and its social/cultural "software" (democratic familiarity, national identity, human capital). Iraq has stronger hardware than Somalia but weaker software than Germany.
- **Security vacuum**: The period immediately after regime collapse when indigenous armed forces, militias, and warlords fill the power void. Haass identifies this as the most dangerous moment — prevention is "first and foremost."
- **Transitional justice**: Mechanisms (tribunals, truth-and-reconciliation commissions) to ensure accountability for past abuses. Haass says the *mechanism* should be chosen by the successor government, not imposed by the US.
- **"Spoilers"**: Internal actors (warlords, crime syndicates, militias) who benefit from chaos and actively undermine reconstruction. Distinguished from "meddlers" (external neighbors like Iran).
- **Oil-for-food program**: UN mechanism allowing Iraq to sell oil for humanitarian goods under sanctions. Haass recommends transforming it post-war to fund reconstruction while protecting creditor claims (Russia, France, Germany, Japan).
- **Full de-Baathification**: Haass explicitly warns against it — argues only *senior* Baath leadership should be excluded, as broader exclusion destroys the administrative capacity needed to run the state. (CPA Order 1 did the opposite.)

## Flashcards

FRONT: What are Haass's five core elements of successful reconstruction?
BACK: (a) Physical security, (b) humanitarian relief and repatriation, (c) physical and economic reconstruction, (d) establishment of legitimate and effective governance, (e) stabilization of the regional environment. | Haass 2002 | All five must succeed together; security is the prerequisite.

FRONT: What is the "MacArthur model" and why does Haass reject it for Iraq?
BACK: The Japan model — a single supreme American proconsul with near-unchecked authority running reconstruction unilaterally. Haass rejects it for Iraq because it would increase U.S. costs, decrease multilateral burden-sharing, and be perceived as an imperial oil grab. | Haass 2002 | Contrast: the US ultimately *did* use this model through the CPA.

FRONT: What does Haass say about de-Baathification?
BACK: Preclude only a *small number* — upper Baath leadership, senior military, security services, and war criminals. The US will likely need "many individuals associated in some way with the old regime to maintain order and establish a new viable state." | Haass 2002 | Directly contradicts CPA Order 1 (2003), which removed all Baath Party members from government jobs.

FRONT: Where does Haass place Iraq on the "development spectrum" for reconstruction?
BACK: Middle of the range. Iraq has oil wealth, a middle class, state apparatus, and secular educated professionals (hardware + some software), but lacks democratic experience, has ethnic/sectarian divisions, and endured decade-long sanctions. Most comparable to South Korea, Greece, Italy, Japan. | Haass 2002 | This placement implies full-scale, long-term nation building is required.

FRONT: What is a "security vacuum" and why is it so dangerous?
BACK: The power void immediately after regime collapse. Warlords and organized crime fill it rapidly; once entrenched they become "spoilers" that are very hard to dislodge. Haass cites Bosnia (organized crime burrowed into economy) and Somalia (permanent failure to restore order) as cautionary cases. | Haass 2002 | Prevention requires rapid deployment of external forces, demobilization of Iraqi combatants, and rapid rebuilding of internal security structures.

FRONT: What does Haass identify as Iraq's key structural liability compared to post-WWII Japan?
BACK: Iraq lacks a recognized national unifying figure equivalent to Emperor Hirohito. Japan's reconstruction benefited from Hirohito serving as a unifying symbol that bridged occupation and indigenous authority. Iraq's ethnic/tribal fragmentation (Sunni, Shia, Kurd) makes this absence especially destabilizing. | Haass 2002 | Explains why governance was the hardest element of Iraq reconstruction.
