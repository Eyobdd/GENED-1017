# Bremer, *My Year in Iraq* (pp. 19–21, 39–49)
**Author:** L. Paul Bremer III | **Week:** 13 | **Type:** PS (Memoir)
**Case Study:** Iraq
**Pages:** 19–21, 39–49

## Summary

L. Paul Bremer III arrived in Baghdad in May 2003 as the Bush administration's Presidential Envoy and head of the Coalition Provisional Authority (CPA), replacing Jay Garner's ORHA. These pages cover his first days in Iraq and the drafting and signing of CPA Order No. 1 on May 16, 2003.

Pages 19–21 show Bremer confronting immediate post-invasion chaos: widespread looting of army barracks, state enterprises, and oil archives; a collapsed police force (4,000 poorly-armed officers, most having fled); rampant violent crime and sexual assault; and a shattered economy. His first staff meeting frames the occupation's agenda — de-Baathification, an interim administration by mid-June, no rushed elections (no census, no parties, no electoral laws), and economic reform toward a private sector. Crucially, he invokes **Germany and Japan** as the model: "Democracies don't work unless the political structure rests on a solid civil society." Yet even in the first hours, Bremer acknowledges the fundamental tension: "Yes, we're an 'occupying power.' No getting around that. But we must never forget that this country belongs to the Iraqis."

Pages 39–49 document the **de-Baathification order** from conception to signing. Bremer received the draft from Under Secretary Douglas Feith at the Pentagon, with Rumsfeld's mandate to "actively oppose Saddam Hussein's old enforcers." The order targeted the top four tiers of Baath Party membership (~20,000 people out of 2 million, overwhelmingly Sunni Arabs), banning them from public employment — explicitly modeled on de-Nazification in Germany and MacArthur's decrees in occupied Japan. Bremer then met the Iraqi Leadership Council (C-7), a body of exile leaders (Chalabi, Allawi, Barzani, Talabani, Jaafari, SCIRI) who welcomed de-Baathification but demanded rapid power transfer. Bremer pushed back: the ILC was unrepresentative (no Turkmen, no Christians, no women, mostly exiles who had not stayed and suffered). He insisted the path to representative government would be "incremental" — a word that masked the CPA's determination to control the political process. The meeting ended with the exiles taking over twenty minutes to choose a spokesman, which Bremer reads as a "harbinger of difficulty."

**So what:** This text is invaluable as an occupier's self-justification. Bremer positions himself as a reformer drawing on the right lessons of history (Germany, Japan), yet the same pages reveal the contradictions: the occupier decides what "representative" means, exiles are sidelined while also being the only available interlocutors, and a sweeping social-engineering decree (de-Baathification) is issued within days of arrival. The memoir form — confident, first-person, retrospective — papers over improvisation and bureaucratic infighting (e.g., the parallel management structure Bremer admits he needed to build around Garner). Relates most directly to: **WHAT did Americans do?** and **HOW did occupation end?** (the latter through the incremental sovereignty transfer logic established here).

## Key Terms

- **CPA (Coalition Provisional Authority):** The US-led governing body that replaced ORHA after the fall of Baghdad; Bremer served as its administrator. Effectively the occupation government of Iraq, 2003–2004.
- **De-Baathification:** CPA Order No. 1 (May 16, 2003), banning the top four tiers of Baath Party membership (~20,000 Sunnis) from public employment. Modeled on de-Nazification. Critics argue it destroyed state capacity and drove Sunnis into the insurgency.
- **ORHA (Office of Reconstruction and Humanitarian Assistance):** Jay Garner's predecessor body to the CPA; dismissed as inadequate by Washington and replaced by Bremer's more overtly political apparatus.
- **Iraqi Leadership Council (ILC / C-7):** Small body of exile political leaders — Chalabi, Allawi, Barzani, Talabani, Jaafari, Chaderchi, SCIRI reps — that the US had been dealing with. Bremer found them unrepresentative and resisted their demand for quick power transfer.
- **Interim Iraqi Administration:** Bremer's proposed body to govern Iraq while a constitution and elections were organized; the CPA intended it to be broadly representative, not dominated by exiles.
- **Baath Party:** Arab nationalist-socialist party that ruled Iraq under Saddam Hussein; had ~2 million nominal members (many coerced). Its removal from public life was the central act of early CPA governance.
- **Fedayeen Saddam:** Paramilitary irregular forces loyal to Saddam; identified in Rumsfeld's memo as targets alongside the Baath Party.
- **Peshmerga:** Kurdish militias (KDP and PUK) that fought alongside US Coalition forces; their leaders (Barzani, Talabani) sat on the ILC.
- **Exiles vs. Internals:** Tension between Iraqis who had spent the Saddam years abroad (the ILC leadership) versus those who stayed and suffered inside Iraq. Bremer explicitly worried that exile-dominated governance would lack legitimacy — the same dynamic Chang identifies in Korea (1945).
