# Reform of the Japanese Governmental System (SWNCC 228)
**Author:** State-War-Navy Coordinating Committee (SWNCC)
**Week:** 6
**Type:** Primary Source
**Case Study:** Japan
**Lecture Context:** Writing and Rewriting Constitutions
**Date processed:** 2026-03-20

## Summary (~200 words)

SWNCC 228 (drafted November 1945, finalized January 1946) is the secret U.S. policy document that defined what constitutional reforms the occupation would demand of Japan. It was produced by the State-War-Navy Coordinating Subcommittee for the Far East and forwarded as guidance to the American representative on the Far Eastern Commission. Its central tension is explicit from the start: the U.S. had publicly committed (via the Potsdam Declaration and the August 11 Allied note) that Japan's "ultimate form of government" would be determined by "the freely expressed will of the Japanese people" — yet SWNCC 228 lists in exhaustive detail precisely what that government must look like.

The document diagnoses six structural defects of the Meiji Constitution: absence of government accountability to the people, military independence from civilian control, the excessive power of the House of Peers and Privy Council, and inadequate civil rights protections. It concludes with seven general objectives the Supreme Commander should "indicate" to the Japanese — from popular sovereignty and legislative budget control to civilian cabinet members and civil rights guarantees. On the Emperor, it takes a carefully hedged position: the retention of the institution "in its present form is not consistent" with democratic objectives, but the Japanese people should decide for themselves. Crucially, paragraph 5 states that direct imposition should be used "only as a last resort."

## Key Quotes

> "The drafting and adoption of constitutional amendments or of a constitution in a manner which will express the free will of the Japanese people." (Conclusion 4a.7)

The final of seven general objectives — foregrounding Japanese agency, though the preceding six objectives substantially predetermine the content.

> "Though the ultimate form of government in Japan is to be established by the freely expressed will of the Japanese people, the retention of the Emperor institution in its present form is not considered consistent with the foregoing general objectives." (Conclusion 4b)

The document's most consequential judgment: it does not abolish the Emperor but declares the current form incompatible with democracy, leaving SCAP to define the middle path.

> "Only as a last resort should the Supreme Commander order the Japanese Government to effect the above listed reforms, as the knowledge that they had been imposed by the Allies would materially reduce the possibility of their acceptance and support by the Japanese people for the future." (Conclusion 5)

The most quoted line. Its logic — imposed reforms are less durable than apparently indigenous ones — is shared exactly by Hahn's account of U.S. Germany policy. The difference is what happened in practice: in Japan, SCAP drafted the constitution directly within weeks.

> "The ultimate form of government of Japan shall, in accordance with the Potsdam Declaration, be established by the freely expressed will of the Japanese people." (Appendix A, citing Allied note of August 11)

The commitment the document is simultaneously honoring and circumventing.

> "It is not the responsibility of the Allied Powers to impose upon Japan any form of government not supported by the freely expressed will of the people." (Appendix A, citing SWNCC 209/D)

U.S. initial post-defeat policy on Japan — placed in the factual record to show the doctrinal context, but immediately qualified by the discussion that follows.

> "The existing Japanese governmental system... has shown itself unsuited to the development of peaceful practices and policies primarily by reason of the defects described in the following paragraphs." (Appendix B, para. 2)

The premise for all that follows: the Meiji Constitution is not a neutral instrument but a structural cause of militarism.

> "The present Japanese Constitution was drawn up with the dual purpose of, on the one hand, stilling popular clamor for representative institutions, and on the other, of fortifying and perpetuating the centralized and autocratic governmental structure which its framers, the Meiji leaders, believed necessary for Japan's continued existence." (Appendix B, para. 3b)

Sharp historical diagnosis of the Meiji Constitution's origins — the document frames reform as correcting a deliberate anti-democratic design, not imposing foreign values.

> "To have lasting value and hence to be most effective, the constitutional and administrative reforms necessary to correct the defects in Japan's governmental system should be initiated and carried into effect by the Japanese Government out of a desire to eliminate elements of the national structure which have brought Japan to its present pass." (Appendix B, para. 7)

The preferred method: Japanese-initiated reform. This is the theoretical ideal that SCAP's direct drafting in February 1946 abandoned.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **SWNCC (State-War-Navy Coordinating Committee):** The interagency body coordinating U.S. occupation policy. SWNCC 228 is its Japan constitutional reform document; compare SWNCC 150/4 (W5PS, initial post-defeat policy) and SWNCC 176 (W5PS, Korea directive).
- **Emperor institution:** SWNCC 228 neither abolishes nor endorses the Emperor but insists it must be subordinated to civilian and parliamentary control. This hedged position reflects genuine U.S. uncertainty about whether retaining the Emperor would stabilize or destabilize reform.
- **Potsdam Declaration (1945):** The basis of Allied authority over Japan; established the withdrawal condition (establishment of a "peacefully inclined and responsible government"), which SWNCC 228 uses to justify demanding reforms.
- **Dual government:** The document's term for Japan's structural defect — the military's ability to act independently of the Cabinet and Diet by claiming direct imperial authority. Eliminating this is a central objective.
- **Privy Council:** The unelected imperial advisory body that functioned as a "third chamber" with veto power over cabinets; identified in SWNCC 228 as a major barrier to parliamentary government.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Hahn (W6RR):** SWNCC 228's "only as a last resort" principle is the direct parallel to Hahn's analysis of U.S. Germany policy, which also sought managed self-determination over direct imposition. The contrast between the stated ideal and actual SCAP practice (direct drafting by February 1946) is the central Japan-Germany comparison for the course.
- **Connects to Dower, Chs. 12–13 (W6):** Dower shows that when the Japanese government's own constitutional draft proved inadequate in February 1946, SCAP abandoned SWNCC 228's preferred approach and drafted the constitution directly — the "last resort" became the first resort. SWNCC 228 is the document SCAP violated.
- **Connects to SWNCC 150/4 (W5PS):** The initial post-defeat policy for Japan; SWNCC 228 elaborates specifically on constitutional reform within that broader framework. The same logic of occupation withdrawal conditioned on reform appears in both.
- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4PS):** The Atlantic Charter's self-determination language is the international norm SWNCC 228 invokes in its factual appendix. The document performs compliance with self-determination while establishing the conditions under which it is exercised — a pattern Duara (W4RR) identifies as characteristic of postwar liberal imperialism.
- **Connects to Fradera (W1RR):** The document's claim that the Meiji Constitution was itself an anti-democratic imposition (designed to "still popular clamor") implies that the real Japanese tradition is being liberated, not overwritten. This mirrors the exceptionality arguments Fradera identifies in imperial transitions — the colonized/occupied people need guidance to realize their own best interests.
