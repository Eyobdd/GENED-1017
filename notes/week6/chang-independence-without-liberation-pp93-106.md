## Summary (~500 words)

Pages 93–106 bring Chang's argument to its conclusion, covering three interlocking phases: the Autumn 1946 uprisings and the institutionalization of the police state; the U.S. "exit strategy" of UN-supervised elections in 1948; and the constitution-drafting crisis that ended with Syngman Rhee capturing an imperial presidency. Chang's overall argument is that the United States converted decolonization into empire-maintenance by deploying democratic proceduralism — elections, constitutions, UN legitimation — as a substitute for genuine liberation.

**Continuation from pp. 77–93:** The earlier pages (Week 5) established the opening conditions: Korea's "Asian Spring" of 1945, the diversity of Korean aspirations for self-determination (workers seizing factories, peasants seizing land, the Korean People's Republic attempting to channel revolutionary energy), and the U.S. occupation's early decisions to dissolve the KPR, revive the colonial police (KNP), back landlord-allied rightists (KDP), suppress labor organizing, and fail to implement land reform. By p. 93 the occupation has already generated a legitimacy crisis: Korean confidence in the U.S. has collapsed, and wages have fallen catastrophically against inflation.

**Autumn Strikes and the Police State (pp. 90–93):** The September 1946 general strike — initiated when the railway workers union's demands were ignored — drew over 300,000 workers. Hodge declared the strikes illegal, deployed American troops alongside the KNP, and crushed them violently. Sympathy uprisings followed, culminating in the October Taegu uprising where protesters seized the police station after police killed a demonstrator. Chang's argument here is that the occupation's Manichean anticommunism was self-defeating: legal adviser Roger Baldwin found that very few strikers were committed communists, but "the military officials are making more of them daily by their policies of suppression." The crackdown institutionalized a police state — the exact colonial system the U.S. had claimed to supersede.

**Scrambling for a Way Out (pp. 93–94):** By 1947 the occupation had no good options. Liberal and conservative advisers converged on an "exit strategy": refer the Korea question to the United Nations, hold elections in the southern zone, and establish a separate anticommunist client state under Syngman Rhee. Chang shows that U.S. officials understood this would leave Korea permanently divided and would install an authoritarian, but accepted it under "Cold War logic of self-determination" — the idea that half a Korea free from communism was still self-determination. Elections gave the U.S. a "semblance of sovereignty" to bestow while withdrawing.

**Staging Democracy (pp. 94–101):** UNTCOK (the UN Temporary Commission on Korea) arrived to monitor the May 1948 elections and immediately found the South controlled by a right-wing police state. Leftists were in prison or exile; the 1946 interim elections had been rigged. UNTCOK representatives from Australia, Canada, and India drafted a resolution to withdraw, but were overruled. Hodge made cosmetic reforms — a Bill of Rights, pardons for some political prisoners — under UNTCOK pressure. Meanwhile the Jeju April 3 uprising against the elections (and the Pyongyang Unity Conference boycott, backed by right-wing nationalists Kim Ku and Kim Kyusik) demonstrated that Korean resistance came from across the political spectrum. The May 10 election proceeded with 93% turnout nationally but was heavily boycotted and disrupted in Jeju. UNTCOK validated the results despite internal dissent.

**Strongman Rising and the Constitution (pp. 102–106):** The constitutional process took less than forty days — a fraction of the time spent in Japan or Germany. U.S. advisers had informally agreed with Korean drafters on a parliamentary system to check Rhee's power. Rhee attended the drafting committee on June 15 and demanded a strong presidency; when rebuffed, he threatened to withdraw entirely, which would have thrown UN recognition into doubt. KDP leaders capitulated. The resulting constitution contained what constitutional adviser Pergler described as a "crisis clause" (Article 57) "rather reminiscent of the unfortunate Art. 48 of the German Weimar Constitution" — conferring extraordinary presidential powers in emergencies — and Article 87 allowing government takeover of enterprises "having public character." Arthur Bunce concluded the draft "provided for a presidential dictatorship rooted in economic oligarchy." Chang's conclusion: the U.S. sponsored a sham election to exit gracefully, and in so doing created the conditions for authoritarian rule. "An empire without colonies was built upon the foundation of decolonization without liberation."

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **UNTCOK (UN Temporary Commission on Korea):** The eight-nation UN body created in November 1947 to monitor peninsula-wide elections. Its mandate was to "facilitate and expedite" elections; in practice, the U.S. pressured compliant members and ignored dissenting ones (Australia, Canada, India). UNTCOK's divided but ultimately affirmative validation of the May 1948 elections gave the U.S. its crucial international legitimation for withdrawal.
- **Democratic proceduralism:** Chang's term for U.S. self-determination policy in practice — conceiving self-determination as the holding of elections and the ratification of constitutions, regardless of whether conditions for free participation existed. Follows Bradley Simpson's formulation that Americans conceived of self-determination "in liberal, procedural terms." The form of democracy (ballot, constitution) was used as a substitute for its substance.
- **Semblance of sovereignty:** Term from Alexander Aleinikoff used by Chang to describe what the U.S. granted Korea through the 1948 elections — formal legal independence without genuine control over one's political, economic, or military future. South Korea received sovereignty, but within America's sphere of influence and with an autocrat the U.S. could not actually control.
- **"Crisis clause" (Article 57):** The emergency powers provision of the 1948 South Korean constitution, which granted the president extraordinary authority during civil war, foreign threats, or economic crises. U.S. adviser Pergler compared it to Weimar's Article 48 (which enabled Hitler's rise) and Japan's Article 8 (imperial emergency ordinances). It became a foundation of Rhee's authoritarian rule.
- **Exit strategy / decolonization management:** Chang's framing of U.S. elections policy. "Exit strategy" is his term for the decision to sponsor elections not because conditions were favorable for democracy but because it allowed the U.S. to withdraw forces while retaining influence. "Decolonization management" is his framing for the broader pattern: the U.S. orchestrated decolonization to produce client states rather than genuinely self-governing ones.
- **"Great crescent" (Dean Acheson):** The strategic concept that framed Korea as an economic hinterland and defensive buffer for a rehabilitated Japan within a U.S.-Pacific security architecture. Chang uses it to show that Korean "independence" was always subordinate to U.S. regional imperial planning, not a goal in itself.
- **Jeju April 3 Uprising (4.3 사건):** The armed resistance on Jeju Island beginning April 3, 1948, organized by the South Korean Labor Worker's Party against the separate elections. Rebels framed their actions as a "save-the-nation-movement" against an imperialist plot to divide Korea. The occupation sent 1,700 police and three constabulary battalions; elections remained disrupted for a full year. Chang uses Jeju to show Korean resistance was grounded in sovereignty claims, not just communist ideology.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Hahn (W6RR) and Dower on Germany and Japan:** Chang explicitly notes that the Korean constitutional process took less than forty days — "a fraction of the time it took in occupied Japan and Germany." Hahn shows the U.S. in Germany managing a multi-year process through negotiation and managed self-determination, careful to preserve the appearance of German authorship. Dower shows SCAP directly drafting Japan's constitution in nine days. Korea was different from both: the U.S. largely disengaged from drafting until Rhee hijacked the process, and the result was a constitution that alarmed even U.S. advisers. The three cases together reveal that how much the occupier controls the constitutional text does not determine how democratic the outcome is — Korean "self-authorship" produced an authoritarian document, German "managed self-determination" produced the Basic Law.

- **Fradera, "Reading Imperial Transitions" (W1RR):** Fradera argues that imperial transitions consistently involve the metropole claiming to act for subject populations while designing outcomes that serve imperial interests. Chang's analysis of "decolonization management" is precisely this pattern in a postwar register: the U.S. claims to bring Korea to self-determination while engineering an outcome (a client anticommunist state, rapid withdrawal, UN validation) that serves U.S. strategic interests. The "semblance of sovereignty" Chang borrows from Aleinikoff is structurally analogous to Fradera's concept of differential incorporation — different formal statuses (colony, protectorate, client state) all serving the same imperial function.

- **Atlantic Charter (W4) on self-determination:** The Atlantic Charter committed the U.S. to self-determination as "a basic principle of international order," and Chang's earlier pages (pp. 79-80) note how U.S. planners rushed to carve out exceptions for European empires. By pp. 93–106, we see the full deformation of that promise in Korea: the U.S. invoked self-determination language to legitimate elections it knew were unfree, pressured UNTCOK members to validate tainted results, and used the outcome to secure a geopolitical exit. The "Cold War logic of self-determination" Chang identifies — where half-a-Korea saved from communism counts as self-determination — shows how Cold War ideology distorted the Atlantic Charter's already-compromised promise.

- **Mabini, "Introductory Manifesto" (W3RR):** Mabini, writing from inside the Philippine Revolution against U.S. occupation in 1898–1902, anticipated every element of Chang's Korean case: occupying forces dissolving indigenous governing structures (KPR/People's Committees = Mabini's revolutionary government), backing collaborationist elites over genuine independence movements, and using elections and constitutional forms to legitimize continued dominance. Chang's Koreans who argued "All our activities are suspected as Communist. The army stops our strikes... You can't criticize a thing the army does without going to jail" (Kim Tong Suk, p. 92) echo Mabini's characterization of U.S. policy as suppressing liberation under the cover of tutelage.

- **SWNCC 176 (W5PS) — Korea Directive:** The U.S. directive governing the occupation of Korea (SWNCC 176) instructed occupation forces to treat Korea as a liberated nation, foster democratic institutions, and avoid restoring colonial structures. Chang's pp. 93–106 show that every one of these instructions was violated: the colonial police were revived, democratic organizing was suppressed, and the constitution was rushed through in the service of an exit timetable rather than Korean self-determination. SWNCC 176 thus functions as a document of self-indictment when read alongside Chang — the gap between stated policy and actual practice is the core of Chang's argument.

## Practice Questions

### 3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice)

**Set 1: Jeju Uprising / Atlantic Charter / Self-Determination**
The Jeju rebels described the U.S.-sponsored election as "an imperialist plot to subvert the will of the Korean people" and framed their resistance as a "save-the-nation-movement." This language mirrors the Atlantic Charter's promise of self-determination for "all peoples." The conjunction reveals Chang's central irony: the United States, the self-proclaimed champion of self-determination, was violently suppressing a movement that used the U.S.'s own international vocabulary to resist the U.S.'s own occupation policies.

**Set 2: Article 57 / Weimar Article 48 / Constitutional Drafting Speed**
U.S. adviser Pergler's alarm about Article 57 of the Korean constitution — the crisis clause he compared to Weimar's Article 48 — is all the more striking given that the entire Korean constitutional process took less than forty days. In Germany, the U.S. spent years ensuring all-party consensus specifically because Weimar had failed for lack of it (Hahn). In Korea, the exit timetable precluded any such deliberation, and the resulting crisis clause created the exact authoritarian instrument the U.S. feared in Weimar. Speed, in the service of U.S. withdrawal, produced the danger the U.S. had devoted enormous effort to preventing in Germany.

**Set 3: Democratic Proceduralism / Semblance of Sovereignty / Decolonization Without Liberation**
Chang's argument is that the three concepts are synonymous: "democratic proceduralism" (elections as the form of self-determination) produces a "semblance of sovereignty" (formal independence without genuine self-governance), which is "decolonization without liberation" (the nominal end of colonial status without the substantive end of domination). The Korean case makes visible a mechanism that Chang argues operated across all U.S.-occupied former Japanese territories — the Philippines, Okinawa, the Marshall Islands — wherever the U.S. converted colonial administration into client-state management.
