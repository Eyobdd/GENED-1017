# Forced to be Free: The Artificial Revolution in Germany and Japan — Introduction
**Author:** John D. Montgomery | **Week:** 10 | **Type:** PS (Book Chapter)
**Case Study:** Germany / Japan
**Pages:** pp. 1–9 (Introduction)

## Summary

Montgomery's Introduction poses a sharp analytical question: what exactly did the Allied occupations of Germany and Japan *do*, and does it constitute a new form of political action in world history? His answer is the concept of **"artificial revolution"** — a programmed, externally imposed transformation of political leadership and institutions on behalf of democracy, distinguished from both "natural" revolution (organic, popular upheaval) and "permanent" revolution (Marxist agitation). This is the first time, Montgomery argues, that a great power systematically attempted to export democracy not through colonialism, not through propaganda, but through a structured overhaul of a defeated nation's elite, legal order, and institutional fabric.

The program had three interlocking elements: (1) **elimination of despotic elites** through denazification and the Japanese political purge; (2) **encouragement of new democratic leadership**; and (3) **constitutional, legal, and institutional guarantees** of the new order. Yet Montgomery's central judgment is that "ambition outran performance." The Cold War — specifically Stalin's ambitions — cut the artificial revolution short. Decartelization and zaibatsu dissolution were abandoned to resume war manufacturing; demilitarization reversed into rearmament; and even the MacArthur Constitution was rushed through to forestall Communist participation in its design. What remained was largely negative: the purge of old leaders rather than the positive installation of new democratic structures.

This creates a profound tension that runs through the book: the Hague Convention (Art. 43, 1907) forbids occupiers from tampering with the political machinery of defeated nations — yet unconditional surrender, announced against the backdrop of Crimea and Potsdam's political objectives, arguably compelled Germany and Japan to forfeit sovereign rights under international law. "It is not yet clear," Montgomery writes, "that defeat in war may legally become the means of forcing a people to become free." This tension between self-determination and imposed democracy is precisely the contradiction Dower identifies as a "revolution from above," and it resonates backward to the Philippines (justifying American colonial governance as civilizing mission) and forward to Cold War nation-building.

Montgomery's study is based on field research: Japan in 1952 (immediately before/after occupation's end) and West Germany in 1953–54. He stresses this is not a verdict on whether the occupations succeeded — it is too early to know if land reform, new constitutions, and labor movements will hold. His narrower claim is about leadership change as a political mechanism: remove the old elite, and what fills the vacuum?

**Connects to:** WHAT did they do? / HOW did it end? / WHY occupy? (the ideological anti-totalitarian motive)

## Key Terms

- **Artificial revolution**: Montgomery's coinage — externally induced, top-down political transformation on behalf of democracy. "Artificial" contrasts with "natural" (organic popular revolution) and "permanent" (Marxist continuous revolution). It is programmed, peaceful, and reversible — which is both its defining feature and its weakness.
- **Denazification**: The Allied program in Germany to remove Nazi party members from positions of public life, business, and government. Montgomery treats it as the paradigmatic example of artificial revolution's *negative* phase — removing old elites without guaranteeing new democratic ones.
- **Political purge (Japan)**: The Japanese equivalent of denazification — removal of militarist leaders from public office. Like denazification, it was the main surviving artifact of artificial revolution after Cold War pressures ended positive reform programs.
- **Decartelization / zaibatsu dissolution**: The positive economic reform programs in Germany and Japan respectively — breaking up industrial monopolies. Abandoned due to Cold War need for rapid economic recovery and war production.
- **Hague Convention (Art. 43, 1907)**: International law governing military occupation — required occupiers to respect existing laws of defeated nations. The Allied occupations violated this in principle; Montgomery argues unconditional surrender created a legal exception.
- **Cold War reversal ("reverse course")**: The shift away from reformist occupation policy beginning ~1947–48 as anti-Communist priorities overtook democratization goals. Montgomery frames this as Stalin's ambitions undoing what Hitler's had created.
- **Unconditional surrender**: The surrender terms accepted by Germany and Japan, which (unlike conditional surrenders) arguably stripped them of protections under international law, enabling the scope of Allied reforms.
