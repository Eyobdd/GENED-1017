## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 10 is the course's retrospective pivot. Where Weeks 5–7 examined occupation as a process being lived through — directives issued, constitutions drafted, land redistributed under active SCAP or Allied authority — Weeks 9–10 assess what occupation *became* once the formal period ended. Montgomery's Introduction to *Forced to be Free* (written in the early 1960s, based on 1952–54 field research) provides the conceptual architecture for this retrospective: he names the postwar occupations the first "artificial revolution" in world history and immediately asks what that revolution actually achieved. Ladejinsky's *Foreign Affairs* essay (1959), written a decade after Japan's land reform was completed, answers that question for one specific domain with unusual precision.

The two readings share a temporal vantage point — they are both looking backward from the mid-to-late occupation legacy period — but they reach structurally different conclusions, and understanding why is the week's central analytical task.

Montgomery's verdict is cautiously pessimistic. The occupation's three-part program — eliminate despotic elites, encourage new democratic leadership, install institutional guarantees — was cut short by Cold War pressure. Stalin's ambitions "caused them to alter its course" just as Hitler's had created its opening. What survived was primarily the negative phase: denazification in Germany, the political purge in Japan. Decartelization and zaibatsu dissolution were abandoned; demilitarization reversed into rearmament; even the MacArthur Constitution was rushed through to prevent Communist participation in its design. "Ambition outran performance." The artificial revolution, in Montgomery's account, was durable in destruction (removing old elites) but incomplete in construction (building new democratic ones).

Ladejinsky's verdict is unambiguously triumphant — but his triumph is precisely the exception Montgomery cannot account for. Japan's land reform was the "conspicuous exception" to the reverse course: while "virtually every other reform measure has been revised or legislated out of existence," 5.8 million acres remained redistributed, 3 million farmers remained owners, and the rural Communist movement remained destroyed. For Ladejinsky, the reform worked because private land ownership created the irreversible stake in the existing order that Montgomery's institutional guarantees alone could not. A farmer who owns his land is structurally resistant to Communist collectivization — not because of ideology but because of property interest. The "artificial revolution," in the one domain where it was fully executed at the material level, produced precisely the political stability Montgomery argued the revolution aimed at.

The week's deepest tension is about what makes occupation reform *durable*. Montgomery, looking at the totality of the program, argues that durability came from elite replacement (who held power changed) and institutional design (what rules governed power). Ladejinsky, looking at land reform specifically, argues that durability came from material redistribution (who owned what). The first is subject to Cold War reversal — old elites could be rehabilitated, new ones co-opted, institutions redesigned when convenient. The second is resistant to it: you cannot re-collectivize 3 million Japanese farmers without the political violence that would destroy the stability the whole exercise sought to create. Land reform survived the reverse course precisely because reversing it would have been more destabilizing than leaving it in place. Montgomery's framework describes why most occupation reforms failed; Ladejinsky's retrospective shows what a reform had to do at the level of property and livelihood to become genuinely irreversible.

Both readings also complicate the "American-made" narrative in ways that the course has consistently demanded. Montgomery frames his core inquiry as whether occupied peoples can be "forced to be free" — acknowledging that the democratic idea "cannot consistently be imposed upon a people." Ladejinsky is even more direct: "Contrary to a widespread view, the land reform program was not American-made, packaged and delivered in Japan. The idea was as indigenous as the conditions which impelled it." He credits Hiroo Wada — Japan's Socialist Minister of Agriculture — as "the real architect of the revolution," with MacArthur providing the political cover and the authority to overcome Japanese government reluctance. The "strange partnership of a left-wing Socialist and an American five-star General, and in the order cited" is Ladejinsky's summary of what the occupation really was when it worked: not American imposition but American enabling of indigenous reform that local politics had already conceived and repeatedly blocked.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 7 (Dower Ch. 17, Kim, Gillingham, Ladejinsky "From a Landlord to a Land Reformer"):** Week 10's Ladejinsky is a retrospective from 1959 on the same program he was advocating from inside SCAP in the Week 7 primary source (pp. 148–154). The Week 7 Ladejinsky was a practitioner making the case in real time; the Week 10 Ladejinsky is the same practitioner vindicated by a decade of evidence. Reading them together shows what he got right (land reform as permanently reversible achievement, rural Communist collapse) and what he was still uncertain about (whether Japan's agrarian fragmentation and reliance on off-farm income would eventually threaten the reform's democratic effects). Montgomery's *Forced to be Free* provides the Week 10 conceptual frame within which Dower's "reverse course" (Ch. 17) can be re-analyzed: what Dower described empirically (zaibatsu reconstitution, Dodge Line, Korean War special procurements) Montgomery theorizes as the structural consequence of Cold War pressure cutting artificial revolution short. Where Dower documented the reverse course as Japanese history, Montgomery frames it as a near-universal pattern of 20th-century occupation.

**Week 5 (Dower Ch. 6, SWNCC 150, Chang, JCS 1067):** Montgomery's three-part program of artificial revolution maps directly onto Dower's "revolution from above" concept (Dower Ch. 6, p. 211). Both identify the same structural paradox: that occupation's democratic project was launched by fiat from an authority whose legitimacy derived from military conquest rather than popular mandate. SWNCC 150's directive to "democratize" Japan and JCS 1067's analogous directives for Germany were, in Montgomery's analytical vocabulary, the launch documents of the artificial revolution — and Montgomery's retrospective confirms what Dower's Ch. 6 argued: that the contradiction between the method (imposition) and the goal (democracy) was not resolved but displaced, surviving as a permanent tension in the occupation's legacy. Chang's "independence without liberation" for Korea is the Week 5 case where the artificial revolution was never seriously attempted: no purge, no constitutional moment, no land reform beyond vested lands — a confirmation of Montgomery's warning that without all three elements, the revolution cannot take hold even in its negative phase.

**Week 6 (Hahn, Dower Ch. 12–13, Adenauer Ch. 8, Sirota, SWNCC 228):** Montgomery's legal question — "it is not yet clear that defeat in war may legally become the means of forcing a people to become free" — is the Hague Convention tension that Hahn (Germany) and Sirota (Japan) documented from within. Hahn's "managed self-determination" in Germany's Basic Law drafting and Sirota's Article 24 (women's equality) were precisely Montgomery's second element of artificial revolution (encouraging new democratic leadership / constitutional guarantees) — executed imperfectly, under Cold War pressure, with American authorities rushing constitutional provisions to prevent Communist participation. Sirota's experience — drafting feminist constitutional language in SCAP headquarters at 22 years old — is Montgomery's "artificial revolution" from the inside, humanized. The MacArthur Constitution that Montgomery mentions as "rushed through" is the same document Dower (Ch. 12) analyzed as "a revolution from above that was embraced from below." The two characterizations coexist: Montgomery captures the process's illegitimacy; Dower captures the outcome's genuine appeal.

**Week 4 (Duara, Merritt, Atlantic Charter, Reischauer):** Montgomery's framing of the postwar occupations as historically novel — the first systematic attempt to export democracy through a structured overhaul of a defeated nation — is in direct dialogue with Duara's "imperialism of free nations." Duara argued that the 20th century's "free nations" used the rhetoric of universal democratic values to construct client states; Montgomery is narrating the institutional mechanism through which that construction operated. The Atlantic Charter's promise of "self-determination for all peoples" was the ideological cover for what Montgomery shows was a legally problematic suspension of national sovereignty. Reischauer's "Japan as anchor" strategy (W4PS) is the geopolitical logic within which Montgomery's artificial revolution operated: Japan needed to be democratized not primarily for Japan's sake but to anchor the Pacific against Soviet expansion. This framing makes Ladejinsky's "plow outbids the sword" the economic-agrarian articulation of Reischauer's geopolitical argument.

**Weeks 1–3 (Fradera, Kramer, May, Constantino, Mabini):** Montgomery's Hague Convention argument — unconditional surrender as the legal mechanism that allowed occupiers to override existing national law and reshape political institutions — is the 20th-century legal expression of Fradera's "exceptionality" concept from Week 1. Fradera showed that modern empires carved out formal legal exceptions for colonial populations within liberal political systems; Montgomery shows that "unconditional surrender" was the 20th century's mechanism for enacting the same exception under the language of anti-totalitarianism rather than civilization. Constantino's "miseducation of the Filipino" finds a Week 10 echo in both readings: the ideal outcome of land reform and purge, in Ladejinsky's and Montgomery's frameworks respectively, is occupied peoples who come to want what the occupier installed — farmers who defend private property, citizens who vote for democratic parties. The difference from Constantino's critique is that Ladejinsky believes this outcome genuinely serves the occupied peoples' interests; Constantino argues that making colonized peoples want what colonizers want is itself the deepest form of colonial damage.

**Week 9 (Jarausch, Kojima, Callam et al., Hurh, Jaima):** Week 9 showed what occupation's cultural legacy looked like a decade after formal authority ended — Westernization still proceeding, soft power still operating, performance demanded. Week 10's retrospective readings address the same period (Montgomery's 1952–54 Germany field research; Ladejinsky's 1959 Japan assessment) but from a policy/institutional vantage rather than a cultural one. Jarausch's "star-pupil syndrome" — Germany's unconditional embrace of Western values masking evasion of guilt — is visible in Montgomery's concern that denazification (removing old elites) was the negative phase that left a vacuum filled by whoever was available, not necessarily by genuine democratic new leadership. Kojima's typology of occupied response (passive resistance / opportunistic collaboration / pragmatic adaptation) maps onto Montgomery's question about leadership succession: after the purge, who stepped into the vacated positions, and on what terms? Week 9's cultural analysis and Week 10's institutional retrospective together show that the artificial revolution and the cultural project were two faces of the same attempt — and both assessments, a decade out, remain uncertain whether success or failure is the right word.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 4 (Cold War reversal / "reverse course")** is Week 10's organizing tension. Montgomery theorizes it most concisely: "If the totalitarian ambitions of Hitler had induced the democracies to engage in artificial revolution, those of Stalin caused them to alter its course." The reverse course is not, in Montgomery's analysis, a betrayal of occupation's ideals — it is the structural consequence of those ideals having been launched in wartime urgency and sustained through Cold War competition. Ladejinsky confirms and complicates this: land reform is the exception that proves the rule, the one reform that became irreversible precisely because its reversal would have created more Cold War instability than its preservation. The two together define the Cold War logic with unusual precision: reforms survived when their reversal would have been destabilizing; they were abandoned when their continuation competed with more pressing Cold War priorities.

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy & planning — punitive vs. reformist)** is redefined in Week 10. Montgomery's three-part program (eliminate old elites, encourage new leadership, install institutional guarantees) is the most precise analytical framework the course has offered for what reformist occupation was *supposed to do*. His conclusion — that only the first element (elimination) was executed with any durability, while the second and third were interrupted — provides the conceptual summary for the punitive/reformist tension the course has tracked across all case studies. Ladejinsky's land reform is the one documented case where the reformist program achieved all three elements: old landlord class eliminated as political force; new freeholder class empowered as democratic constituency; institutional guarantees (elected local land commissions, security tenure) built into the reform's operational structure.

**Theme 3 (Land reform & economic restructuring)** takes its retrospective form in Week 10. Ladejinsky's 1959 essay is the most sustained assessment in the course's readings of whether a specific occupation economic policy worked. His verdict — that 5.8 million acres transferred, tenancy collapsed from 46% to 11%, and Communist appeal in rural Japan eliminated — is the positive case for occupation economic transformation. But he also identifies the structural limit: Japan's farm population continued growing (unlike the U.S.) and 40% of farmer income was already coming from off-farm work by 1957. Land reform stabilized rural Japan politically but could not permanently sustain 37 million agrarians economically — industrialization, not more redistribution, would have to absorb the surplus. This anticipates the course's broader theme that occupation's economic reforms, where they succeeded, created conditions for subsequent development rather than development itself.

**Theme 2 (Democratic reform & constitutionalism)** appears in Week 10 at its most theoretically dense. Montgomery's paradox — "the democratic idea, unlike the totalitarian one, cannot consistently be imposed upon a people" — is the theoretical distillation of the constitutional moments Week 6 documented in practice. Hahn's Basic Law, Sirota's Article 24, SWNCC 228's guidance all involved Allied authorities drafting or directing democratic constitutional provisions for occupied peoples who were simultaneously excluded from the drafting process. Montgomery's Hague Convention concern — whether unconditional surrender legally allows occupiers to force a people to become free — is the international law version of the same contradiction that Dower's "revolution from above" captured empirically. Week 10 brings this tension to its most explicit and unresolved articulation: the occupations may have been legally dubious, democratically paradoxical, and historically novel — and may still have produced genuinely democratic outcomes.

**Theme 8 (Collaboration & resistance by occupied peoples)** finds its most nuanced Week 10 expression in Ladejinsky's "strange partnership." Hiroo Wada — Japan's left-wing Socialist Minister of Agriculture — is the course's clearest example of an occupied actor driving the occupation's most durable reform from inside its institutional structures. He was not a reluctant collaborator; he was an indigenous reformer who used MacArthur's authority as leverage against the Japanese government's foot-dragging. This is neither Kim's KDP obstruction (Week 7) nor Constantino's miseducated colonial subject (Week 3) — it is something closer to Adenauer's strategic embrace (Weeks 6–7): using the occupier's power to accomplish what domestic politics had repeatedly blocked. Week 10's retrospective suggests that this model — indigenous reformer + occupier authority — produced the most durable outcomes, more durable than either pure American imposition or purely occupied-peoples' initiative within colonial constraints.

## Combined Key Terms

*Deduplicated across both Week 10 readings, with cross-references.*

- **"Artificial revolution"** (Montgomery): Montgomery's coinage for the postwar occupations' historical novelty — an externally imposed, programmed installation of democracy through elite removal, new leadership, and institutional design. "Artificial" contrasts with "natural" (popular revolution) and "permanent" (Marxist continuous revolution). Programmed, peaceful, and reversible — which is its defining feature and its fundamental weakness. Cross-reference: Dower's "revolution from above" (W5); Fradera's "exceptionality" (W1).

- **Three-part program of artificial revolution** (Montgomery): (1) Elimination of despotic elites through denazification/purge; (2) encouragement and support of new democratic leadership; (3) constitutional, legal, and institutional assurances of the new order. Montgomery's retrospective verdict: only (1) was durably executed; (2) and (3) were interrupted by Cold War reversal. Cross-reference: denazification, political purge, MacArthur Constitution (W5/W6).

- **Denazification** (Montgomery): Allied program removing Nazi party members from positions in public life, business, and government. Montgomery's paradigm case of artificial revolution's "negative" phase — removing old elites without guaranteeing new democratic ones stepped in. Cross-reference: political purge in Japan; connects to JCS 1067 (W5).

- **Political purge (Japan)** (Montgomery): Japanese equivalent of denazification — removal of militarist leaders from public office. Like denazification, the primary surviving artifact of artificial revolution after Cold War pressure ended positive reform programs. Cross-reference: SWNCC 150 (W5); Dower Ch. 6 (W5).

- **Hague Convention (Art. 43, 1907)** (Montgomery): International law requiring occupiers to respect the laws of defeated nations. Allied postwar occupations systematically violated this in reforming political, economic, and social institutions. Montgomery's argument: unconditional surrender may have created a legal exception, but "it is unpleasantly close to suggesting that a great power may force a nation to abdicate a portion of its sovereign rights." Cross-reference: connects to Fradera's "exceptionality" (W1) — liberal systems constructing formal legal exceptions for colonial populations.

- **Unconditional surrender** (Montgomery): The surrender terms of Germany and Japan — contrasted with conditional surrenders — that arguably stripped them of protections under international law and enabled the scope of Allied reforms. Distinguished from earlier occupation precedents (post-WWI Germany) which were subject to armistice conditions. Cross-reference: connects to SWNCC 150/JCS 1067 (W5) as the documents operationalizing what unconditional surrender made possible.

- **Cold War reversal / "Reverse course"** (Montgomery + Ladejinsky): Montgomery's formulation: "If the totalitarian ambitions of Hitler had induced the democracies to engage in artificial revolution, those of Stalin caused them to alter its course." Artificial revolution left mainly in its negative (elite-removal) phase. Ladejinsky's counter-case: land reform as "conspicuous exception" — the one reform that survived because its reversal would have been more destabilizing than its preservation. Cross-reference: Dower Ch. 17 (W7); Kim (W7); Gillingham (W7).

- **"Conspicuous exception"** (Ladejinsky): Ladejinsky's characterization of Japan's land reform — the one Occupation reform that survived intact when "virtually every other reform measure has been revised or legislated out of existence." Survived because its reversal would have re-destabilized the same rural population the reform had pacified. Cross-reference: Dower's "reverse course" (W7); Kim's partial Korea land reform (W7).

- **"Strange partnership"** (Ladejinsky): Ladejinsky's formula for what made Japan's land reform work — "a left-wing Socialist [Hiroo Wada] and an American five-star General, and in the order cited." Japanese indigenous reformer driving the reform's design; American authority providing cover against Japanese government reluctance. The occupation's most successful model: enabling indigenous reform, not imposing American preferences. Cross-reference: Adenauer's strategic collaboration (W6/W7); Wada vs. KDP obstruction in Korea (Kim W7).

- **Hiroo Wada** (Ladejinsky): Japan's Socialist Minister of Agriculture; Ladejinsky calls him "the real architect of the revolution." Complicates the "American-made" narrative — the reform idea was indigenous to prewar Japanese agrarian debate; SCAP's role was to force the Japanese government's hand. Cross-reference: Kim's KDP as obstruction (W7) — contrasting example of indigenous actors *blocking* rather than driving reform.

- **Freeholder / Cold War agrarianism** (Ladejinsky): Ladejinsky's ideal political subject — the small farmer who owns his land, is economically independent, and is naturally resistant to Communist collectivization. Grounded in both Jeffersonian agrarianism ("freeholder as natural supporter of free government") and Cold War political logic ("plow outbids the sword"). Cross-reference: Week 7 Ladejinsky (W7PS); Kim's critique (W7).

- **Rack-rent tenancy** (Ladejinsky): Pre-reform Japanese system in which tenants paid exorbitant rents (often in kind), leaving insufficient produce to sustain families. The structural basis of rural poverty, political discontent, and Communist recruitment vulnerability. 70% of Japan's farm families were tenants or part-tenants before 1946. Cross-reference: Kim's pre-reform Korean tenancy (W7).

- **Decartelization / zaibatsu dissolution** (Montgomery + Week 7 cross-reference): The positive economic reform programs in Germany and Japan respectively — abandoned due to Cold War need for rapid economic recovery and industrial war production. Montgomery's clearest example of the artificial revolution's positive program being cut short by Cold War reversal. Cross-reference: Gillingham's "industrial revival without reform" (W7); Dower's keiretsu reconstitution (W7).

- **"Ambition outran performance"** (Montgomery): Montgomery's summary judgment on the postwar occupations — the program was more ambitious than what was actually accomplished. Not a condemnation (the ambition was unprecedented and partly warranted) but a sober assessment of the gap between what was attempted and what survived Cold War pressure. Cross-reference: Dower's more detailed Japanese case showing same pattern (W5/W7).

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: "Artificial Revolution" / "Revolution from Above" / "Reverse Course"**
*(Montgomery W10 + Dower W5/W7 + Ladejinsky/Montgomery W10 — spanning Weeks 5, 7, and 10)*

Montgomery's "artificial revolution" and Dower's "revolution from above" are two framings of the same historical event — the postwar occupations as externally imposed democratic transformation — that reach different conclusions about its most important feature. Dower emphasizes the contradiction between method (imposition) and goal (democracy), arguing that Japanese society's enthusiastic embrace partially redeemed the paradox. Montgomery emphasizes the temporal arc: the revolution was cut short by Cold War pressure, leaving primarily the negative phase (elite removal) intact. Ladejinsky's land reform, analyzed across both Week 7 and Week 10, is the empirical test case: it is the one domain where revolution from above became irreversible — precisely because it redistributed not just political authority (Montgomery's institutional focus) but material property (Ladejinsky's emphasis). The reverse course could revise labor laws, rehabilitate zaibatsu, and re-arm the demilitarized — but it could not re-collectivize 3 million farmers who had just acquired land for the first time. The difference between revolution from above that endured and revolution from above that was reversed is the difference between institutional reform and material redistribution.

---

**Set 2: "Conspicuous Exception" / "Strange Partnership" / "Blank Slate"**
*(Ladejinsky W10 + Ladejinsky W10 + Kim W7 — linking Week 7 and Week 10)*

Ladejinsky's two Week 10 formulations — "conspicuous exception" (the reform that survived) and "strange partnership" (the mechanism that made it work) — together answer Kim's Week 7 methodological point that "the occupied territory is not a blank slate." Kim showed that in Korea, the occupied territory's indigenous political actors (KDP landlords) used the occupation's own alliance structures to block land reform from inside. Japan's land reform succeeded not despite this dynamic but through a different version of it: Hiroo Wada (indigenous actor) used MacArthur's authority (occupier's power) to force land redistribution against the Japanese government's resistance. Both cases confirm Kim's anti-blank-slate thesis — the occupied territory's internal political structure was decisive in determining what the occupier could actually accomplish. But they yield opposite outcomes: in Korea, indigenous conservatism captured the occupation's political alliance and produced obstruction; in Japan, indigenous reformism captured the occupation's military authority and produced transformation. The blank slate was never available; what determined outcomes was *which* local actors succeeded in capturing occupation power for their own purposes.

---

**Set 3: "Plow Outbids the Sword" / "Artificial Revolution" / Hague Convention**
*(Ladejinsky W7/W10 + Montgomery W10 + Montgomery W10 — spanning Weeks 7 and 10)*

Ladejinsky's Cold War agrarianism — "no government in Asia can hope to survive without peasant support" — and Montgomery's artificial revolution framework look like complementary strategies, but they rest on incompatible premises. Montgomery's framework assumes democracy must ultimately be chosen, not imposed — the Hague Convention tension runs throughout his analysis because democracy's legitimacy depends on consent. Ladejinsky's framework assumes that material redistribution can *produce* consent: give farmers land, and consent to the democratic order follows from interest rather than ideology. Montgomery's worry is legal and philosophical (can defeat in war legally become the means of forcing a people to become free?); Ladejinsky's answer is pragmatic and empirical (it worked: Communists became the one party Japanese farmers consistently rejected). The resolution is that the Hague Convention paradox was bypassed in land reform because the occupied people who benefited were not "forced to be free" by a constitutional mandate they didn't write — they were given land they genuinely wanted. The artificial revolution's legitimacy problem disappears when the reform produces a material stake that the recipient has every reason to defend.

---

**Set 4: Denazification / "Miseducation" / "Star-Pupil Syndrome"**
*(Montgomery W10 + Constantino W3 + Jarausch W9 — spanning Weeks 3, 9, and 10)*

Montgomery identifies denazification as the one element of artificial revolution that was durably executed — old elites removed — but worries that removal created a vacuum filled by "other domestic forces" without guarantee that those forces were genuinely democratic. Jarausch's "star-pupil syndrome" (Week 9) names what filled that vacuum in Germany: unconditional Western embrace that masked evasion of guilt beneath the performance of democratic transformation. Constantino's "miseducation" (Week 3) names the deeper problem across all three: colonial/occupation transformation that produces subjects who understand their own liberation in the occupier's terms. Denazification removed Nazi *personnel* but could not remove Nazi *formation* — the habits of mind, the political instincts, the social networks that years of totalitarian culture had built. "Star-pupil" behavior was what that formation looked like after denazification: performed democracy, not internalized democracy. Together, the three concepts define the problem of "negative" artificial revolution — you can purge an elite without producing its democratic replacement, and the resulting culture may be Westernized on the surface while unchanged in the depths.

---

**Set 5: "Imperialism of Free Nations" / "Forced to be Free" / "Indigenous Reform"**
*(Duara W4 + Montgomery W10 + Ladejinsky W10 — spanning Weeks 4 and 10)*

Duara's Week 4 framework — "imperialism of free nations" using the rhetoric of universal self-determination to construct client states — raises the sharpest challenge to Montgomery's and Ladejinsky's retrospective assessments. If the occupations were systematically instruments of client-state formation (Duara's view), then Montgomery's "artificial revolution" is not historically novel but simply the latest version of liberal imperialism's foundational technique: imposing dependency while performing liberation. Ladejinsky's "indigenous reform" counter-argument — that Japan's land reform was "not American-made, packaged and delivered" but conceptually indigenous — is the empirical response to Duara's structural critique. Ladejinsky is saying: yes, the occupation operated within an imperialist framework, but within that framework, indigenous reformers used American power to accomplish what Japanese conservative politics had blocked for decades. This does not refute Duara — Japan remained a U.S. Cold War client regardless — but it complicates the purely extractive logic of "imperialism of free nations" by showing that some occupied peoples successfully used imperial leverage to achieve genuinely desired outcomes. "Forced to be free" is most accurate as a description of the *method*; whether it describes the *outcome* depends on whether what was installed was what the occupied actually wanted.

## Essay Questions

1. Montgomery argues that the postwar occupations constituted "the first halting effort to induce 'artificial revolution' on behalf of democracy" — but that "ambition outran performance," as Cold War pressure reduced the program to its negative phase (denazification and purge) while abandoning positive reform. Ladejinsky's 1959 retrospective on Japan's land reform represents the course's most detailed evidence of an occupation reform that *did* achieve durable results. Using Montgomery's three-part framework alongside Ladejinsky (Weeks 7 and/or 10), Dower (Week 5 or 7), and at least one reading from the Germany or Korea cases (Kim, Gillingham, Hahn, Chang), assess what made the difference between occupation reforms that survived the reverse course and those that did not. Was durability primarily about timing, the nature of the reform itself (material vs. institutional), the role of local actors, or Cold War contingency?

2. Ladejinsky insists that Japan's land reform was "not American-made, packaged and delivered" but driven by the "strange partnership" of Hiroo Wada and MacArthur — Japanese indigenous initiative enabled by American authority. Montgomery's retrospective similarly focuses on whether occupied peoples' own leadership filled the vacuum left by denazification. Using these two Week 10 readings alongside Kim's Korea (Week 7), Adenauer's Germany (Week 6 or 7), and at least one earlier reading about colonial agency (Mabini W3, Chang W5/W6, or Constantino W3), analyze the role of indigenous actors in shaping occupation outcomes. When did occupied people most successfully redirect what Americans intended — and what distinguished cases where that redirection produced durable reform from cases where it produced obstruction or co-optation?

3. Montgomery writes that "it is not yet clear that defeat in war may legally become the means of forcing a people to become free." The Hague Convention forbade occupiers from altering the political machinery of defeated nations; unconditional surrender arguably suspended that protection. Using Montgomery's legal-philosophical tension alongside Fradera's "exceptionality" concept (Week 1), Duara's "imperialism of free nations" (Week 4), and at least two readings documenting specific occupation reforms (Sirota W6, Hahn W6, Ladejinsky W10, or the constitutional directives from Weeks 5–6), assess whether the postwar occupations of Germany and Japan can be understood as genuinely distinct from earlier imperial occupation — or whether Montgomery's "artificial revolution" is liberal imperialism under new historical conditions. What would it take to distinguish the two?
