# Agrarian Revolution in Japan
**Author:** Wolf Ladejinsky | **Week:** 10 | **Type:** PS (analytical retrospective)
**Case Study:** Japan
**Pages:** 95–109 (*Foreign Affairs*, vol. 38, no. 1, October 1959)

---

## Summary

Writing a decade after Japan's land reform, Ladejinsky — one of its architects — delivers a verdict: the redistribution worked, and it worked *because* it was free. This is not a neutral assessment; Ladejinsky is defending his own program. But the evidence he marshals is substantial and the Cold War argument is explicit: Japan proves that non-Communist governments can solve the agrarian question.

**Pre-reform conditions.** 70% of Japanese farm families were tenants or part-tenants, cultivating more than half the land under exploitative rack-rent conditions. Landlords held money, culture, and political power; farmers were idealized as bearers of tradition while actually trapped in feudal poverty. This structural inequality made rural Japan a textbook Communist recruitment target.

**The reform mechanics.** MacArthur's directive of December 15, 1945 ordered equal opportunity for farmers to enjoy the fruits of their labor. The October 1946 legislation operationalized it: compulsory sale of *all* absentee landlord land; compulsory sale of resident landlord land above 2.5 acres (the average farm size); cash rentals and security tenure for remaining tenants. Between 1947 and 1949, 5.8 million acres transferred. Owner-operated share rose from 54% to 92%; approximately 3 million farmers acquired land.

**Why the "so what" matters.** Ladejinsky's core argument is anti-Communist polemics backed by sociology: private ownership of land *creates* political stability. New owners held onto their land (annual sales <1% of total, one-third the prewar rate); the reform proved irreversible even as other Occupation reforms were rolled back. Village political culture shifted — farmers began voting their economic interests rather than following village bosses, and Communists became the one party that rural Japan consistently rejected, having opposed the reform as a MacArthur "plot."

**Authorship question.** Ladejinsky complicates the "American-made" narrative. The reform was *indigenous* — prewar Japan had already debated it. The Occupation's role was to overcome Japanese government reluctance and support reform-minded Japanese, chief among them Hiroo Wada (Socialist Minister of Agriculture), the reform's real architect. The "strange partnership of a left-wing Socialist and an American five-star General" is Ladejinsky's formula. This directly complicates the W7 Dower material on top-down democratic revolution.

**Contrast with Communist agrarian revolutions.** Soviet forced collectivization (1929–30) required mass violence; Chinese communization is still ongoing and unresolved. Japan's reform achieved the same redistribution goal without bloodshed or coercion — and then *kept* the land in private hands. This is the model Ladejinsky wants non-Communist Asia to emulate: Korea, Taiwan, South Vietnam, Burma, India, Pakistan.

**Remaining limits.** Ladejinsky is honest about structural problems: Japan's farm population grew 10% between 1920 and 1957 (while the US shrank 22%), meaning holdings will keep fragmenting. 40% of farmer income already comes from non-agricultural work. The long-term answer lies in industrialization absorbing rural surplus — land reform alone cannot sustain 37 million agrarians.

**Connects to guiding questions:** WHAT did they do (land reform as core occupation policy); HOW did occupation end (land reform as durable legacy that survived the reverse course); WHY occupy (Cold War anti-Communist rationale explicitly stated).

## Key Terms

- **Land reform (Japan, 1946–1949):** Compulsory redistribution of landlord land to tenants; 5.8 million acres transferred; owner-operated share rose from 54% to 92%. MacArthur directive (Dec. 1945) + October 1946 legislation.
- **Rack-rent tenancy:** Pre-reform system in which tenants paid exorbitant rents, often in kind, leaving them with insufficient produce to sustain families. Structural basis of rural poverty and political discontent.
- **Freeholder:** Small farmer who owns the land he cultivates. Ladejinsky's ideal political subject — economically independent, naturally conservative, resistant to Communist mobilization.
- **MacArthur directive (Dec. 15, 1945):** The order to the Japanese government to redistribute land; framed in democratic/dignity language beyond mere economic redistribution.
- **Hiroo Wada:** Socialist Minister of Agriculture; Ladejinsky calls him "the real architect of the revolution" — Japanese agency over American imposition.
- **Cold War agrarianism:** The logic that land reform = anti-Communism; occupied peoples with a stake in private property will reject Communist collectivization. Japan as proof of concept; model for Korea, Taiwan, South Vietnam.
- **Reverse course:** Post-1947–48 shift in Occupation policy away from reform toward anti-Communist stabilization. Ladejinsky notes most Occupation reforms were "revised or legislated out of existence" — land reform is the "conspicuous exception."
- **Village oligarchy / boss system:** Pre-reform rural political structure in which local bosses delivered peasant votes en bloc to conservative parties. Land reform disrupted this, producing more independent voting behavior.
- **Collectivization (Soviet/Chinese model):** Communist alternative to private land redistribution — state seizure, collective farms, communes. Ladejinsky uses this as contrast case: violence, coercion, ongoing instability versus Japan's peaceful freeholding.
