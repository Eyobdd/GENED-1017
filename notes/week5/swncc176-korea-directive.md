## Summary

SWNCC 176/8, issued 13 October 1945 as a Top Secret directive to the commanding general of US Army Forces in Korea, is the operational framework for the American occupation of Korea south of the 38th parallel. Read together with SWNCC 150 (Japan) and JCS 1067 (Germany), it completes the triptych of American occupation directives from 1945 — and its tensions reveal the fundamental contradictions in US occupation ideology.

The document's stated objectives in Section 3 are explicitly liberationist: the US mission is to "foster conditions which will bring about the establishment of a free and independent nation capable of taking her place as a responsible and peaceful member of the family of nations." The Cairo Declaration (1943) had promised Korea independence "in due course," and SWNCC 176 operationalizes that promise through democratization language and reference to the UN Charter. Section 3c even specifies "To foster the establishment of local self-government and the restoration of a free and independent nation which will conform with the principles expressed in the Charter of the United Nations."

Yet the operative restrictions in Section 5 directly contradict this liberationist framing. Section 5g — the most important passage for Chang's analysis — states: "You will not extend official recognition to, nor utilize for political purposes, any self-styled Korean provisional government or similar political organizations." This prohibition dissolved the Korean People's Republic (KPR) and blocked Korean political actors from translating grassroots mobilization into recognized authority. As Chang documents, this was not a neutral stance — the US simultaneously "threw their support behind reactionary rightists" (Chang, p. 84), meaning the prohibition on recognizing the KPR was politically one-sided.

Section 5's other provisions continue existing laws, abrogate laws restricting civil liberties (continuity with Japanese colonial legal framework, with humanitarian modifications), and establish a US Military Government. The effect, as Chang argues, was to preserve the colonial administrative apparatus — including the Korean National Police — while displacing the grassroots democratic organizations that had emerged in the occupation's first weeks.

Sections 6 (Demilitarization) and 7 (Arrest and Internment) complete the security framework: arrest Japanese and Korean war criminals, disarm all military forces, and establish US control over the security apparatus. Again, the gap between stated objectives (free and independent Korea) and operational tools (retain colonial police, suppress KPR, arrest political opponents) is the crucial analytical problem.

SWNCC 176 is most productively read against SWNCC 150: both use reformist, self-determination language; both invoke popular will and democratic process. But SWNCC 176's Korea produces a different outcome — because the US was simultaneously managing decolonization from Japanese rule AND the emerging Cold War competition with the Soviet Union in the north. Chang's thesis — "independence without liberation," "decolonization without liberation" — is rooted in the gap between SWNCC 176's text and the occupation's practice.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **SWNCC 176/8**: State-War-Navy Coordinating Committee document 176, eighth revision, October 13, 1945. Top Secret directive for Korea occupation. Assigned pages 4-7 cover the core policy objectives and operational constraints.
- **Section 5g**: The prohibition on recognizing "any self-styled Korean provisional government or similar political organizations." The operative clause that dissolved the Korean People's Republic (KPR) and blocked grassroots Korean political authority — Chang's key analytical target.
- **Korean People's Republic (KPR)**: The grassroots government formed by Korean activists in September 1945, led by Lyuh Woon-hyung. Had established local committees across the country within days of liberation. Dissolved by US Military Government under authority of Section 5g of SWNCC 176.
- **Direct vs. indirect rule**: SWNCC 176 established direct US Military Government in Korea, contrasting with SWNCC 150's indirect rule through the Emperor in Japan. The difference reflected both Korea's colonial (rather than sovereign) status and the US decision not to work through Korean political structures.
- **"Foster conditions"**: The governing language of both SWNCC 150 (Japan) and SWNCC 176 (Korea) — the US role as facilitator of democratization, not imposer of a specific form. But the conditions actually fostered (through Section 5g and support for rightists) contradicted this language.
- **38th parallel**: The arbitrary division line established by US-Soviet agreement in August 1945, dividing Korea into US (south) and Soviet (north) occupation zones. SWNCC 176 governs only the southern zone.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to Chang, "Independence without Liberation" (W5)**: SWNCC 176 is the primary source that Chang's article analyzes. Chang shows that Section 5g's prohibition on the KPR was not politically neutral — it cleared the field for rightist collaborators while disabling the democratic popular movement. The document's liberationist language (Section 3) and operative restrictions (Section 5g) embody Chang's thesis: "independence without liberation."

- **Connects to SWNCC 150 (Japan directive, W5)**: Both documents use similar reformist, self-determination language. Both invoke popular will and democratic process. But SWNCC 176 (Korea) produced a different outcome because of the Cold War context, the absence of indirect-rule through existing legitimate institutions, and the US decision to dissolve the KPR rather than work through it as MacArthur worked through the Emperor.

- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4)**: Section 3c explicitly invokes "principles expressed in the Charter of the United Nations" — which traced its lineage to the Atlantic Charter's self-determination clause. But as Chang shows, the US "wanted to have their Atlantic Charter and retreat from it too" (p. 80). SWNCC 176 demonstrates exactly how: the Atlantic Charter language was in the objectives, while the operative clauses undermined it.

- **Connects to JCS 1067 (Germany directive, W5)**: The comparison highlights how Korea falls between Germany (explicitly punitive) and Japan (explicitly reformist) in terms of stated objectives. Korea gets the reformist language of SWNCC 150 but the operative effect of JCS 1067 — political structures dissolved, security apparatus retained.

- **Connects to US/Soviet occupation flyers (W5)**: The Soviet flyer declared "Korea has become a free state" and addressed workers and citizens directly. The US flyer warned against "hasty and ill-advised acts." SWNCC 176's Section 5g is the policy backdrop for that warning — it is the operative document that authorized suppressing the KPR and the labor movement that the Soviet flyer rhetorically celebrated.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*SWNCC 176 / Chang / Section 5g*
SWNCC 176's Section 5g prohibited recognition of any "self-styled Korean provisional government." Chang shows this dissolved the Korean People's Republic and enabled US support for rightist collaborators — the exact opposite of liberation. The document's reformist objectives (Section 3) and operative restrictions (Section 5g) embody Chang's thesis: the US built "independence without liberation" on the foundation of "decolonization without liberation."

*SWNCC 176 / Atlantic Charter / Self-determination*
SWNCC 176 explicitly invokes the Charter of the United Nations and promises to foster a "free and independent" Korea. The Atlantic Charter had declared the right of peoples to choose their governments. Yet Section 5g prohibited recognizing the government Koreans had actually formed (KPR). The gap between principle and practice — self-determination invoked rhetorically, denied operationally — is the defining tension of American occupation ideology.

*SWNCC 176 / SWNCC 150 / Direct vs. indirect rule*
SWNCC 150 governed Japan through the Emperor — indirect rule through existing legitimate institutions. SWNCC 176 established direct US Military Government in Korea and prohibited recognition of existing Korean political organizations (Section 5g). The contrast reveals how the same occupier applied different governance strategies: Japan's indirect rule preserved and directed Japanese agency; Korea's direct rule displaced it.
