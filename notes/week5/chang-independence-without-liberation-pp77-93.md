# "Independence without Liberation": Democratization as Decolonization Management in U.S.-Occupied Korea, 1945–1948
**Author:** Kornel Chang | **Week:** 5 | **Type:** RR
**Case Study:** Korea
**Pages:** pp. 77-93 (Journal of American History, June 2020; pp. 93-106 assigned Week 6)

## Summary

Kornel Chang's central argument: the US occupation of Korea (1945-1948) should be read not as a botched liberation but as a successful exercise in **"decolonization management"** — a strategy by which the United States built its postwar informal empire precisely *through* the language and machinery of decolonization. His thesis: "an empire without colonies was built upon the foundation of decolonization without liberation" (p. 81, concluding statement paraphrased; also stated at p. 106).

Chang reframes the occupation away from Cold War causation (the conventional lens since Cumings 1981) toward a decolonization-studies lens, showing that the US faced the challenge of unwinding Japanese colonialism across the Pacific — Solomon Islands, Marshall Islands, Guam, Philippines, and Korea — and that Korea illuminates how it handled this challenge everywhere. The key move: pivot from actual liberation (supporting popular democratic movements) to "decolonization management" (controlled transition that preserves American strategic interests).

**Korea's Asian Spring (pp. 81-84):** In the weeks between Japan's surrender and US troops arriving (August-September 1945), Koreans launched one of the most remarkable bursts of popular democratic self-organization in postwar history. The Korean People's Republic (KPR), a broad coalition of communists, socialists, Christians, and social democrats under Lyuh Woon-hyung, established People's Committees across the country. Workers spontaneously seized Japanese-owned factories — by November 1945, workers' control committees had taken over more than 700 factories involving ~80,000 workers. Peasants seized Japanese lands and those of collaborators. Chang stresses these were not anarchic: workers wanted wages, working conditions, and a share of production; peasants wanted "land to the tillers." The communist vanguard was actually nervous about this spontaneity, fearing American reaction. Liberation meant different things to different Koreans — socialist revolution for some, reform for others — but it was genuinely popular.

**Narrowing Independence (pp. 84-92):** US forces arrived and systematically reversed this:
- **Dissolved the KPR** and People's Committees; threw support to "reactionary rightists" (Syngman Rhee, Korean Democratic Party — landed and business elites with collaborationist pasts)
- **Revived the Korean National Police (KNP)**: the single most-hated institution of Japanese colonial rule, reconstituted as an arm of the right, used to suppress leftist and center-left activists. By late 1946, most prominent Korean leftists were in prison or underground
- **Labor policy**: Ordinance No. 19 enshrined "free labor" (individual right to work) without the right to strike, organize, or bargain meaningfully; National Labor Mediation Board imposed binding arbitration; October 1945 made strikes illegal. Liberal adviser Owen Jones called these rights "hollow"
- **Rice market liberalization**: Free market experiment failed catastrophically — overconsumption and black-market smuggling converted a bumper crop to shortage; by March 1946 the military government reverted to Japanese-style rationing
- **Land reform paralysis**: Military government took custody of Japanese-owned farmlands but made virtually no progress on redistribution; the KDP (their political ally) consisted of the landlords who would lose land
- **Torture and police brutality**: Robinson-Roberts investigation found systematic water torture and abuse; military command ordered advisers not to interfere: "out of necessity they must be used"

The result was a downward spiral. By late 1946, polls showed more than half of Korean respondents preferred Japanese colonial rule to US occupation.

**Autumn Strikes (pp. 89-93):** In September 1946, the communist-led Chŏnp'yŏng union organized workers across the South. The Southern Korean Railway Workers Union struck September 23 after their demands were ignored; within hours 36,000 railway workers joined; a general strike of over 300,000 workers followed. Hodge had the KNP and allied rightist youth violently suppress the strikes — 1 worker killed, 40 injured, 1,700 arrested in Seoul alone. Peasant uprisings followed in North and South Kyŏngsang and Chŏlla Provinces after police killed an unarmed demonstrator in Taegu. The Taegu uprising killed 50 policemen; US troops were sent in alongside Korean police. The occupation's suppression of the strikes institutionalized a police state in the South.

Chang insists the strikes were not Soviet-directed: adviser Meacham documented they began as a "desperate last resort" after repeated rebuffs; legal adviser Roger Baldwin investigated and found "the actual Communists among them who want a single-party Communist state allied with the Soviet are comparatively few. But the military officials are making more of them daily by their policies of suppression." The Manichean anticommunist lens was self-fulfilling.

**Course guiding questions:** WHY occupy? To manage Korea's decolonization in ways compatible with US Cold War strategy and regional plans to rehabilitate Japan. WHAT did they do? Dissolved democratic structures, revived colonial police, criminalized labor action, paralyzed land reform. HOW did it end? Not covered in pp. 77-93, but the foundation laid here — elections as "semblance of sovereignty" — anticipates Chang's Week 6 argument.

## Key Terms

- **"Independence without liberation"**: Formal sovereignty achieved without liberation from colonial economic structures, political collaborators, or social hierarchies. Korea got independence from Japan without liberation from the conditions that colonialism created.
- **Decolonization management**: Chang's term for US strategy — active management of the colonial-to-postcolonial transition to ensure Cold War-compatible outcomes, not neutral facilitation of self-determination.
- **Korean People's Republic (KPR)**: Grassroots government formed September 1945, dissolved by US forces under SWNCC 176 §5g. Its dissolution is the pivotal moment in Chang's argument.
- **"Semblance of sovereignty"** (borrowing from Alexander Aleinikoff): What elections gave South Korea — the procedural form of self-determination without the substantive content.
- **Korean National Police (KNP)**: Colonial police apparatus revived by US occupation; the single most-hated institution under Japanese rule, reconstituted by the "liberating" power.
- **Autumn Strikes (1946)**: September-October 1946 general strike of ~300,000 Korean workers, violently suppressed. Represents the failure of labor emancipation promised under occupation's democratization rhetoric.
- **"Asian Spring"**: Chang's term for the burst of popular democratic mobilization following Japan's surrender — deliberate parallel to "Arab Spring" language, emphasizing its genuinely democratic character before US arrival foreclosed it.
- **Anticommunist technocratic liberalism**: Chang's label for the ideology guiding US policy — US advisers believed in liberal democracy and free markets but interpreted any radical popular action as communist subversion, foreclosing actual democratic possibilities.
- **Roger Baldwin**: ACLU founder invited to Korea by MacArthur in 1946; his investigations corroborated Korean leftists' accounts and produced "fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy."
- **Stewart Meacham**: Labor adviser to the military government; the most consistently critical US official in Chang's account; documented police suppression of labor organizing and wrote the "Korean Labor Report" (Nov. 1947) that is one of Chang's key sources.

## Flashcards

FRONT: Chang's thesis — "independence without liberation"
BACK: "An empire without colonies was built upon the foundation of decolonization without liberation." US occupation of Korea managed transition from Japanese colonialism in ways that ensured Cold War-compatible outcomes — dissolving the KPR, supporting rightists, reviving colonial police, suppressing labor. Korea got formal independence (elections, sovereignty) without liberation (democratic popular power). Source: Chang (2020), pp. 77-93/106.

FRONT: "Fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy" (Roger Baldwin, p. 87)
BACK: ACLU founder Roger Baldwin on the US occupation of Korea — anticommunist logic used to suppress the Korean labor movement and KPR, which were democratic popular movements, not Soviet instruments. The most quotable line in the article. Significance: exposes anti-communism as the mechanism that foreclosed democratic possibilities. Connects to: Chang's "decolonization management," Dower's "contradictions of democratic revolution from above."

FRONT: Korean People's Republic (KPR)
BACK: Grassroots Korean government formed September 1945 under Lyuh Woon-hyung — people's committees across country, drew on workers, peasants, communists, socialists, Christian democrats. Dissolved by US Military Government under SWNCC 176 §5g. Chang argues its dissolution was the critical moment that converted decolonization into "independence without liberation." Source: Chang, pp. 81-85; connects to SWNCC 176 W5PS.

FRONT: "Semblance of sovereignty" (Chang, p. 80)
BACK: Chang's (borrowing Aleinikoff's) concept for what elections gave South Korea — the procedural form of self-determination (voting, recognized government, formal independence) without the substantive content (popular control, democratic institutions rooted in popular movements). Core of Chang's argument about how informal empire works: project power through the sovereignty of others. Source: Chang, p. 80.

FRONT: Autumn Strikes (1946)
BACK: September-October 1946 general strike of ~300,000 Korean workers; began with Railway Workers Union, spread to all major industries. Violently suppressed by US occupation forces with KNP support. Hodge called workers "childish" and declared strikes illegal as government employees. Baldwin documented that communist true believers were a minority — suppression manufactured the radicalism the US feared. Source: Chang, pp. 89-93.

FRONT: Decolonization management (Chang)
BACK: Chang's key concept for US occupation strategy in Korea — active management of transition from Japanese colonialism to ensure outcomes compatible with American Cold War interests (anticommunist client state), rather than neutral facilitation of Korean self-determination. Broader argument: US faced this challenge across all Pacific territories inherited from Japan; Korea is the case study that illuminates the pattern. Source: Chang (2020).
