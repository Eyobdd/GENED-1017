## Summary

Kornel Chang's "Independence without Liberation" (2020, Journal of American History) is one of the most analytically sharp readings in the course, offering a framework that cuts across the entire Week 5 cluster. Chang argues that the US occupation of Korea (1945-1948) should be understood not as a failure to apply occupation principles but as a successful exercise in "decolonization management" — a strategy by which the US built postwar empire precisely through the language and machinery of decolonization. His central thesis: "an empire without colonies was built upon the foundation of decolonization without liberation" (p. 81, paraphrased).

The article opens by situating Korea at the intersection of two historical forces: the collapse of Japanese colonialism and the emerging US-Soviet Cold War. The US faced a genuine dilemma — Korea was explicitly promised independence (Cairo Declaration, 1943; Atlantic Charter language in SWNCC 176), yet independence for Korea meant Korean self-determination, and Korean self-determination in 1945 meant the Korean People's Republic (KPR), the factory councils, the peasant land seizures — a leftist popular movement incompatible with US strategic interests. Chang's formulation captures this perfectly: the US "wanted to have their Atlantic Charter and retreat from it too" (p. 80).

The section "Korea's Asian Spring" (approximately pp. 81-85) documents the extraordinary burst of popular political energy that followed Japan's surrender on August 15, 1945. Within days, the KPR had established local people's committees across the country. Workers seized factories. Peasants appropriated land from Japanese landlords and Korean collaborators. Chang emphasizes that this was not chaos — it was organized, purposeful democratic mobilization. These were exactly the "hasty and ill-advised acts" the US occupation flyer warned against.

"Narrowing Independence" (approximately pp. 85-90) traces the US response: dissolution of the KPR under SWNCC 176 Section 5g, support for rightist political figures (Syngman Rhee, the Korean Democratic Party), revival of the Japanese colonial police apparatus through the Korean National Police, and systematic suppression of the labor movement. Richard Robinson, a US military government officer, observed that "the police did more to ruin the reputation of the United States in Korea" (p. 86) — the US imported the tools of Japanese colonialism to manage the decolonization moment. Roger Baldwin captured the paradox in a phrase Chang quotes at p. 87: "fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy."

The final section in the Week 5 assignment (approximately pp. 90-93) covers the Autumn Strikes of September-October 1946, when 300,000 workers participated in a general strike that US forces violently suppressed. Hodge's internal communications described Korean workers as "childish" and "highly volatile" (p. 90) — the paternalism of the public flyer expressed in the privacy of official correspondence. His instruction that "there is absolutely no reason for disorders in connection with legitimate labor disputes" (p. 92) essentially criminalized labor action.

Chang's argument speaks directly to the three course questions. WHY did the US occupy Korea? To manage the decolonization moment and prevent a leftist independent Korea from emerging in the Cold War context. WHAT did they do? Dissolved democratic structures, revived colonial police, suppressed labor. HOW did it end? Not covered in pp. 77-93, but the foundation laid here — "semblance of sovereignty" through elections — points toward Chang's argument that elections gave South Korea the form of self-determination without the substance.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **"Independence without liberation"**: Chang's central concept — formal independence (sovereignty, elections, self-government in name) achieved without liberation from colonial economic structures, political collaborators, or social hierarchies. The title and thesis of the article. Korea got independence from Japan without liberation from the conditions that Japanese colonialism had created.
- **Decolonization management**: Chang's term for US occupation strategy — active management of the transition from Japanese colonialism to ensure outcomes compatible with American Cold War interests, rather than neutral facilitation of Korean self-determination.
- **Korean People's Republic (KPR)**: The grassroots Korean government formed September 1945 under Lyuh Woon-hyung. Had people's committees across the country, drew on factory workers, peasants, and leftist organizers. Dissolved by US Military Government under SWNCC 176 Section 5g. Represents what "liberation" would have looked like if pursued.
- **"Semblance of sovereignty"**: Chang's phrase for what elections gave South Korea — the procedural form of self-determination (voting, recognized government) without the substantive content (popular control, democratic institutions rooted in genuine popular movements).
- **Autumn Strikes (1946)**: The September-October 1946 general strike of approximately 300,000 Korean workers — the climax of the labor movement that Chang documents in pp. 77-93. Violently suppressed by US forces. Represents the failure of labor emancipation promised by the occupation's democratization rhetoric.
- **Korean National Police (KNP)**: The colonial police apparatus revived by the US occupation — staffed by collaborators who had served Japanese colonial administration. Richard Robinson's observation that the KNP "did more to ruin the reputation of the United States in Korea" captures how the colonial security apparatus became the occupation security apparatus.
- **"Reactionary rightists"**: Chang's characterization (p. 84) of the political forces the US supported after dissolving the KPR — Syngman Rhee, the Korean Democratic Party, collaborators with the Japanese colonial regime. US support for these forces reversed the decolonization logic of the stated US mission.
- **Roger Baldwin**: Founder of the American Civil Liberties Union, quoted by Chang at p. 87: "fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy." Provides a contemporaneous liberal American critique of US occupation practice.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to SWNCC 176 (W5)**: SWNCC 176 is Chang's foundational primary source. Section 5g — the prohibition on recognizing any Korean provisional government — is the operative mechanism Chang analyzes. Chang shows that this "neutral" prohibition was politically one-sided: it dissolved the leftist KPR while enabling the US to support rightist alternatives. Reading Chang alongside SWNCC 176 makes visible the gap between stated objectives and operative effects.

- **Connects to Fradera (W1, "Reading Imperial Transitions")**: Fradera's concept of "dangerous symmetries" between rival imperial formations illuminates Chang's thesis. Chang shows that US and Soviet occupations mirrored each other structurally — both displaced Korean popular politics, both imposed external political frameworks — while using opposite rhetorical registers. The "empire without colonies" Chang identifies is a new form of what Fradera tracks across imperial transitions.

- **Connects to Mabini (W3, "Introductory Manifesto") and Constantino (W3, "Miseducation of the Filipino")**: The US pattern in Korea replicates the Philippines: support for collaborators, suppression of popular democratic movements, paternalistic characterizations of local populations as unready for self-government. Mabini argued that Philippine independence was compromised from the start by US terms; Chang makes the same argument about Korean independence. Constantino's "miseducation" thesis — colonial power shapes how subjects understand their own liberation — applies to how the occupation framed Korean aspirations.

- **Connects to Dower, Embracing Defeat Ch. 6 (W5)**: Dower examines Japanese popular responses to occupation in the same period — workers organizing, women mobilizing, political parties forming. The comparison with Korea reveals how occupation context shaped what "democratic reform" meant: in Japan, SCAP enabled and then constrained popular democratic energies; in Korea, the US dissolved the democratic structures that already existed. The comparison illuminates why "reformist occupation" produced different outcomes in Japan and Korea.

- **Connects to US/Soviet occupation flyers (W5)**: The flyers are the public-facing expression of the political logics Chang analyzes. The US flyer's warning against "hasty acts" is directed at exactly the KPR mobilization Chang documents. Hodge's public paternalism (the flyer) and private racism (calling Koreans "childish") are two faces of the same ideology.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*Chang / Mabini / "Independence without liberation"*
Chang's "independence without liberation" in Korea echoes Mabini's critique of Philippine independence in 1898: both cases involve formal sovereignty achieved on terms set by the occupying power, with popular democratic movements suppressed to ensure compatible outcomes. Mabini argued that independence compromised by imperial conditions was not true independence; Chang shows the same logic at work 50 years later in the American decolonization management of Korea.

*Chang / Atlantic Charter / Decolonization management*
The Atlantic Charter promised self-determination; Chang shows the US "wanted to have their Atlantic Charter and retreat from it too" in Korea. The gap between Atlantic Charter universalism and US occupation practice was not hypocrisy but strategy — "decolonization management" used the language of self-determination to legitimate an occupation that foreclosed the most radical democratic possibilities. Korea got "a semblance of sovereignty" through elections while being denied the liberation that popular mobilization had sought.

*Chang / Dower / Labor and democracy*
Chang and Dower both document labor movements as the cutting edge of popular democratic mobilization under occupation — Korean workers in the Autumn Strikes (300,000 workers, 1946), Japanese workers organizing under SCAP. Both also document how occupation authorities eventually suppressed or constrained these movements as Cold War pressures intensified. The comparison reveals labor politics as the central site where the promise of democratic reform was tested and found wanting across both occupations.
