# Week 5 Synthesis: Remaking Enemies and Liberating Friends
**Case Study:** Japan, Korea, Germany
**Lectures:** Getting Started / Promoting Democracy: Labor and the Left

---

## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 5 is the course's comparative core: three occupation directives (JCS 1067, SWNCC 150, SWNCC 176), two secondary sources (Dower, Chang), and two competing propaganda flyers all treating the same historical moment — 1945 — but from different vantage points and with different analytical purposes. The week's central problem is explaining why the same power applied such radically different frameworks to three contemporaneous occupations in the same year.

The three directives define a spectrum. JCS 1067 (Germany, April 1945) frames occupation as punishment: "Germany will not be occupied for the purpose of liberation but as a defeated enemy nation." Section 16 explicitly prohibits economic rehabilitation. SWNCC 150 (Japan, September 1945) frames occupation as democratic transformation through popular will: "it is not the responsibility of the Allied Powers to impose upon Japan any form of government not supported by the freely expressed will of the people." SWNCC 176 (Korea, October 1945) occupies a peculiar middle position — its stated objectives echo SWNCC 150's liberationist language ("foster conditions which will bring about the establishment of a free and independent nation"), but its operative restriction, Section 5g, prohibited recognition of any Korean provisional government, functionally dissolving the Korean People's Republic. Korea received the rhetoric of liberation without its reformist substance.

Dower and Chang are the readings that convert these directive texts into social history. Dower's "neocolonial revolution" analyzes what SWNCC 150's "Promotion of Democratic Forces" actually produced in Japan: rapid, genuine, labor-driven democratic mobilization that SCAP both enabled and ultimately constrained. The revolution was real; its context was imperial — hence Dower's paradox. Chang's "independence without liberation" analyzes what SWNCC 176's Section 5g produced in Korea: dissolution of the Korean People's Republic, support for rightist collaborators, revival of the Japanese colonial police apparatus, and violent suppression of the Autumn Strikes. Both secondary sources demonstrate that the gap between directive text and occupation practice is where the most important historical action occurs.

The US/Soviet occupation flyers complete the picture by showing how occupation ideology was packaged for direct popular consumption. Hodge's paternalism ("hasty and ill-advised acts," "speed the day when Koreans may once again enjoy life under a more democratic rule") and MacArthur's death threat in Proclamation No. 2 encode the suppressive logic of SWNCC 176 Section 5g in mass-distributed rhetoric. Chistiakov's Soviet flyer ("Korea has become a free state," "The Korean people must become the creator of its own happiness") mirrors the emancipatory register of the KPR mobilization the US was simultaneously dismantling. Read alongside Chang, the flyers reveal that Hodge's public paternalism and his private racism (calling Koreans "childish" and "highly volatile," Chang p. 90) are two registers of the same ideology — the flyer performs for the street what official correspondence says in private.

The week's deepest interpretive challenge is explaining the Japan/Korea divergence: why did the US dissolve the Korean People's Republic and suppress its labor movement while enabling (initially) Japan's comparable democratic mobilization? The answer involves Cold War proximity (Korea bordered the Soviet occupation zone), the decision to use indirect rule through the Emperor in Japan versus direct rule in Korea, and the ideological ambiguity of the KPR's leftist politics. Dower and Chang together show that "democratic reform" and "Cold War management" were not separable objectives — they were in tension from the first day of occupation, and which one dominated depended on local context, strategic calculation, and the specific institutional choices made in the critical early months.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 1 (Fradera, "Reading Imperial Transitions"):** Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" — structural similarities between rival imperial formations that use opposed rhetorical registers — is nowhere more visible than in the US/Soviet Korea flyers. Compliance vs. agency; caution vs. celebration; obedience-as-path-to-democracy vs. liberation-already-achieved. Yet both flyers were distributed by military occupiers who had displaced Korean political agency. Fradera's "exceptionality" concept — constructing inferior rules for colonial populations within otherwise liberal systems — maps precisely onto SWNCC 176 Section 5g: within a liberationist policy framework, a single operative clause carved out the exception that dissolved Korean democracy while preserving its rhetorical architecture.

**Week 2 (Kramer, Beveridge, Schurz, Teller/Platt):** Chang's "decolonization management" is the 1945 version of the Platt Amendment's informal empire. In both cases, the US gave a formally independent nation sovereignty in name while retaining operative conditions of control: the intervention clause and fiscal veto in Cuba (Platt, Art. III), the US Military Government and Korean National Police in Korea. Chang's "semblance of sovereignty" echoes what Week 2 identified as "sovereignty in name, protectorate in practice." The racial paternalism threading through Hodge's flyer and Chang's account of Hodge calling Koreans "childish" continues Kramer's "imperial racial grammar" — the civilizationist premise that non-white peoples require external guidance toward self-government, and that the occupier always determines the timetable.

**Week 3 (May, Mabini, Constantino):** Mabini's argument — coercive pacification cannot produce genuine consent — applies directly to Chang's account of the Autumn Strikes. Hodge's instruction that "there is absolutely no reason for disorders in connection with legitimate labor disputes" criminalizes labor action in the same gesture that Root's letter immunized Smith's "kill and burn" order by individualizing excess while protecting systemic policy. Constantino's "miseducation" thesis — colonial power shapes how subjects understand their own liberation — connects to the US flyer's rhetorical strategy: Koreans are told to comply to earn democracy, redefining Korean agency as the capacity for orderly obedience rather than political self-determination. The Thomasites' "second army of occupation" has its analog in Hodge's occupation apparatus — both are institutions that teach the colonized to understand liberation on the colonizer's terms.

**Week 4 (Duara, Merritt, Atlantic Charter, Reischauer):** Duara's "imperialism of free nations" — strategic competition driving all choices, anticolonial rhetoric as the vehicle for client-state formation — is the structural explanation for Week 5's comparative divergence. The Atlantic Charter promised "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live"; SWNCC 176 Section 3c invokes "the Charter of the United Nations" while Section 5g blocks Korean self-government. Chang's formulation — the US "wanted to have their Atlantic Charter and retreat from it too" (p. 80) — extends Duara's argument into operational specificity. The Cold War reversal Merritt traces in Germany (1947-48) is already underway in Korea in 1945-46 — the same sequence of reformist rhetoric followed by suppression of democratic forces, compressed into months rather than years. Reischauer's "emperor as puppet" finds its Korea analog in Hodge's decision to work through Syngman Rhee rather than the KPR — strategic reliability over democratic legitimacy.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 1 (Occupation policy & planning — punitive vs. reformist)** is the explicit comparative axis. JCS 1067 is the punitive pole; SWNCC 150 is the reformist pole; SWNCC 176 falls in between, with reformist stated objectives and punitive operative effects. The lesson Dower and Chang together teach is that the punitive/reformist distinction operates at the directive level but the "reverse course" brings reformist occupations back toward suppression as Cold War pressures intensify. The distinction was always partly rhetorical.

**Theme 4 (Cold War reversal / "reverse course")** is already visible in this week's material. The labor movement in Japan and Korea both represent the first phase of the reversal — democratic mobilization met by occupation suppression. MacArthur will ban Japan's planned general strike in February 1947; Hodge violently suppressed Korean strikes in 1946. The "reverse course" did not begin with a policy announcement in 1947; it began with choices made in the critical first months of occupation.

**Theme 5 (Race, civilization, and imperial ideology)** operates across the week in multiple registers. The JCS 1067/SWNCC 150 contrast raises the racial question — why punitive Germany, reformist Japan? — implicating racial ideology, alliance politics, and strategic calculation. The flyers' paternalism continues the civilizationist tradition. Dower's analysis of SCAP as "neocolonial" authority shows that even the reformist Japan occupation was structured by racial hierarchy — MacArthur as proconsul governing from above while managing Japanese democratic energies rather than unleashing them.

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is the week's dominant thread. The SWNCC 176 Section 5g — Atlantic Charter language in the objectives, KPR dissolution in the operatives — is the course's most compressed instantiation of this tension. Chang's "wanted to have their Atlantic Charter and retreat from it too" (p. 80) and "fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy" (Roger Baldwin, p. 87) are the week's two most important formulations. The flyers show the same tension at the rhetorical level: Soviet vs. US approaches to who holds agency over Korean liberation.

**Theme 8 (Collaboration and resistance by occupied peoples)** is centered by both secondary sources. Dower's "embrace" concept insists that Japanese workers, women, and intellectuals actively shaped the occupation's democratic opening — the "embrace" was mutual and complex. Chang's "Korea's Asian Spring" insists that the KPR, factory councils, and land appropriations were organized, purposeful democratic mobilization, not chaos. In both cases, the occupied peoples were not passive recipients; in both cases, occupation authorities eventually constrained or suppressed the most radical popular initiatives.

## Combined Key Terms

**From Dower (Japan):**
- **"Neocolonial Revolution"** (Dower, Ch. 6, ~pp. 203-224): [paraphrased] Dower's paradox for occupied Japan — genuine social transformation (labor organizing, democratic mobilization, cultural opening) occurring within the framework of American military occupation. The revolution was real; its context was imperial. Most concentrated expression of the Week 5 comparative axis.
- **"Embracing Defeat"** (Dower, Introduction, ~pp. 20-30): [paraphrased] Dower's title concept — Japanese society actively shaped the occupation's transformations rather than passively submitting. Centers Japanese agency; contrasts with the US flyer's framing of Koreans as passive recipients of managed liberation.
- **Indirect rule** (Dower; SWNCC 150): MacArthur's occupation strategy of governing through the Emperor and existing Japanese institutions. Contrasts with Korea's direct rule (SWNCC 176). Enables the managed democratic transformation Dower analyzes; the Emperor's preserved authority shaped what democratic reforms were possible.
- **Labor movement (occupied Japan)** (Dower, Ch. 6): [paraphrased] Rapid organization of Japanese workers in 1945-46 — unions across sectors, drawing on socialist, communist, and liberal currents. Initially enabled by SCAP; suppressed from 1947. The "reverse course" turning point.
- **"Reverse course"** (Dower): The shift in US occupation policy beginning ~1947, when Cold War containment logic superseded democratic reform — labor suppressed, zaibatsu dissolution slowed, conservative forces strengthened. Anticipated in Korea from 1945 itself.

**From Chang (Korea):**
- **"Independence without liberation"** (Chang, p. 81): Chang's thesis — formal independence (sovereignty, elections) achieved without liberation from colonial economic structures, political collaborators, or social hierarchies. Korea's defining outcome; also a structural description of American postwar empire more broadly.
- **Decolonization management** (Chang, p. 81): Chang's term for US occupation strategy — active management of Korea's transition from Japanese colonialism to ensure Cold War-compatible outcomes, rather than neutral facilitation of Korean self-determination.
- **Korean People's Republic (KPR)** (Chang; SWNCC 176): Grassroots Korean government formed September 1945 under Lyuh Woon-hyung, with people's committees across the country. Dissolved by US Military Government under SWNCC 176 Section 5g. Represents what liberation would have looked like if genuinely pursued.
- **"Semblance of sovereignty"** (Chang, p. 80): Elections gave Korea the procedural form of self-determination without popular control or democratic institutions rooted in genuine popular movements.
- **Autumn Strikes (1946)** (Chang, pp. 90-93): General strike of ~300,000 Korean workers, violently suppressed by US forces. Climax of the labor movement Chang documents. Failure of labor emancipation promised by the occupation's democratization rhetoric.
- **Korean National Police (KNP)** (Chang, p. 86): Colonial police apparatus revived by the US occupation, staffed by collaborators who served Japanese colonialism. Richard Robinson: "the police did more to ruin the reputation of the United States in Korea" (p. 86).
- **"Fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy"** (Roger Baldwin, quoted Chang p. 87): ACLU founder's observation — anti-communist logic was used to suppress the KPR and Korean labor movement, both democratic popular movements rather than Soviet instruments.

**From JCS 1067 (Germany):**
- **Punitive occupation** (JCS 1067): Occupation premised on punishment and prevention of future aggression — canonical example is Germany under JCS 1067. Contrast with reformist occupation (Japan under SWNCC 150).
- **Morgenthau Plan logic** (JCS 1067, Section 16): Prohibition on economic rehabilitation of Germany; reversed by Marshall Plan. Represents the punitive peak before Cold War strategic interest redirected policy.
- **Denazification** (JCS 1067, Sections 6a-6e): Systematic removal of Nazi Party members from public office and professions. More comprehensive than any comparable Japan program; eventually selective as Cold War priorities shifted.
- **"Defeated enemy nation"** (JCS 1067, p. 1): The governing premise — occupation as consequence of defeat, not liberation. Direct contrast with SWNCC 150 and SWNCC 176's liberationist framing.

**From SWNCC 150 (Japan):**
- **Reformist occupation** (SWNCC 150): Occupation premised on democratization, structural reform, and popular legitimacy — the Japan model. "Foster conditions" language of popular-driven change.
- **Zaibatsu dissolution** (SWNCC 150, Part IV): Breaking up large Japanese industrial and banking conglomerates as part of economic democratization. Partially reversed during the "reverse course" as Cold War priorities shifted to Japanese industrial recovery.
- **"Promotion of Democratic Forces"** (SWNCC 150, Part IV): Encouragement of labor, agricultural, and industrial organizations on a democratic basis. The policy whose effects Dower documents in Ch. 6.

**From SWNCC 176 (Korea):**
- **Section 5g** (SWNCC 176): Prohibition on recognizing "any self-styled Korean provisional government or similar political organizations." Chang's key analytical target — dissolves KPR, enables support for rightists, embodies "independence without liberation" in a single operative clause.
- **Direct vs. indirect rule** (SWNCC 176 vs. SWNCC 150): SWNCC 176 established direct US Military Government in Korea; SWNCC 150 governed Japan through the Emperor. Same occupier, opposite governance strategies — reveals how context shaped institutional choices.

**From Flyers (Korea):**
- **Paternalism** (US Hodge flyer): Treating Koreans as requiring external guidance toward democracy — "hasty and ill-advised acts" as the thing to avoid. Connects to Hodge's private description of Koreans as "childish" (Chang, p. 90) and to Kramer's imperial racial grammar.
- **Compliance vs. agency**: Central rhetorical contrast — US: comply to earn democracy; Soviet: be the creator of your own happiness. Neither framing was honest about the limits of occupation, but they encoded different political logics for Korean popular action.
- **Proclamation No. 2** (MacArthur, Sept. 7, 1945): Threatened death penalty for violations of occupation authority, issued one week after Hodge's cautionary paternalism. Revealed the coercive foundation beneath liberationist rhetoric.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: "Neocolonial Revolution" / Section 5g / "Decolonization Management"**
*(Dower + SWNCC 176 + Chang — within Week 5)*

Dower's "neocolonial revolution" and Chang's "decolonization management" describe the same US project — managing popular democratic mobilization in formerly Japanese-controlled territories — from opposite vantage points. In Japan, SCAP used indirect rule through the Emperor to channel democratic energies, producing Dower's paradox of genuine revolution within an imperial framework. In Korea, SWNCC 176 Section 5g dissolved the Korean People's Republic and channeled political authority toward rightist collaborators instead. The difference between Japan and Korea was not the US goal (Cold War-compatible outcome) but the institutional vehicle chosen: an indirect-ruled Emperor-mediated democracy in Japan; direct rule and KPR dissolution in Korea.

**Set 2: JCS 1067 / SWNCC 150 / "Defeated Enemy Nation"**
*(JCS 1067 + SWNCC 150 + within Week 5)*

JCS 1067 declared Germany would be occupied "as a defeated enemy nation" — prohibition on economic rehabilitation, denazification as punishment. SWNCC 150 declared it was "not the responsibility of the Allied Powers to impose upon Japan any form of government not supported by the freely expressed will of the people" — reformist, popular-legitimacy framing. The same power, the same year, opposite premises. Race, European alliance politics demanding punitive Germany, and the strategic calculation that an Emperor-mediated Japan was more useful than a destroyed one explain the gap. The comparison reveals that "punitive vs. reformist" was never primarily a moral choice — it was a strategic calculation shaped by who the enemy was and what postwar role was envisioned for them.

**Set 3: "Fear of Democracy" / Platt Amendment / KPR**
*(Chang p. 87 + Platt Amendment W2PS + SWNCC 176 — Weeks 2 and 5)*

Roger Baldwin's phrase — "fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy" — names the same political logic that produced the Platt Amendment in 1901. In both cases, the US used the language of order and legitimacy to suppress popular democratic movements: Cuba's mobilization was managed through the Platt protectorate's intervention clause; Korea's KPR was dissolved through SWNCC 176 Section 5g. Chang's "decolonization management" is the 1945 version of what Week 2 called "sovereignty in name, protectorate in practice." The Platt Amendment is the 1901 Section 5g — a mechanism for giving independence the form while retaining its substance, framed as protection against instability.

**Set 4: Paternalism / "Hasty Acts" / Mabini**
*(US Hodge flyer + Chang + Mabini W3PS — Weeks 3 and 5)*

The US occupation flyer's warning against "hasty and ill-advised acts" encodes the same paternalist logic Mabini confronted in the Philippines after 1899. Mabini argued from natural law that the question of when a people was "ready" for self-government was unanswerable within colonial logic — sovereignty resides inherently in the people themselves and cannot be withheld by a conqueror pending maturation. Hodge's promise that compliance would "speed the day when Koreans may once again enjoy life under a more democratic rule" is the same temporal deferral: democracy as future reward for present obedience, the occupier controlling both the terms and the timetable. Constantino showed this deferral function in Philippine colonial education; the Hodge flyer performs it in mass-distributed rhetoric targeted at Korean workers and citizens.

**Set 5: "Dangerous Symmetries" / US/Soviet Flyers / Atlantic Charter**
*(Fradera W1RR + Flyers + Atlantic Charter W4PS — Weeks 1, 4, and 5)*

Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" — rival imperial formations using opposed rhetoric while sharing structural logic — is starkly visible in the competing Korea flyers. The Soviet flyer celebrated Korean liberation; the US flyer warned against disorder. Yet both powers displaced Korean political agency: the Soviet north was also building a client state, supporting Kim Il-sung over other Korean leaders. The Atlantic Charter's self-determination promise was invoked by neither flyer honestly — the US was dissolving the KPR while invoking UN Charter principles in SWNCC 176 Section 3; the Soviets celebrated liberation while imposing external political structures. The rhetorical asymmetry between the two flyers conceals the structural symmetry Fradera's framework identifies: two imperial formations using opposite registers to achieve the same outcome.

## Essay Questions

1. Dower calls occupied Japan a "neocolonial revolution" and Chang calls occupied Korea "independence without liberation." Using these two secondary sources alongside the three 1945 occupation directives (JCS 1067, SWNCC 150, SWNCC 176) and the US/Soviet flyers, compare how the United States managed democratic popular mobilization across its three simultaneous occupations. What explains the different strategies applied to Japan, Korea, and Germany? What were the consequences — for occupied peoples and for American occupation ideology — of these strategic divergences?

2. "Fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy" (Roger Baldwin, quoted in Chang, p. 87). Using Chang's "Independence without Liberation," SWNCC 176, the US/Soviet occupation flyers, and at least one reading from Weeks 1–4 (Fradera, Kramer, Mabini, or Beveridge), assess this claim. Was the US suppression of Korean democratic mobilization a coherent anti-communist strategy, a continuation of older patterns of American empire-building, or both? What does your answer reveal about the relationship between anti-communism and American imperial ideology across the course's case studies?

3. Merritt (Week 4) observed that JCS 1067 was "virtually a dead letter within months," and Chang shows a comparable gap between SWNCC 176's stated liberationist objectives and its operative effects. Using the three 1945 occupation directives alongside Dower and Chang, assess the relationship between occupation planning documents and occupation outcomes. Do the directives' differences explain the divergent outcomes in Germany, Japan, and Korea — or did on-the-ground institutional choices (indirect vs. direct rule, which local actors to support, how to handle labor mobilization) matter more than the directives themselves?
