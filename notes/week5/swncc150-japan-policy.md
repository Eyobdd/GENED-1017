## Summary

SWNCC 150/4, approved by President Truman on 6 September 1945, is the foundational American occupation directive for Japan. Issued just days after Japan's formal surrender, it establishes the guiding principles and objectives for the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers (SCAP, General MacArthur). Read alongside JCS 1067 (Germany), SWNCC 150 reveals how dramatically different the US conceived of its two major occupation projects — and why.

The document's most revealing passage is in Part I, Ultimate Objectives: "it is not the responsibility of the Allied Powers to impose upon Japan any form of government not supported by the freely expressed will of the people." This is reformist, even liberal language — a commitment to popular legitimacy rather than imposed transformation. Compare JCS 1067's opening: Germany is occupied "as a defeated enemy nation," not for liberation. The same power, the same moment, and an entirely different political philosophy applied to the two former enemies.

Part II, on Allied Authority, explains the operational approach: the US would "exercise its authority through Japanese governmental machinery, including the Emperor, to the extent that such an arrangement produces satisfactory results." This is indirect rule — using existing Japanese institutions, including the Emperor, rather than dismantling them. The decision to retain the Emperor was strategically calculated: MacArthur and policy planners believed imperial authority would make Japanese compliance more reliable. Chang's analysis of Korea (W5) shows the parallel logic — the US dissolved the Korean People's Republic and worked through rightist collaborators rather than revolutionary popular forces.

Part III (Political Objectives) includes both punitive and reformist elements: disarmament, punishment of war criminals, and abolition of militarist organizations, but also "Encouragement of Desire for Individual Liberties and Democratic Processes." This phrase encapsulates the reformist occupation vision — democratization as aspiration and program, not just byproduct.

Part IV (Economic Objectives) is similarly dual-track: destroy the economic basis of Japan's military strength (punitive), but also pursue the "Promotion of Democratic Forces" through encouragement of labor, agricultural, and industrial organizations "on a democratic basis." Most consequentially, it calls for dissolution of large industrial and banking combinations — the zaibatsu — that had fueled Japan's war economy. This zaibatsu dissolution program is the economic analog of denazification in Germany, but conceived within a broader democratization frame.

Dower's Embracing Defeat (W5, Ch. 6) provides the Japanese perspective on these policies — showing how Japanese citizens, workers, and political actors responded to and shaped the occupation's democratic initiatives. The gap between SWNCC 150's reformist intent and actual occupation practice (particularly the "reverse course" beginning in 1947) is a central thread of the course.

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **SWNCC 150/4**: The State-War-Navy Coordinating Committee document 150, fourth revision, approved by Truman September 6, 1945. The foundational policy document for the US occupation of Japan; supersedes earlier planning documents.
- **Indirect rule**: MacArthur's occupation strategy of governing Japan through existing Japanese governmental machinery, including the Emperor, rather than establishing direct military administration. Contrasts with Korea, where the US Military Government displaced existing Korean political structures (including the KPR).
- **Zaibatsu dissolution**: The SWNCC 150 policy of breaking up large Japanese industrial and banking conglomerates (zaibatsu) that had funded Japan's war economy. Part of the economic democratization program; partially reversed during the "reverse course."
- **Ultimate Objectives (Part I)**: SWNCC 150's statement of Japan policy goals — peaceful, responsible government; rights of others; self-determination. Sets the tone for reformist occupation.
- **Reformist occupation**: Occupation premised on democratization, structural reform, and popular legitimacy — the Japan model under SWNCC 150, contrasted with the punitive Germany model under JCS 1067.
- **"Promotion of Democratic Forces"**: SWNCC 150 Part IV language encouraging labor, agricultural, and industrial organizations on democratic basis. Represents occupation's social democratization agenda; connects to Dower's analysis of labor politics in Ch. 6.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to JCS 1067 (Germany directive, W5)**: The direct comparative contrast. Same occupation moment, same power, same ultimate stated goals (peaceful, democratic, non-threatening), but JCS 1067 prohibits economic rehabilitation and frames occupation as punishment, while SWNCC 150 frames occupation as democratic transformation through popular will. Explains why punitive-vs.-reformist is a central course debate.

- **Connects to Dower, Embracing Defeat Ch. 6 (W5)**: SWNCC 150 is the policy; Dower's Ch. 6 "Neocolonial Revolution" is the on-the-ground reality. Dower examines how Japanese workers, women, and political activists tried to realize the democratic promises of SWNCC 150 — and how SCAP both enabled and constrained those energies. The gap between SWNCC 150's reformist text and occupation practice is Dower's central subject.

- **Connects to Chang, "Independence without Liberation" (W5)**: Chang's Korea analysis shows what happened when the US applied a different framework — dissolving the Korean People's Republic, supporting rightists, and suppressing labor. SWNCC 176 (Korea) uses similar reformist language to SWNCC 150 (Japan), but implementation diverged sharply. Together these three documents (SWNCC 150, SWNCC 176, Chang) reveal how policy language could be consistent while practice varied by Cold War calculation.

- **Connects to Reischauer, "Memorandum on Policy Towards Japan" (W4)**: Reischauer's 1942 memorandum anticipated many SWNCC 150 arguments — retain emperor, work through Japanese institutions, focus on democratization. SWNCC 150 can be read as the institutional realization of the Reischauer approach, making it a window into the intellectual genealogy of occupation policy.

- **Connects to SWNCC 176 (Korea directive, W5)**: Both documents use liberation and democratization language. But SWNCC 176 adds the crucial Section 5g prohibition on recognizing any "self-styled Korean provisional government" — a restriction that reveals how self-determination was applied differently to Japan (where indirect rule preserved existing institutions) and Korea (where existing political structures were dissolved).

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*SWNCC 150 / JCS 1067 / Punitive vs. reformist*
SWNCC 150 and JCS 1067 represent the twin poles of American occupation policy in 1945. Germany was occupied "as a defeated enemy nation" with economic rehabilitation prohibited; Japan's policy declared it was "not the responsibility of the Allied Powers to impose" any form of government and called for promoting democratic forces. The contrast asks why the same power applied such different frameworks — and implicates race, allies, and strategic calculation.

*SWNCC 150 / Chang / Zaibatsu dissolution*
SWNCC 150 called for dissolving Japan's zaibatsu industrial combinations as part of economic democratization. Chang shows that in Korea, comparable economic restructuring never happened — US occupation supported rightist landowners and revived colonial economic structures. The comparison reveals how the reformist promise of SWNCC 150 was selectively applied, with Korea receiving the security logic but not the democratic economic program.

*SWNCC 150 / Dower / Emperor*
SWNCC 150's decision to govern through the Emperor — "to the extent that such an arrangement produces satisfactory results" — reflects indirect rule as strategic calculation. Dower's Embracing Defeat explores the consequences: the Emperor's retained legitimacy shaped what kinds of democratic reform were possible and what kinds were off-limits. The decision to keep Hirohito was perhaps SWNCC 150's most consequential operational choice.
