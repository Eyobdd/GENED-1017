## Summary

JCS 1067, issued in April 1945 to General Eisenhower as Supreme Commander of Allied Expeditionary Forces, is the foundational American occupation directive for Germany. It establishes the governing framework for the US zone and reveals the fundamental tension in American occupation policy between punitive logic and long-term reform goals.

The document opens with an unambiguous declaration of purpose: "Germany will not be occupied for the purpose of liberation but as a defeated enemy nation." This formulation is deliberate and consequential — it directly inverts the liberationist language that would saturate the parallel policy for Japan (SWNCC 150) and Korea (SWNCC 176). Where those documents invoke democratic aspiration and self-determination, JCS 1067 frames occupation as consequence and punishment.

The directive's four basic objectives are: eliminating Nazism and militarism, punishing war criminals, industrial disarmament and prevention of future war production, and eventual democratic reconstruction. The sequencing matters — democracy appears last, preceded by three punitive aims. The economic clauses are especially striking: Section 16 explicitly prohibits Eisenhower from taking "any steps (a) looking toward the economic rehabilitation of Germany, or (b) designed to maintain or strengthen the German economy." This is the Morgenthau Plan logic in operational form — Germany must be held down, not rebuilt.

The denazification provisions (Sections 6a–6e) require the arrest of Nazi Party members, removal from public office, and prohibition of German-controlled media and education. The demilitarization section (Section 7) goes further: dismantling the Wehrmacht, banning military training, and eliminating Germany's war-making industrial base. Together these sections reflect a vision of occupation as surgical removal — excising the malignant structures of the Nazi state while keeping Germany functional enough not to collapse entirely.

The document sits at the center of a major course debate: why was US Germany policy so much more punitive than US Japan policy? Several explanations emerge from the broader course. First, racial ideology — Germany was not racialized as inherently inferior but rather as having been corrupted by Nazism, meaning the punitive measures targeted the political system rather than the people. Japan policy, paradoxically, combined racial condescension (Dower, Embracing Defeat) with reformist optimism. Second, European allies — Britain, France, and the Soviet Union all had territorial stakes and demanded punitive terms. Third, the Morgenthau Plan (Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr.) had strong influence in 1944–45 before being partially reversed. The reversal would come with NSC 68 and the Marshall Plan — but JCS 1067, as written, represents the punitive peak.

For the midterm, JCS 1067 is essential for the punitive-vs.-reformist comparison and for understanding how Cold War logic eventually reversed course (Gillingham, W7).

## Key Terms & Concepts

- **Denazification**: The Allied program to remove and exclude Nazi Party members from public life, government, and the professions. JCS 1067 provides the legal and operational framework. Implemented inconsistently in practice; more systematic in US zone than Soviet zone initially, then reversed.
- **Morgenthau Plan**: Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr.'s 1944 proposal to deindustrialize Germany and convert it to an agricultural economy, preventing future war production. JCS 1067 embeds Morgenthau logic in Section 16's prohibition on economic rehabilitation.
- **Punitive occupation**: Occupation premised on punishment, containment, and prevention of future aggression — contrasted with "reformist occupation" (Japan, Korea) premised on democratization and reconstruction. JCS 1067 is the canonical punitive document.
- **Fraternization prohibition**: JCS 1067's directive discouraging social contact between US troops and German civilians — reflects the "defeated enemy" framing and distinguishes Germany from the liberation-narrative occupations.
- **Demilitarization**: Systematic dismantling of military capacity — Wehrmacht, war industries, military training. A shared objective across Germany, Japan, and Korea directives, but implemented most punitively in Germany under JCS 1067.
- **Allied Control Council**: The four-power (US, UK, France, USSR) governing body for occupied Germany. JCS 1067 authorizes Eisenhower to coordinate within this structure while maintaining US zone authority.

## Cross-Reading Connections

- **Connects to SWNCC 150 (Japan policy)**: The direct counterpart — same occupation moment, same power, opposite framing. SWNCC 150 says "it is not the responsibility of the Allied Powers to impose upon Japan any form of government not supported by the freely expressed will of the people." JCS 1067 says Germany is occupied "as a defeated enemy nation," not for liberation. The contrast illuminates how racial ideology, alliance politics, and strategic interest shaped divergent policies.

- **Connects to Gillingham (W7, "From Morgenthau Plan to Schuman Plan")**: Gillingham traces the reversal of JCS 1067 logic — the shift from punitive deindustrialization to economic integration under the Marshall Plan and Schuman Plan. JCS 1067 represents the policy that had to be overcome for West Germany to become a Cold War ally.

- **Connects to Atlantic Charter (W4)**: The Atlantic Charter proclaimed self-determination and the right of peoples to choose their own form of government. JCS 1067 applies to a population that fought against the Allied powers — revealing the limits of universalist self-determination rhetoric when applied to defeated enemies. Compare with Korea (SWNCC 176), where the rhetoric of liberation appears despite US refusal to recognize the Korean Provisional Government.

- **Connects to Merritt (W4, "American Influences in Occupation of Germany")**: Merritt examines how American cultural and political influence penetrated German society despite JCS 1067's formal restrictions. The gap between directive and implementation is a recurring course theme.

- **Connects to Chang (W5, "Independence without Liberation")**: Both documents reveal the gap between stated occupation aims and actual policy logic. Chang shows how the US "wanted to have their Atlantic Charter and retreat from it too" in Korea; JCS 1067 shows no pretense of Atlantic Charter principles was ever extended to Germany. The contrast — no liberation rhetoric for Germany vs. liberation rhetoric without liberation for Korea — is analytically productive.

## Practice Questions

**3-Word Connection Drills (Part 1 practice):**

*JCS 1067 / SWNCC 150 / Racial ideology*
The contrast between JCS 1067's punitive Germany directive and SWNCC 150's reformist Japan policy cannot be explained by ideology alone. Both targeted enemies; both sought democratization eventually. But SWNCC 150 explicitly deferred to Japanese popular will while JCS 1067 prohibited economic rehabilitation — reflecting how race and alliance politics shaped which enemies deserved transformation versus punishment.

*JCS 1067 / Atlantic Charter / Self-determination*
The Atlantic Charter proclaimed the right of all peoples to choose their government. JCS 1067, issued four years later, explicitly frames the US as occupying Germany "as a defeated enemy nation," not as liberator. The contradiction reveals how self-determination was always conditional — applied rhetorically to colonial subjects and allied peoples, withheld from defeated enemies, and selectively honored even where invoked.

*JCS 1067 / Gillingham / Morgenthau Plan*
JCS 1067 embedded Morgenthau Plan logic in operational policy: Section 16 prohibited economic rehabilitation of Germany. Gillingham shows this logic was rapidly reversed — the Marshall Plan and Schuman Plan transformed West Germany from punished enemy to Cold War industrial partner. The reversal illustrates how strategic interest, not principled occupation theory, drove American policy.
