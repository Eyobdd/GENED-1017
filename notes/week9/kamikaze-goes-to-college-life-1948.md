# "Kamikaze Goes to College"
**Author:** *Life* Magazine (with introductory commentary drawing on Naoko Shibusawa, *America's Geisha Ally*, 2006) | **Week:** 9 | **Type:** PS
**Case Study:** Japan
**Pages:** pp. 124–8 (*Life*, October 4, 1948)

## Summary

This five-page *Life* photo-essay documents Robert Yukimasa Nishiyama — a former kamikaze pilot who never flew his mission — arriving at Lafayette College in Pennsylvania in fall 1948 as the first Japanese student to attend an American university after WWII. He came on the Johnstone Scholarship, funded by the $10,000 government life insurance of a U.S. soldier killed in the Philippines who (according to the article's framing) wanted his "enemies" taught the American way of life.

The article's significance is less what it describes than what it performs. It is a piece of deliberate cultural work: the narrative arc — enemy-turned-freshman, grateful and polite, welcomed by the dead soldier's mother — enacts the transformation of the Japanese from savage threat to manageable dependent. Nishiyama is portrayed as embarrassed by unfamiliar tests, deferential to upperclassmen, wanting to teach international relations when he returns home. He does not challenge; he absorbs. Shibusawa (whose commentary frames the document) argues these scholarships functioned less to spread pro-American sentiment in Japan and more to help *Americans* accept the Japanese as "junior allies" — to work through their own racial hostility by encountering an unthreatening, assimilable Japanese body on campus.

The source connects directly to course Theme 7 (education and cultural transformation) and Theme 5 (race, civilization, and imperial ideology). Re-education in Japan was not only about rewriting textbooks or purging militarists — it was also about producing *legible* subjects: Japanese people who could be absorbed into an American-led postwar order. Nishiyama's role is to be exemplary, to make reconciliation visible. His roommate is an ex-Marine studying for the ministry. The symbolism is heavy-handed: former enemies, now brothers in Christ and democracy.

This also speaks to the HOW question — how does occupation end? Not only through institutional change (constitutions, land reform) but through the cultivation of affect: American audiences must *feel* that the Japanese are now safe, teachable, worthy of benevolence. The occupation's legitimacy depends on this emotional work as much as on policy.

## Key Terms

- **Kamikaze Special Attacks Corps**: Japanese suicide-pilot unit; in this article, serves as the most extreme possible marker of wartime enmity — Nishiyama's former identity is the article's entire hook
- **Johnstone Scholarship**: Fellowship funded by a dead GI's insurance, established 1945 for a Japanese student at Lafayette College; origin story is partly mythologized (Shibusawa corrects the "dying wish" framing)
- **Re-education (cultural)**: The occupation goal of reforming Japanese values, attitudes, and self-understanding; here extended beyond formal schooling into the symbolic arena of campus life and American public opinion
- **"Junior allies"**: Shibusawa's term for the postwar reframing of Japan — no longer enemy, not yet equal, but a subordinate partner in the U.S.-led Cold War Pacific order
- **Grateful pupil**: The acceptable postwar Japanese subject position — assimilated, appreciative, non-threatening; contrasted implicitly with resistant or proud Japanese identity
- **Reverse course (context)**: By 1948, U.S. occupation policy had shifted from democratization/demilitarization toward rebuilding Japan as a stable anti-communist ally; this article's soft diplomacy is consistent with that pivot
- **"Indoctrination program"**: Word used neutrally in the *Life* article for freshman orientation — inadvertently reveals how thoroughly Americanization was naturalized as education
