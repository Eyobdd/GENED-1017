# "The American School" (*Amerikan sukūru*)
**Author:** Kojima Nobuo (trans. William F. Sibley) | **Week:** 9 | **Type:** PS (Short Story/Fiction)
**Case Study:** Japan
**Pages:** 120–144 (in *Contemporary Japanese Literature*, ed. Howard Hibbett, Knopf, 1977)
**Original publication:** 1954

---

## Summary

Written just a few years after the Allied Occupation ended (1952), Kojima's satirical short story follows a group of thirty Japanese English teachers on a mandatory excursion to an American school. Three figures anchor the story's exploration of how occupied people internalize, resist, and adapt to imperial power:

**Isa** — an English teacher who cannot speak English. His paralysis is total: he has faked illness to avoid interpreters' duties, fled into the woods, and now endures the eight-mile march in borrowed shoes that shred his feet. His silence is not laziness but a form of psychological self-preservation. Speaking the occupier's language, he believes, would make him a foreigner in his own country — a real disgrace. He represents the deepest form of passive resistance, the refusal to perform competence for the occupier's benefit.

**Yamada** — aggressively collaborative, self-promoting, militaristic. He bullies Isa, solicits American connections for his study-abroad ambitions, and performs enthusiasm for English while privately deriding it. He is the most dangerous figure in the story: he mimics the occupiers' authority structure while angling to benefit from it, and his wartime boastfulness (beheading prisoners) sits barely beneath the surface of his "democratic" posturing.

**Michiko** — a war widow whose pragmatism is neither collaboration nor resistance. She actually speaks fluent, natural English; she accepts chocolate from soldiers and navigates encounters with Americans without losing herself. She is the most psychologically intact figure, and the story's most ambiguous: her English fluency grants her real advantages but also exposes her to Yamada's contempt and Isa's confused attraction.

The story's central irony: Japanese *English teachers* cannot speak English. The occupation's cultural project — transforming Japan through language, education, and American institutional models — has produced a corps of instructors whose relationship to English is one of shame, terror, and resentment rather than competence. The American school itself, gleaming and material-rich, functions as a monument to Japanese defeat: they are admitted only as observers, never as participants. The climax — Mr. Williams banning Japanese instructors from ever teaching at the school and forbidding high heels on the premises — reveals occupation authority as arbitrary, petty, and total.

**So what?** The story is not just about Japan; it is a precise anatomy of the psychology of occupation. It asks: what does it do to a person to be required to perform fluency in the language of power while knowing that performing it confirms their subordination? Kojima's answer — that different people manage this through collaboration, adaptation, or silence — maps exactly onto the range of responses to occupation that the course's primary sources document elsewhere.

- Central argument: Occupation's "educational" and "democratic" reforms produce not transformation but a layered psychology of performance, shame, and resistance
- Relates to: WHAT did Americans do? / HOW did occupied peoples respond?

## Key Terms

- **Performed competence**: The occupation demands that Japanese teachers *demonstrate* English fluency to American observers — not for their students, but for the occupiers' evaluation and ranking. The story exposes this as theater that serves American authority, not Japanese education.

- **Passive resistance (Isa's silence)**: Isa's refusal to speak English is not simple failure but a psychologically coherent strategy — he will not give the occupation what it demands. Compare to other forms of noncooperation documented across the course (e.g., Korean resistance to American directives).

- **Opportunistic collaboration (Yamada)**: Yamada seeks to leverage the occupation's institutional structures for personal advancement (study abroad, connections). He mimics American administrative authority while disdaining it privately. This is distinct from genuine embrace of occupation goals.

- **Pragmatic adaptation (Michiko)**: She speaks real English, accepts material benefits from soldiers, navigates without either Isa's paralysis or Yamada's aggression. Her strategy is presented most sympathetically but is also the most ideologically ambiguous.

- **The "kamikaze spirit" misreading**: Williams's use of the term reveals the occupier's fundamental inability to read Japan except through the lens of the war. The occupation frames all Japanese behavior through wartime categories — exactly what Dower calls the "embrace" of defeat that requires the defeated to perform their transformation for the victors.

- **Material asymmetry**: The American school — built with Japanese funds but staffed with American teachers earning ten times Japanese wages — embodies the structural inequality of occupation. Japanese teachers are admitted as visitors to observe what their own resources built.
