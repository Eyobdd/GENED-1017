**Author:** Won Moo Hurh | **Week:** 9 | **Type:** PS (Memoir)
**Case Study:** Korea
**Pages:** 20–54 (book pages; chapters 3–4 of McFarland, 2012)

## Summary

Hurh's memoir gives a Korean civilian's-eye view of *three successive occupations*: Japanese colonialism, the American post-WWII occupation, and the North Korean invasion of 1950. Written retrospectively by a sociologist who eventually emigrated to the U.S., the text is analytically self-aware without losing its immediacy as lived testimony.

**Chapter 3 (pp. 20–34): Growing Up Under Japanese Rule**

Hurh's childhood in Seoul during the late 1930s–1945 was shaped entirely by Japanese colonial policy. Schools operated in Japanese only; Korean history and language were banned. In 1939 the colonial government issued forced name-change decrees (*soshi-kaimei*): Hurh's family became "Moriyama," a psychic rupture the author marks as a central wound of colonialism. Children were mobilized for war labor (collecting brass, sewing uniforms), indoctrinated against the Allied powers, and compelled to worship at Shinto shrines. Yet Hurh simultaneously absorbed Japanese literature — Natsume Soseki, translated European classics — illustrating the complexity of colonial cultural transfer: the colonizer's language became the vehicle for Hurh's intellectual formation even as it erased his ethnic identity.

Japan's surrender on August 15, 1945 brought jubilation but also instant structural vulnerability. The U.S. and USSR, without Korean input, divided the peninsula at the 38th parallel. Hurh's reaction to the first American soldiers — "They all looked alike, except for skin differences — black and white" and "I thought Americans were 'a superior race'" — captures the colonial optics through which U.S. power arrived: liberators who immediately reproduced an occupying structure.

**Chapter 4 (pp. 35–54): The Korean War and Shattered Life Course**

The war's outbreak on June 25, 1950 destroyed Hurh's planned trajectory (law school at Seoul National University). He hid from North Korean Army recruiters in a hole beneath his living-room floor for months, witnessing impromptu "People's Courts" in the street below. The text is relentlessly concrete about what occupation means at the civilian level: corpses rotting under bridges, families riding freight-car rooftops in winter, morphine bought on black markets, 2.2 million crossing the frozen Han River as refugees.

Hurh's decision to join the ROK Artillery Officer Candidate School is presented not as patriotism but as practical calculation — artillery is safer than infantry, and officer status exempts him from conscription as a private. His ten-day refugee walk to Daegu in January 1951, following railroad tracks through tunnels and abandoned farmhouses, gives the most analytically rich moment: he explicitly questions the "patriotism, national unification, and democracy" invoked by leaders who did not send their own children to fight.

**The "so what":** This source places the Korean experience at the center of a narrative usually told from the perspective of American planners. It demonstrates that occupied peoples were shaped by *accumulated* colonial encounters (Japan → U.S./USSR → North Korea), none of which gave Koreans agency over their own political fate. It directly complicates the course's Cold War framework: from the Korean civilian perspective, the 38th parallel division was simply the latest in a series of great-power arrangements in which Korea was "a pawn in the great chess match."

## Key Terms

- **Soshi-kaimei**: Japanese colonial decree forcing Koreans to adopt Japanese names; Hurh frames it as the central act of identity erasure under colonialism — not just cultural but juridical
- **Haebang (liberation)**: Korean term for the August 15, 1945 liberation from Japan; Hurh's account complicates "liberation" since the U.S. and USSR immediately imposed a division that denied Korean self-determination
- **38th parallel**: The administrative division imposed by the U.S. and USSR in 1945, without Korean consent, which became the border between the ROK and DPRK; the structural cause of the Korean War
- **Pusan Perimeter**: The last-stand defensive line in southeastern Korea (summer 1950) held by ROK and U.S. forces; Hurh walks through its aftermath, literalizing what Cold War geopolitics meant on the ground
- **ROK Artillery Officer Candidate School**: Hurh's path into the war; his rationale (safety, mental challenge) undercuts glorified narratives of military service — occupation produces soldiers through calculation, not ideology
- **"People's Courts"**: Impromptu North Korean street tribunals held during the occupation of Seoul; Hurh witnesses a summary execution, connecting occupation with juridical terror
- **Cold War reversal**: While the course uses this term for Japan's "reverse course," Korea is the site where Cold War logic was *most violent* — the division precedes the reversal and produces the war
- **Accumulated occupations**: Hurh's childhood spans Japanese colonialism, American military government, and North Korean invasion — three distinct occupying powers in one decade, a density that makes Korea the course's sharpest case for examining occupation as a structural condition
