## How This Week's Readings Speak to Each Other

Week 9 is the course's cultural turn. Where Weeks 5–7 examined the institutional architecture of occupation (directives, constitutions, land reform), Week 9 asks what occupation *felt like* — and what it left behind in the register of identity, performance, and everyday encounter. All six readings concern the cultural dimension of occupation: how people performed compliance, learned languages, heard music, cleaned houses, and crossed racial lines under conditions of unequal power. The institutional machinery of occupation (SWNCC 150, Article 24, the Dodge Line) recedes; the maid's kitchen, the college campus, and the concert hall come forward.

The week's central tension is between two readings of how occupation's cultural project worked. Jarausch's "Embracing the West" offers the relatively optimistic account: Westernization was three-layered (personal encounters, political bonding, popular Americanization) and largely *chosen* by West Germans through transnational acculturation rather than dictated from above. Germans who "never had to work before" hired maids, listened to jazz, wore blue jeans, and eventually voted for Westbindung because these choices served their interests and appetites, not because Allied occupation authorities commanded them. But Jarausch also warns: the "star-pupil syndrome" — unconditional embrace masking opportunism and evasion of guilt — is the pathological version of the same process.

Kojima's "The American School" is the counter-weight. Written from Japan just two years after formal occupation ended, it is a precise anatomy of what the cultural project looked like *from below*: English teachers who cannot speak English, a gleaming American school built with Japanese funds that Japanese instructors are admitted to observe but never participate in, and an occupation authority so culturally illiterate it misreads Japanese resolve as "the old kamikaze spirit." Where Jarausch's voluntary Westernization looks like genuine acculturation, Kojima's occupied Japanese carry the psychological weight of a demanded performance: they must display competence in the occupier's language while knowing the display confirms their subordination. Isa's silence — "It is foolish for Japanese to speak this language like foreigners. If they do, it makes them foreigners, too" — is what Westernization looks like when the occupied refuse to *be* absorbed.

The Callam et al. Marian Anderson article occupies the space between these two readings: it shows how occupation's cultural machinery (GHQ-reformed NHK, US Embassy coordination, imperial family ceremonies) continued operating *after* formal occupation ended, now as Cold War soft power. Anderson was deployed to demonstrate that American democracy was racially inclusive — even as she remained barred from the Metropolitan Opera. "Performing democracy" names the mechanism: using the performance of a Black artist as evidence of a democracy that still did not include her. The Japan-Korea contrast within the article maps directly onto the course's comparative axis: in post-occupation Japan, the apparatus was elaborate and ceremonially saturated (imperial palace audiences, NHK broadcasts, Embassy receptions); in still-occupied Korea, Anderson encountered rain delays, failed microphones, and 100,000 bodies surrounding her car. Cultural diplomacy is occupation by other means, and it arrives at different phases.

Jaima's "Black Wives, White Maids" runs a precisely analogous double inversion in Germany. Black American military wives were sent abroad to *restore* racial order by reducing Black GI relationships with German women; instead, their daily intimacy with white German maids — who served in their households, dined at their tables, and sometimes became their friends — quietly turned "race relations on their head." The same Cold War military structure that reproduced American racial hierarchy (segregated units, racialized civilian relations) inadvertently created social spaces in which those hierarchies became unstable. Together, Jaima and Callam et al. show that race was not a secondary issue in occupation's cultural project — it was the primary medium through which America's claim to democratic leadership was tested, performed, and often exposed as fraudulent.

Hurh's memoir is the week's grounding weight. Where the other readings analyze occupation as a cultural or social phenomenon, Hurh puts a single Korean body at the center of three successive occupations — Japanese colonialism, American/Soviet division, and North Korean invasion — across one decade. He experienced forced name-change (*soshi-kaimei*), colonial indoctrination, the arbitrary division of his country at the 38th parallel without Korean input, street-level executions under People's Courts, and a ten-day refugee walk through frozen winter as 2.2 million Koreans crossed the Han River. No Westernization, no cultural diplomacy, no maid relationship — just the accumulated weight of great-power competition conducted on Korean bodies. "While whales fight, shrimps' backs burst." Korea is what the week's other readings would look like if all the cultural apparatus were stripped away and only the structural violence remained.

The week's five primary sources triangulate three responses to occupation's cultural demands. Isa (Kojima) represents *passive resistance*: refusing to perform the demanded competence because performing it would make him foreign in his own country. Yamada (Kojima) represents *opportunistic collaboration*: learning to mimic the occupier's authority while privately despising it and angling for personal benefit. Michiko (Kojima) represents *pragmatic adaptation*: using real fluency to navigate without either paralysis or aggression. Nishiyama ("Kamikaze Goes to College") is the *grateful pupil*: Shibusawa's term for the ideal occupied subject, whose acceptability depends entirely on confirming American benevolence. These four positions, crystallized in Kojima's fiction, map exactly onto the real-life figures documented across all of Week 9's secondary readings.

## Connections to Previous Weeks

**Week 3 (May, Constantino, Mabini):** Constantino's "miseducation" thesis — that colonial education produces students who understand their own liberation on the colonizer's terms — runs through Week 9 like a thread. Kojima's English teachers cannot speak English; their relationship to the language is shame, not competence, because the colonial educational project taught the form of transformation without the conditions for genuine learning. The *Life* magazine article's neutral use of "indoctrination program" for freshman orientation is Constantino's point materialized: Americanization is so thoroughly naturalized as "education" that the word for forcible ideological conditioning can be applied to standard college orientation without irony. Callam et al. show Anderson being included in Japanese schoolchildren's textbooks by 1957 — she literally became a lesson for teaching democracy to children whose democratic transformation GHQ had managed. May's "business of education" was being run through jazz, film, and scholarship programs as much as through textbooks.

**Week 5 (Dower, Chang, SWNCC directives):** Dower's "embrace" concept — that Japanese society actively shaped the occupation's democratic opening — is present in Week 9, but complicated. Kojima's "American School" shows what the embrace looked like from beneath: it was not mutual enthusiasm but a compelled performance, made bearable only by Michiko's pragmatism and Isa's resistant silence. Hurh's Korea shows the grimmest version of Week 5's "independence without liberation": the 38th parallel division that Chang analyzed as a structural mechanism of Korean dispossession is lived in Hurh's memoir as the latest in an unbroken sequence of great-power arrangements in which "Koreans did not and could not play any role." The Cold War reversal that Dower and Chang documented as a policy shift appears in Hurh's text as the direct cause of a war that killed millions.

**Week 6 (Hahn, Sirota, Dower Chs. 12–13, Adenauer):** Sirota's Article 24 guaranteed Japanese women constitutional equality drafted by a 22-year-old American woman; Mogi's 1949 letter asked for "spiritual blood transfusion" rather than refrigerators. Kojima's Michiko is the 1954 fiction equivalent of Mogi's 1949 letter: a Japanese woman who actually inhabits the occupation's cultural promises (she speaks real English, accepts material benefits, navigates encounters with Americans) while remaining subject to Yamada's contempt and the occupier's arbitrary authority (Mr. Williams's "no high heels on premises"). The gap between constitutional text and social reality that Week 6 documented finds its Week 9 form in the gap between Nishiyama's "grateful pupil" acceptability and the structural subordination Kojima's story encodes.

**Week 7 (Dower Ch. 17, Kim, Gillingham, Adenauer):** Jarausch's political Westernization (Marshall Plan → Westbindung → Godesberg 1959) is the Week 9 cultural extension of Week 7's economic reconstruction arc. In Week 7, the Marshall Plan rebuilt Germany's industrial economy; in Week 9, the Berlin Airlift, Fulbright scholarships, and America Houses rebuilt Germany's cultural orientation. The two processes were not separate: Adenauer's "wholehearted" embrace of the Schuman Plan (Week 7) and his CDU's "Christian Occident" Western integration ideology (Week 9's Jarausch) were two faces of the same strategic calculation. Anderson's 1953 Japan tour is the Week 9 continuation of the Cold War economic "reverse course": the special procurements that rebuilt Japan's industrial capacity (Dower Ch. 17) were accompanied by cultural soft-power instruments (Anderson, exchange scholarships, GHQ-reformed NHK) that rebuilt Japan's geopolitical identity as a U.S. partner — the cultural reverse course running in tandem with the economic one.

**Weeks 2–4 (Kramer, Beveridge/Schurz, Duara):** Kramer's "imperial racial grammar" (race as the hidden grammar of American empire) is Week 9's most active prior concept. Anderson being deployed to prove racial democracy before Japanese audiences while she remained barred from the Metropolitan Opera is the 1953 version of Kramer's point: race structured American imperial ideology precisely in its suppression — the claim to democratic universalism required the performance of racial inclusion while the reality was racial exclusion. Jaima's Black wives in Germany show the same grammar operating at the social level: the racial order was maintained, inverted, and disrupted simultaneously within the same institutional structure. Duara's "imperialism of free nations" (Week 4) — liberal states using anticolonial rhetoric as the vehicle for client-state formation — describes precisely what "performing democracy" was: deploying Anderson as proof of American racial equality was the cultural face of the same strategy Duara identified in American occupation ideology more broadly.

## Key Themes This Week

**Theme 5 (Race, civilization, and imperial ideology)** is Week 9's master theme. Every single reading engages it directly. Jaima's domestic labor inversion, Hurh's colonial racial hierarchy reproduced in his perception of "superior" American liberators, Kamikaze's "savage-to-dependent" racial transformation, Kojima's humiliation of occupied subjects forced to perform linguistic competence, Anderson's deployment as living proof of racial democracy that excluded her, Jarausch's warning that the "West" Germany embraced was also the West of imperialism and slavery — race is not incidental to occupation's cultural project. It is the medium through which occupation's legitimacy claims were made and contested.

**Theme 7 (Education and cultural transformation)** is the week's explicit content. Week 9 shows what cultural transformation looks like from *inside* — not as a policy directive (SWNCC 150) or a constitutional provision (Article 24) but as felt experience. The results are consistently ambivalent: Jarausch's Westernization succeeded but may have masked guilt; Kojima's occupied Japanese learned performance, not competence; Anderson's Japan tour produced a textbook entry about democracy, not democratic practice; Jaima's Black wives produced interracial friendship within the same structure that sought racial separation.

**Theme 8 (Collaboration and resistance by occupied peoples)** takes its most psychologically nuanced form in Week 9. Kojima's three characters (Isa/Yamada/Michiko) are the week's analytical typology, but the other readings show the same range: Jarausch's Germans actively chose Westernization for their own reasons; Jaima's Black wives broke down racial barriers while also reproducing some of them; Japanese music critics used Anderson as a proxy for their own postoccupation questions about non-Western selfhood; Hurh's survival across three occupations was a form of resistance that required neither ideology nor heroism, just calculation and endurance.

**Theme 4 (Cold War reversal / "reverse course")** appears in Week 9 as cultural continuation rather than political reversal. Marian Anderson's 1953 tour and Kamikaze's 1948 Lafayette scholarship are Cold War soft power operating through the same institutional infrastructure that GHQ built during occupation. The occupation "ended" formally (Japan 1952, Germany 1949 Basic Law), but culturally it continued — through NHK, through exchange programs, through the imaginary of America as "an incarnation of a better life" (Jarausch on GDR Americanization). The reverse course that shifted occupation from democratization to stabilization (Weeks 5–7) is paralleled in Week 9 by the cultural stabilization project: soft power replaced hard power as the instrument for maintaining U.S. hegemony.

**Theme 6 (Self-determination vs. imperial control)** is sharpest in Hurh. Korea's accumulated occupations — Japanese, American/Soviet, North Korean — constitute the course's most concentrated case for occupation as a structural condition: a country that was never the subject of its own history, always the site of others' geopolitical contests. Hurh's question — "Why did Korea always have to be an occupied country?" — is the humanizing, individual-scale version of Chang's structural "independence without liberation." The 38th parallel that Chang analyzed as a mechanism of decolonization management is the structural fact Hurh experienced as a boy becoming a refugee becoming a soldier.

## Combined Key Terms

*Deduplicated across all six Week 9 readings, with cross-references.*

- **"Performing democracy"** (Callam et al.): Using cultural performance — especially by racially marginalized artists — to assert democratic credentials while concealing actual democratic deficits. Anderson's 1953 Japan tour as paradigm case. Cross-reference: connects to Constantino's "miseducation" (W3) and Jaima's "unofficial ambassadors" concept.

- **"Unofficial ambassadors"** (Donna Alvah, used by Jaima): U.S. military dependents overseas as informal vectors of democratic modeling. Black military wives in Germany occupied this role contradictorily — their racial position destabilized the ideology they were meant to embody. Cross-reference: Callam et al. (Anderson as literal "best ambassador to Japan," per US Ambassador Murphy).

- **Double inversion** (Jaima): (1) Black American women who were themselves at the bottom of U.S. racial hierarchies became employers of white German maids; (2) sent to restore racial order, they inadvertently disrupted it through cross-racial intimacy. Core thesis of Jaima's article.

- **"Breath of freedom"** (Höhn/Klimke, extended by Jaima): Originally described Black GIs' experience of racial freedom in Germany through access to white German women; Jaima extends it to Black military wives whose freedom came differently — through the employer/maid relationship and cross-racial friendship. Shows the same occupation structure produced different freedoms by gender.

- **Westernization (Verwestlichung)** (Jarausch): Jarausch's preferred term for Germany's post-1945 cultural-political reorientation, broader than "Americanization." Three mutually reinforcing layers: personal encounters, political Westbindung, popular absorption of American culture. Transnational acculturation, not imposition. Cross-reference: Americanization, Westbindung, Godesberg Program.

- **"Star-pupil syndrome"** (Jarausch): West German opportunism — unconditional embrace of Western parameters masking evasion of guilt rather than genuine democratic transformation. Critical caveat on Westernization's success.

- **Normative Westernization** (Jarausch): Westernization oriented on the positive values of the American and French Revolutions (human rights, civil society, free public sphere) rather than passive adoption of U.S. consumer culture or foreign policy. Required critical German agency.

- **Westbindung** (Adenauer/Jarausch): Policy of anchoring West Germany in the Western alliance; contested by neutralists and nationalists; became bipartisan consensus at Godesberg 1959. Political dimension of Westernization.

- **Transnational acculturation** (Jarausch): Cultural change as bidirectional, contested process across national borders — not simple diffusion from conqueror to conquered. Cross-reference: connects to Callam et al.'s "transnational history" methodology.

- **"Grateful pupil"** (Shibusawa): The ideal occupied Japanese subject — appreciative, assimilated, non-threatening. Nishiyama as paradigm case. Used critically by Shibusawa to show how Japanese individuals were valued insofar as they confirmed American benevolence. Cross-reference: Kojima's Yamada as the corrupted version of the same type.

- **"Junior allies"** (Shibusawa): The postwar reframing of Japan — no longer enemy, not yet equal, but subordinate partner in the U.S.-led Cold War Pacific order. Racial hierarchy softened but not dissolved.

- **Soft power** (Nye, applied by Callam et al.): Influencing other nations through cultural attraction rather than military coercion. Anderson's 1953 tour is Cold War soft power as continuation of occupation's institutional project. Distinguished from, but continuous with, hard power (GHQ directives).

- **African American cultural ambassadors**: State Department strategy of deploying Black artists (Anderson, Armstrong, Ellington) abroad to rebut Soviet propaganda about American racism. Places the burden of defending democracy on those it discriminated against. Cross-reference: Jaima's "unofficial ambassadors" in Germany.

- **"Universal musicality"** (Ginji Yamane): Coded racial-civilizational hierarchy: European classical = universal; African American spirituals = ethnic/particular. Japanese critics reproduced Western racial aesthetics even as some (Yoshida) resisted them.

- **Konketsuji**: Mixed-race children of U.S. GIs and Japanese women; a charged social issue during Anderson's 1953 Japan visit. The *Asahi shinbun* placed Anderson coverage next to a film review about konketsuji. Shows how occupation's sexual politics shadowed even Anderson's humanitarian acts.

- **Passive resistance** (Kojima — Isa): Refusing to perform the occupier's demanded cultural competence (English fluency) as a form of psychological self-preservation. "It is foolish for Japanese to speak this language like foreigners." Cross-reference: connects to Hurh's refusal to idealize his "liberators."

- **Opportunistic collaboration** (Kojima — Yamada): Using occupation's institutional structures for personal advancement while privately disdaining them. Wartime militarism resurfaces, showing "democratic transformation" as thin veneer.

- **Pragmatic adaptation** (Kojima — Michiko): Real fluency and material acceptance without either Isa's paralysis or Yamada's aggression. Most psychologically intact but ideologically ambiguous. Cross-reference: connects to Adenauer's strategic collaboration under constraint (Weeks 6–7).

- **Performed competence** (Kojima): Occupation demands Japanese teachers *demonstrate* English fluency to American evaluators — not for students' benefit but for the occupier's ranking. Theater serving American authority, not Japanese education.

- **Soshi-kaimei** (Hurh): Japanese colonial decree forcing Koreans to adopt Japanese names; Hurh's paradigmatic act of colonial identity erasure. Cross-reference: connects to Constantino's "miseducation" (W3) and the broader theme of colonial education as cultural destruction.

- **Haebang (liberation)** (Hurh): Korean term for August 15, 1945 liberation from Japan; Hurh's account complicates "liberation" since U.S./USSR immediately imposed a division denying Korean self-determination.

- **Accumulated occupations** (Hurh): Hurh's childhood spans Japanese colonialism, American military government, and North Korean invasion — three distinct occupying powers in one decade. Korea as the course's sharpest case for occupation as structural condition rather than contingent event.

- **"While whales fight, shrimps' backs burst"** (Korean proverb, Hurh): Korea's geopolitical self-description — great-power competition conducted on Korean bodies. The Cold War as another round of imperial indifference to Korean self-determination. Cross-reference: Chang's "independence without liberation" (W5); the 38th parallel as structural cause.

## 3-Word Connection Drills

**Set 1: "Performing Democracy" / "Star-Pupil Syndrome" / "Unofficial Ambassadors"**
*(Callam et al. + Jarausch + Jaima — all three Germany/Japan cases, Week 9)*

All three terms describe how the cultural project of occupation required *performance* — by its subjects, its instruments, and its enthusiasts — rather than genuine transformation. Callam et al.'s "performing democracy" captures the U.S. instrumentalizing Anderson to stage racial inclusion for Japanese audiences while Jim Crow remained in force at home. Jarausch's "star-pupil syndrome" captures the German pathology of over-performance: embracing Western values so unconditionally as to mask the evasion of guilt and opportunism beneath the display. Jaima's "unofficial ambassadors" captures the role assigned to Black military wives — modeling American democratic normalcy — that their actual social lives simultaneously confirmed and subverted. All three cases reveal occupation's cultural project as a theater of legitimacy in which performance often substituted for the reality it was supposed to represent. The dangerous implication, which all three authors draw, is that the performance sometimes worked on the audience it was designed for while changing nothing of substance.

---

**Set 2: Passive Resistance / Opportunistic Collaboration / Pragmatic Adaptation**
*(Kojima — all three characters, with connections to Adenauer W6/W7 and Mabini W3)*

Kojima's three characters crystallize the range of responses to occupation's cultural demands that the entire course has documented. Isa's passive resistance — refusing English as the refusal to become foreign in his own country — is the analog of Mabini's argument (Week 3) that genuine sovereignty cannot be earned through compliance with the occupier's timeline. Yamada's opportunistic collaboration — using occupation structures for personal advancement while disdaining them — maps onto Kim's KDP landlords (Week 7) using the occupation's political alliance to block the very land reform the occupation nominally intended. Michiko's pragmatic adaptation — real English fluency as genuine navigation of unequal power — echoes Adenauer's strategic collaboration (Weeks 6–7): acceptance of Allied terms as the price of a functioning state, with the long-term bet that sovereignty could be expanded from within the institutions the occupiers designed. Kojima's fictional typology is, in this sense, an anatomy of how occupied peoples negotiated occupation at every level the course has examined.

---

**Set 3: "Accumulated Occupations" / "Independence Without Liberation" / Soshi-Kaimei**
*(Hurh + Chang W5/W6 + Hurh — Korea across Weeks 5, 6, and 9)*

Chang's "independence without liberation" described Korea's structural condition: formal sovereignty achieved without liberation from colonial economic structures, political collaborators, or great-power control. Hurh's memoir gives that structure a face and a name. Soshi-kaimei — the forced name-change decree that transformed "Hurh Won Moo" into "Moriyama Tomoyuki" — is colonialism as juridical erasure of identity, not merely political subordination. The 38th parallel division (Chang's Week 5 mechanism, Hurh's lived experience) is the American-version of the same structure: liberation that immediately became a new form of dispossession. Hurh's "accumulated occupations" concept — three distinct occupying powers in one decade — extends Chang's structural analysis into the phenomenological register: what it means to be a Korean body in the twentieth century is to be the site of repeated great-power arrangements that each promise liberation while delivering the next form of dependency. The "independence without liberation" is not just constitutional or economic; it is the condition of someone who, as a child, became "Moriyama" and, as an adult, watched 2.2 million of his countrymen cross a frozen river to survive a war caused by a division made without Korean input.

---

**Set 4: Cultural Diplomacy / Westbindung / "Reverse Course"**
*(Callam et al. + Jarausch + Dower/Gillingham W7 — connecting Week 7 economic analysis to Week 9 cultural continuation)*

The economic "reverse course" (Week 7) that shifted occupation from democratic reform to Cold War stabilization — Japan's Dodge Line, Germany's Marshall Plan pivot — had a precise cultural equivalent. Anderson's 1953 Japan tour and the Fulbright exchange programs that Jarausch documents for Germany were not afterthoughts to the economic reconstruction project: they were its cultural face. Westbindung — Germany's political anchorage in the Western alliance — required not just Marshall Plan dollars and NATO membership but the personal encounters, youth clubs, and popular Americanization that Jarausch's three-layer model describes. Anderson's tour extended GHQ's reformed NHK and the US Embassy's cultural apparatus one year after formal Japanese occupation ended — soft power as occupation-by-other-means in Cold War Japan. The pattern across both cases confirms Callam et al.'s insight: "the occupation didn't end culturally." The reverse course redirected institutional energy from democratization to stabilization, but it intensified the cultural project, because cultural legitimacy — "performing democracy" — was now doing the work that hard-power directives could no longer perform openly.

---

**Set 5: Miseducation / "Indoctrination Program" / "Universal Musicality"**
*(Constantino W3 + Life Magazine W9 + Callam et al. W9 — spanning Weeks 3 and 9)*

Constantino's "miseducation of the Filipino" argued that colonial education produced students who understood their own liberation in the colonizer's terms — taught to aspire to American models while the curriculum was designed for dependency. Week 9 shows two later iterations of the same mechanism. The *Life* magazine article's neutral use of "indoctrination program" for Nishiyama's Lafayette College freshman orientation is Constantino's point precisely materialized: Americanization is so thoroughly naturalized as simply what "education" is that the word for forcible ideological conditioning can be applied to standard orientation without irony. The "universal musicality" framework that Japanese critic Yamane applied to Marian Anderson — finding her spirituals impressive but lacking in the universal standard achieved by German lieder — is colonial miseducation operating in reverse direction: the colonized subject internalizing European cultural norms as universal and measuring non-European art against them. That Japanese critics in 1953 applied the same racial-civilizational hierarchy to Anderson's music that European critics had long applied to Japanese art shows how thoroughly occupation's cultural project had naturalized Western aesthetic standards — not by decree but by the same gradual process Constantino identified in the Philippines five decades earlier.

## Essay Questions

1. Jarausch argues that Germany's Westernization was a product of "transnational acculturation" — bidirectional and largely chosen by Germans — rather than a top-down imposition by occupation authorities. Kojima's "The American School" and Callam et al.'s Marian Anderson article suggest that in Japan, the cultural project was experienced quite differently: as demanded performance, racial instrumentalization, and institutional exclusion. Using at least three of Week 9's readings alongside at least one reading from Weeks 3–7 (Constantino, Dower, Mogi, or Chang), compare how occupation's cultural transformation project operated across Germany and Japan. What variables — phase of occupation, racial dynamics, political bonding, presence of hard vs. soft power — explain the different experiences? Which case offers a more durable model of cultural transformation, and on what terms?

2. Race runs through every Week 9 reading: Jaima's domestic labor inversion, Kamikaze's "savage-to-dependent" racial transformation, Anderson's deployment as proof of a racial democracy that excluded her, Jarausch's warning that the "West" Germany embraced included its history of imperialism and racism, Hurh's perception of American "liberators" as a "superior race." Using at least three Week 9 readings alongside Kramer's "imperial racial grammar" (Week 2) and at least one other earlier reading (Fradera, Duara, or Dower), analyze how race functioned in occupation's cultural project. Was race a contradiction the U.S. tried to manage and conceal, a structural feature that shaped every level of occupation policy, or something more complex?

3. Hurh's memoir begins under Japanese colonialism and ends with him becoming a refugee from a war caused by a division imposed without Korean consent. Callam et al. show that Anderson's 1953 Japan tour operated through the same institutional infrastructure GHQ built during occupation — one year after formal occupation ended. Jarausch shows that Germany's Westernization continued long after Allied sovereignty was restored in 1949–55. Using these three readings alongside Chang (Week 5 or 6) and at least one economic reading from Week 7, assess how occupation "ended" — politically, economically, and culturally — across Japan, Germany, and Korea. Is the end of formal occupation a meaningful historical threshold, or does the course's evidence suggest that occupation's effects outlast and exceed its institutional forms? What determines which occupations leave the most durable legacies?
