# Embracing the West
**Author:** Konrad H. Jarausch | **Week:** 9 | **Type:** RR
**Case Study:** Germany
**Pages:** ~103–129 (chapter from *After Hitler: Recivilizing Germans, 1945–1995*, Oxford, 2006)

---

## Summary

Jarausch's central argument is that Germany's "Westernization" after 1945 was not a top-down imposition by occupation authorities but a complex, multi-layered process produced by three mutually reinforcing dynamics: (1) individual personal encounters between Germans and Allied soldiers; (2) deliberate political choices to anchor West Germany in the Western alliance; and (3) the bottom-up absorption of American popular culture and consumer habits by ordinary Germans, especially youth.

**Why "Westernization" rather than "Americanization"?** Jarausch prefers the broader concept to account for British, French, and Italian influences alongside American ones, while acknowledging that "its content continues to refer primarily to American influences" (p.105). He insists the concept must be used critically: the "West" itself is a myth that suppresses its darker sides—imperialism, racism, slavery.

**Personal encounters** were the crucial first layer. Germans greeted Allied occupation with a paradoxical mixture of fear, hope, and cliché. Comparisons between occupiers almost always favored Americans: GIs were perceived as less violent, more generous, and more relaxed than Russians or French. Women, youth, and workers seeking food or employment were the most intense points of contact. Fraternization ban notwithstanding, "an informal barter" emerged of Nazi souvenirs for chocolate and cigarettes; youth clubs, Fulbright exchanges (12,000 Germans by 1955), and returning émigrés like Ernst Fraenkel and Willy Brandt all deepened these bonds. Crucially, Jarausch stresses that these were not just unilateral impositions: Germans actively sought out Western contacts to rebuild economic and cultural networks.

**Political bonding** was the second layer, and it was contested. Adenauer's CDU backed Western integration as a "Christian Occident" aligned with France and Italy; Schumacher's SPD initially prioritized national reunification; Communists advocated Soviet alignment. The Marshall Plan, Berlin Airlift, Basic Law, coal-steel community (ECSC), and eventual NATO membership each advanced Westbindung over the opposition of nationalists and socialists. The East German workers' revolt of June 1953, crushed by Soviet tanks, further discredited neutrality as an option. By 1959, even the SPD's Godesberg Program abandoned reunification primacy and accepted market democracy. The Cold War comparison—1.58 million migrants East-to-West vs. 164,000 West-to-East (1950–54)—became propaganda proof that the West was "almost ten times more attractive."

**Popular Americanization** was the most far-reaching layer. America Houses promoted U.S. high culture; but more powerful were comic books, cowboy films, jazz, rock 'n' roll, blue jeans, nylon stockings, and Hollywood movies. Consumer goods—refrigerators, cars (VW Beetle), television—spread on an American model. Advertising became more aggressive and image-driven. Even anti-American student protesters of the 1960s borrowed tactics (sit-ins, teach-ins) and aesthetic vocabulary (folk songs, long hair) from the U.S. civil rights and counterculture movements. The GDR officially suppressed Americanism but East Germans watched Dallas on Western TV, traded in hard currency "Intershops," and tuned into rock on Radio Luxembourg. SED anti-Americanism inadvertently intensified the fantasy of America as an "incarnation of a better life."

**Contradictions**: Jarausch is careful not to write a triumphalist story. The "star-pupil syndrome" of unconditional Western embrace often masked opportunism and repression of Nazi guilt. Anti-Americanism—from both elitist conservatives and the leftist anti-imperialist tradition—persisted throughout the postwar period. And the "West" Germany arrived in was also a West of imperialism, racism, and exploitative capitalism. Jarausch argues that genuine "normative Westernization" required critical engagement with Western ideals, not passive adoption of whatever U.S. model was on offer.

**Guiding questions:**
- **WHY occupy?** Implied: to "recivilize" a people that had voluntarily embraced a genocidal dictatorship. The reeducation rationale is continuous with the Philippine and Japanese cases but is here applied to a European, "civilized" nation—complicating race-based theories of occupation.
- **WHAT did they do?** Cultural diplomacy (America Houses, Fulbright), political engineering (Basic Law, federalism), economic integration (Marshall Plan, ECSC). But Jarausch shows the limits: voluntary adoption mattered more than mandated reform.
- **HOW did it end?** Occupation formally ended (German sovereignty restored incrementally 1949–1955), but "Westernization" continued beyond formal occupation through NATO, EEC, and cultural flows—suggesting occupation's legacy outlasts its formal structure.

## Key Terms

- **Westernization (Verwestlichung)**: Jarausch's preferred term for Germany's post-1945 cultural-political reorientation, broader than "Americanization" to include British, French, and Italian influences; implies an internal process of adoption, not mere external imposition
- **Americanization**: More specific process of absorbing American popular culture, consumer habits, and democratic norms; Jarausch treats it as a real phenomenon within Westernization, especially in youth culture and mass consumption
- **Sovietization**: The East German parallel process, by which the Soviet model was imposed on the GDR; Jarausch uses it as a foil to show why Westernization succeeded: it was less coercive and more materially attractive
- **Transnational acculturation**: Jarausch's methodological framing—cultural change as a two-way, contested process across national lines rather than simple diffusion
- **"Christian Occident" (Abendland)**: Conservative ideological framing used by Adenauer and CDU to give Westernization a European/Catholic cultural identity; anti-Communist and anti-Turkish in resonance
- **Westbindung (Western bond/anchorage)**: Adenauer's policy of anchoring West Germany firmly in the Western alliance; contested by neutralists and nationalists; gradually became bipartisan consensus by Godesberg 1959
- **Normative Westernization**: Jarausch's term for Westernization oriented on idealized Western values (human rights, civil society, self-criticism) rather than pragmatic adoption of whatever the U.S. actually did
- **"Star-pupil syndrome"**: Jarausch's critique of overly enthusiastic German conformity to Western models as opportunism and repression of memory rather than genuine democratic transformation
- **Godesberg Program (1959)**: SPD party congress resolution abandoning socialist dogma and reunification primacy; marks the moment Western integration became truly bipartisan in West Germany
- **Fulbright Program**: U.S. exchange scholarships bringing ~12,000 Germans to the U.S. by 1955; instrument of soft-power cultural diplomacy
- **America Houses (Amerikahäuser)**: U.S. information centers in major German cities offering U.S. newspapers, books, exhibits; chief institutional tool of cultural reorientation
- **Atlantic Bridge (Atlantik-Brücke)**: Founded 1952, elite West German organization cultivating transatlantic ties and spreading positive images of the U.S.; Western counterpart to the Soviet DSF
- **"Denglisch" / "Germlish"**: Hybrid German-English spoken language that emerged from mass Americanism adoption; indicator of cultural transformation at the everyday level
- **GYA (German Youth Activities)**: U.S. Army clubs where American soldiers played sports with and showed movies to German youths; part of soft occupation strategy to reduce anti-American sentiment

FRONT: What was "normative Westernization"?
BACK: Jarausch's ideal type: Westernization oriented on the central values of the American and French Revolutions (human rights, civil society, free public sphere) rather than passive adoption of U.S. consumer or foreign policy. FRONT: What role did the Berlin Airlift play in German Westernization?

FRONT: How did East German Americanization differ from West German Americanization?

FRONT: What was the Godesberg Program and why does it matter for Westernization?

FRONT: What does Jarausch mean by "transnational acculturation"?
BACK: Cultural change as a bidirectional, contested process across national borders—not simple diffusion from conqueror to conquered. Westernization required German agency (travel, exchange, adoption) not just Allied imposition. | p.104 | Methodological key term
