## Theme 1: Occupation Policy & Planning (Punitive vs. Reformist)

**Exceptionality / Special regime** (Fradera, W1) — The formal construction of inferior political, legal, and fiscal rules for colonial possessions within otherwise liberal political systems. Fradera's defining feature of modern empire. → Insular cases; JCS 1067; CPA Orders; Theme 5.

**"Exception as the rule"** (Fradera, W1) — The structural normalization of colonial exception — not a temporary emergency but the permanent operating logic of empire. → Hague Convention (Montgomery W10); Section 5g (W5).

**Minimalists vs. Maximalists** (Merritt, W4) — US policy factions over Germany: minimalists sought only to end German belligerency; maximalists wanted permanent subjugation (Morgenthau Plan). JCS 1067 lay between them and became "a dead letter within months."

**JCS 1067 / "Defeated enemy nation"** (W5 PS) — Germany occupation directive (April 1945). Punitive premise: "Germany will not be occupied for the purpose of liberation but as a defeated enemy nation." Prohibition on economic rehabilitation (Section 16). → SWNCC 150 (reformist pole); Morgenthau Plan (W7).

**SWNCC 150** (W5 PS) — Japan occupation directive (September 1945). Reformist pole: "it is not the responsibility of the Allied Powers to impose upon Japan any form of government not supported by the freely expressed will of the people." → Section 5g as contradiction; Dower "neocolonial revolution."

**SWNCC 176 / Direct vs. indirect rule** (W5 PS) — Korea occupation directive (October 1945). Liberationist stated objectives + Section 5g that dissolved the KPR. Direct military government in Korea vs. indirect rule through Emperor in Japan. → Chang "decolonization management"; SWNCC 150 contrast.

**SWNCC 228** (W6 PS) — Document establishing that imposed constitutional reform should be used "only as a last resort." Honored (approximately) in Germany; abandoned in Japan; never seriously applied in Korea. → Fiction of authorship; Hahn.

**Morgenthau Plan** (Gillingham, W7) — Treasury Secretary Morgenthau's 1944 proposal to deindustrialize Germany and reduce it to pre-industrial conditions. Blocked by Stimson; influenced JCS 1067. The punitive extreme abandoned by the Marshall Plan.

**Artificial revolution** (Montgomery, W10) — Montgomery's coinage for the postwar occupations' historical novelty: externally imposed, programmed installation of democracy through elite removal, new leadership, and institutional guarantees. "Programmed, peaceful, and reversible — which is its defining feature and its fundamental weakness." → Dower "revolution from above" (W5).

**Three-part program of artificial revolution** (Montgomery, W10) — (1) Elimination of despotic elites; (2) encouragement of new democratic leadership; (3) constitutional/institutional guarantees. Montgomery's retrospective verdict: only (1) was durably executed; (2) and (3) were interrupted by Cold War reversal.

**Hague Convention / Unconditional surrender** (Montgomery, W10) — The 1907 Hague Convention required occupiers to respect the laws of defeated nations. Montgomery argues unconditional surrender created a legal exception — but an uncomfortable one: "unpleasantly close to suggesting that a great power may force a nation to abdicate a portion of its sovereign rights." → Fradera exceptionality (W1).

**"Ambition outran performance"** (Montgomery, W10) — Montgomery's summary judgment on the postwar occupations: the program was more ambitious than what was actually accomplished. Sober assessment of the gap between intent and Cold War-truncated outcome.

**MacArthur model** (Haass, W13) — Haass's term for unilateral proconsular occupation — a single supreme US authority governing by decree without multilateral legitimacy or local approval mechanisms. Haass explicitly rejected this for Iraq; the CPA became exactly this.

**CPA Order #1 (De-Baathification, May 16, 2003)** (W13) — Bremer's decree purging the top four Ba'ath Party ranks across every ministry, university, and hospital. More sweeping than denazification; no pragmatic revision followed. → JCS 1067 (analogy invoked, lessons ignored).

**CPA Order #2 (Dissolution of Entities, May 23, 2003)** (W13) — Disbanded the entire Iraqi military, intelligence, and security apparatus; ~400,000 suddenly unemployed, armed, humiliated men. Officers (Colonel and above) barred from severance or pension. Created the security vacuum Haass had predicted.

**Tabula rasa state-building** (W13) — The theory implicit in CPA Orders 1–2: total demolition of existing institutions is the precondition for democratic reconstruction. Opposite of what WWII occupiers did in Japan and Germany (retained bureaucracy while purging leadership). → Kim "blank slate" (W7).

**"Uses of history"** (Dower, W12) — Dower's methodological distinction between rigorous historical comparison (cataloging all conditions, crosscutting ideological comfort lines) vs. opportunistic analogy (cherry-picking flattering precedents). The Bush administration's Japan-as-Iraq analogy failed this test.

**Pick-up game** (SIGAR W14; Hadley) — The US has no post-conflict stabilization model; after Vietnam, dismantled its irregular-warfare capacity, then repeated the same mistakes in Iraq and Afghanistan. "We purged ourselves of everything that had to do with irregular warfare." → Annual lobotomy; Montgomery's artificial revolution. (REVISIT SIGAR!!)

**Mission creep** (SIGAR, W14) — Unplanned expansion from destroying al-Qaeda → defeating the Taliban → fighting corruption → building a legitimate state. Same structure as Latham's "tutelage without exit" (W11).

## Theme 2: Democratic Reform & Constitutionalism

**Forced constitutionalization** (Platt Amendment W2; Japan W6) — Occupying forces dictating constitutional content as the price of withdrawal. The US made Cuba's Constitutional Convention accept Platt provisions (1901); SCAP drafted the Japanese constitution and presented it as Japanese (1946). Pattern spans 1901–1947.

**Basic Law / Grundgesetz** (Merritt W4; Hahn W6; Adenauer W6) — West German "provisional constitution" adopted May 1949. Called Basic Law (not Verfassung) to signal provisionality pending reunification. American contributions: federalism, constructive vote of no confidence, five-percent electoral threshold, bill of rights, judicial review.

**Constructive vote of no confidence** (Merritt, W4) — Parliament must elect a replacement chancellor before forcing the incumbent's resignation. Designed to prevent Weimar-style cabinet instability. Direct US contribution to the Basic Law.

**Managed self-determination** (Hahn, W6) — Hahn's term for US Germany policy: Germans wrote the text, Allies set the constraints through informal guidance. Distinct from Japan's "benevolent imposition" and Korea's "proceduralist exit." Produced the most durable constitutional outcome.

**Occupation statute** (Hahn W6; Adenauer W6) — Document defining residual Allied powers after the Basic Law took effect; its contents were kept secret from the Parliamentary Council throughout drafting. Adenauer's central grievance: "our work was made very much harder by our ignorance."

**Parliamentary Council** (Adenauer, W6) — 65-member West German constituent assembly seated in Bonn, September 1948. Adenauer president (CDU/CSU). German-authored but Allied-supervised. Terminology negotiation ("Basic Law" vs. Verfassung) as paradigm of agency-within-constraint.

**Weimar Article 48 / "Crisis clause" (Article 57)** (Hahn W6; Chang W6) — Clay's entire Germany strategy aimed to prevent Weimar's recurrence. Korea's 1948 constitution contained a comparable emergency powers provision (Art. 57); US adviser Pergler compared the two. The same power that spent years preventing Weimar's failure in Germany produced the Weimar danger in Korea because it prioritized exit speed over constitutional quality.

**"Only as a last resort" (SWNCC 228)** (W6) — Principle that imposed reforms are less durable than apparently indigenous ones. Honored (approximately) in Germany; abandoned in Japan (first resort); never applied in Korea.

**Fiction of Japanese authorship** (Sirota, W6) — MacArthur announced the SCAP-drafted constitution as "the work of the Japanese government" — praising it as "epoch-making" as if "he had never seen it in his life."

**Article 24** (Sirota, W6) — Women's equality provision of the Japanese constitution: "Marriage shall be based only on the mutual consent of both sexes." Drafted by 22-year-old Beate Sirota; survived Japanese official opposition through her personal credibility, not principle.

**Government Section (GS)** (Sirota, W6) — MacArthur's administrative unit (Gen. Whitney) that drafted the entire Japanese constitution in roughly nine days. Sirota was one of six women on staff.

**"You cannot impose a new mode of social thought on a country by law" (Rowell)** (Sirota, W6) — The Steering Committee's counterargument to social-engineering constitutionalism. Mogi's 1949 letter is the empirical verdict two years later.

**Democratic proceduralism** (Chang, W6) — US self-determination in practice: elections and constitutions as the *form* of self-determination regardless of whether conditions for free participation existed. Korea's constitutional process as paradigm case.

**UNTCOK** (Chang, W6) — UN Temporary Commission on Korea; validated the May 1948 elections despite internal dissent (Australia, Canada, India); gave the US the international legitimation needed to withdraw.

**"Decolonization without liberation"** (Chang, W6) — Chang's closing thesis: "an empire without colonies was built upon the foundation of decolonization without liberation." → "Independence without liberation" (W5); Platt Amendment (W2); Afghanistan (W14).

**Dual sovereignty** (Hahn, W6) — American federalist principle — power flows simultaneously to states and federal government — that Clay's insistence shaped the Basic Law's fiscal provisions.

**"Spiritual blood transfusion"** (Mogi, W6) — Mogi's phrase for what she wants from the Occupation: genuine intellectual and civic exchange, not consumer goods or surface democracy. → Constantino "miseducation" (W3).

## Theme 3: Land Reform & Economic Restructuring

**Land-to-tiller program** (Ladejinsky W7; W10) — Policy principle (Japan, Korea, Taiwan) transferring cultivated land to those who worked it; implemented through elected local land commissions; compensation at below-market rates.

**"Plow outbids the sword"** (Ladejinsky, W7) — Central slogan: economic land reform defeats communist revolution more effectively than military force. → Kim's critique: dual purpose can produce dual obstruction.

**Vested lands** (Kim, W7) — Formerly Japanese-owned agricultural properties seized by USAMGIK. Only these (18.7% of total South Korean farmland) were redistributed in the March 1948 partial reform. Limitation defined by the KDP alliance.

**"Anti-communist marriage of convenience"** (Kim, W7) — Kim's characterization of the USAMGIK-KDP alliance: Cold War logic (KDP was anti-communist) that paradoxically blocked the democratic land reform the occupation nominally intended.

**"The occupied territory is not a blank slate"** (Kim, W7) — Kim's methodological conclusion: local political actors actively shaped, delayed, and limited US reform plans. The occupier cannot simply impose desired outcomes. Applicable across all course case studies.

**New Deal ideology in occupation policy** (Kim W7; Ladejinsky W7; Gillingham W7) — Truman-era belief that concentrated economic power breeds authoritarianism; dispersal is the prerequisite for democracy. Drove reform plans in Japan, Korea, and Germany. Lost to Cold War pragmatism.

**Reverse course (逆コース, gyaku kōsu)** (Dower, W7) — The shift in US occupation policy from reform/democratization (1945–47) to Cold War stabilization/reconstruction (1947–52). In Japan: Dodge Line + Korean War special procurements. In Korea: structural from first months. In Germany: Morgenthau → Marshall → Schuman arc.

**Dodge Line (1949)** (Dower, W7) — Deflationary austerity imposed by US banker Joseph Dodge: fixed exchange rate ¥360/$1, slashed government spending, reduced inflation. Contradicted earlier pro-labor stance. Most concrete economic expression of the reverse course in Japan.

**Special procurements (特需, tokuji)** (Dower, W7) — US military purchasing in Japan during the Korean War (1950–53), ~$2–3 billion. Rebuilt industrial capacity SCAP had tried to dismantle. Often cited as the true start of Japan's "economic miracle."

**Zaibatsu / Keiretsu** (Dower, W7) — Pre-war Japanese industrial/financial conglomerates SCAP targeted for dissolution. Partially broken up but reconstituted as keiretsu — reverse course halted full decartelization. → "New zaibatsu" (Dower W12).

**Decartelization** (Gillingham, W7) — US effort to break up German industrial cartels held responsible for Nazi militarism. Defeated by Wall Street-oriented officers (General Draper) who prioritized recovery. Parallel to Korea's land reform obstruction by KDP landlords.

**"Industrial revival without reform"** (Gillingham, W7) — Gillingham's phrase for the German reverse course moment: the economy was rebuilt, but structural transformation was abandoned. Describes both Germany and Japan simultaneously.

**Marshall Plan / European Recovery Program (ERP)** (Gillingham, W7) — Announced June 1947. Committed US to Western European reconstruction including former enemy Germany. Fundamental policy shift from disengagement to strategic reconstruction. → "Victor or vanquished" (Atlantic Charter Point 4, W4).

**Schuman Plan (May 9, 1950)** (Gillingham W7; Adenauer W7) — French FM Schuman's proposal (designed by Jean Monnet) to pool French and German coal-steel production under a supranational High Authority. "Not economic, but eminently political" (Adenauer). Led to ECSC (1951), forerunner of the EU. Most durable occupation economic legacy.

**"Conspicuous exception"** (Ladejinsky, W10) — Japan's land reform as the one occupation reform that survived intact when "virtually every other reform measure has been revised or legislated out of existence." Survived because reversal would have been more destabilizing than preservation.

**"Strange partnership"** (Ladejinsky, W10) — The formula for what made Japan's land reform work: "a left-wing Socialist [Hiroo Wada] and an American five-star General, and in the order cited." Indigenous reformer driving design; American authority providing cover.

**Hiroo Wada** (Ladejinsky, W10) — Japan's Socialist Minister of Agriculture; Ladejinsky calls him "the real architect of the revolution." Complicates the "American-made" narrative: the reform was conceptually indigenous; SCAP's role was to force the Japanese government's hand. → KDP as obstruction (Kim, W7).

**Rack-rent tenancy** (Ladejinsky, W10) — Pre-reform Japanese system: tenants paid exorbitant rents in kind, leaving insufficient produce to sustain families. 70% of Japan's farm families were tenants or part-tenants before 1946. Structural basis of rural poverty and Communist recruitment.

**HAVA (Helmand and Arghandab Valley Authority)** (Cullather, W14) — US-Afghan joint development authority created 1952; 1,800 sq. miles; modeled on TVA. Cullather's "controlled experiment" in modernization failure: lavishly funded, sustained thirty years, still failed.

**Commitment/leverage paradox** (Cullather W14; SIGAR W14) — Once US credibility was invested in HAVA, withdrawal became impossible even after failure was confirmed. SIGAR's "conditionality" lesson is the same paradox at scale.

**Development myopia** (Cullather, W14) — Identifying a single technical constraint (water scarcity) as the cause of underdevelopment while subordinating complex social, political, and ecological problems.

**Elite capture** (SIGAR, W14) — When reconstruction funds are seized by local powerbrokers (warlords, corrupt officials) rather than intended beneficiaries. → Ladejinsky's "strange partnership" (W10) as the positive inversion; Kim's Korean landlords (W7) as precursor.

**Absorptive capacity** (SIGAR, W14) — Maximum aid a country can productively absorb (~15–45% of GDP). During the 2009–2011 surge, US spending exceeded 100% of Afghan GDP.

**Annual lobotomy** (SIGAR, W14) — Institutional knowledge lost each year as staff rotated out; successors started from scratch and repeated predecessors' mistakes.

**Off-budget assistance** (SIGAR, W14) — Aid channeled through NGOs/contractors rather than Afghan ministries; prevented the Afghan government from developing fiscal capacity.

## Theme 4: Cold War Reversal / "Reverse Course"

**Cold War reversal — Germany** (Merritt, W4) — 1947–48 shift from democratization to alliance-building; reform-minded Germans sidelined for conservative anti-Soviet counterparts. Precedes and anticipates Japan's "reverse course" by roughly two years.

**Tutelage / limited self-determination** (Duara W4; Latham W11) — The ideological device deferring full self-determination while maintaining client-state control. Direct line from Beveridge's wardship logic (W2) through Lane's "leading-string law" (1922) to postwar US practice and Cold War modernization.

**Ultraimperialism** (Duara, W4) — Post-WWII US global empire: military bases, IMF/World Bank/GATT structures managing cooperation among capitalist powers and developmental imperialism in the decolonized world.

**"Reverse course" (Japan/Korea, 1945–47)** (Dower W5/W7; Chang W5) — Japan's general strike ban (February 1947), Korean Autumn Strikes suppression (1946), Dodge Line (1949). The reverse course did not begin with a policy announcement — it began with choices made in occupation's critical first months.

**"Reverse course" (Okinawa/Korea framing)** (Dower, W12) — Dower's extension: where democratization competed with strategic priorities (Okinawa as permanent military base; South Korea as US-backed autocracy), the US consistently chose the latter. Internal counter-examples to the "Japan as democracy model" narrative.

**Domino Theory** (Eisenhower, W11) — Strategic doctrine that communist takeover of one country triggers sequential collapse across neighbors. Eisenhower's April 7, 1954 press conference. Bundles strategic, economic (tin, tungsten, rubber), and demographic (450 million "already lost") fears. Master justification for Vietnam involvement for twenty years.

**Alternative modernity** (Westad, cited in Latham, W11) — Soviet communism as a rival path to modernity for postcolonial peoples. Made US modernization programs existentially urgent after 1949 (China) and 1950 (Korea).

**"Iraq reverse course (inverted)"** (W13) — Japan and Germany began reformist and reversed toward stabilization; Iraq began strategic (no reformist agenda) and only attempted reform after failure (2007 surge). The reverse course ran backward.

## Theme 5: Race, Civilization, and Imperial Ideology

**Dangerous symmetries** (Fradera, W1) — The inherent contradiction between liberal universalism and colonial exceptionality: colonized peoples use the metropole's own rights-language to demand equality, exhausting empires from within. The motor of anticolonial nationalism from Philippines to Korea to Vietnam to Afghanistan.

**Incorporated vs. unincorporated territories** (Fradera, W1) — Post-1901 American legal distinction. "Unincorporated" territories (Philippines, Puerto Rico, Guam) were owned by but not constitutionally part of the US, enabling indefinite colonial governance.

**Insular cases (1901)** (Fradera, W1) — Supreme Court decisions (*Downes v. Bidwell* et al.) establishing the unincorporated territory doctrine — the American legal architecture of empire.

**Castas pardas** (Fradera, W1) — Free descendants of African slaves (~one-third of Spanish American population), excluded from the 1812 Spanish Constitution via racial "heterogeneity" rhetoric. Paradigmatic case of exceptionality encoded in liberal law.

**Imperial racial grammar** (Kramer, W2) — The shared framework within which both imperialists and anti-imperialists operated: Filipinos as racially inferior, incapable of self-government, objects not subjects of the debate. Neither Beveridge nor Schurz challenged this premise.

**Imperial formation** (Kramer, W2) — A colonial racial and governing structure that is contingent, unstable, assembled from multiple sources (Spanish, British, domestic US racial regimes), and requires constant maintenance. Not a coherent "system" but a contested assemblage.

**Transnational history** (Kramer, W2) — Methodology tracing ideas, people, and structures across colonial/national boundaries. Necessary because American colonial racial ideology was produced at the intersection of prior colonial formations, not invented domestically.

**Civilizationist ideology** (Beveridge W2; Kramer W2) — The belief that world's peoples occupy a racial hierarchy of "civilization" with Anglo-Saxons at apex, obligating them to govern "inferior" peoples. Beveridge's Senate speech is its purest expression. → "Developmental stage" (Latham W11) as Cold War successor.

**Benevolent assimilation** (Beveridge W2; Worcester W3) — McKinley's framing of Philippine colonialism as a gift of civilization. Worcester's administrative catalog of American gifts is its elaborated form. Challenged by Constantino, Mabini, and the NYT/Smith case simultaneously.

**Consent of the governed (racial limitation)** (Beveridge W2; League Platform W2) — Beveridge argues Jeffersonian consent applies only to those "capable of self-government" — a racial limitation turning liberal theory into colonial exclusion.

**Assimilation problem** (Schurz, W2) — Schurz's practical objection to annexation: if the US takes the Philippines, Filipinos must eventually become citizens, which he treats as racially catastrophic. Not a defense of Filipino rights but of racial republican purity.

**Miseducation** (Constantino, W3) — Not absence of education but education designed to serve the colonizer's interests — producing colonial subjects who internalized American values and lost nationalist will. Form of schooling more significant than its content. → "American cultural enslavement" (NLF W11); "Performing democracy" (W9).

**"Backward races"** (May W3; Kramer W2) — Racialized category used to justify industrial rather than academic instruction for non-white populations; applied simultaneously to Filipinos, African Americans, and Native Americans. Making the Philippines a colonial extension of domestic racial governance.

**"Carbon copy colonial"** (Constantino, W3) — Filipino who had absorbed American values, forgotten nationalist heroes, and ceased to require external enforcement of colonial order. Most stable form of special regime: one that makes itself invisible by making the colonized desire their own colonization.

**Pan-Asianism** (Duara W4; Reischauer W4) — Japanese ideological claim of Asian civilizational solidarity against Western imperialism. Japan's framing of the Pacific War as "a holy crusade of yellow and brown peoples for freedom from the white race." → Reischauer's counter-strategy.

**"Performing democracy"** (Callam et al., W9) — Using cultural performance — especially by racially marginalized artists — to assert democratic credentials while concealing actual democratic deficits. Marian Anderson's 1953 Japan tour as paradigm case. → Miseducation (W3); "star-pupil syndrome" (W9).

**"Star-pupil syndrome"** (Jarausch, W9) — West German pathology: unconditional embrace of Western parameters masking evasion of guilt rather than genuine democratic transformation. → Denazification creating a vacuum (Montgomery W10).

**"Grateful pupil"** (Shibusawa, W9) — The ideal occupied Japanese subject — appreciative, assimilated, non-threatening. Nishiyama at Lafayette College as paradigm case. Valued insofar as they confirmed American benevolence. → Kojima's Yamada as corrupted version.

**"Universal musicality"** (Yamane, W9) — Coded racial-civilizational hierarchy: European classical = universal; African American spirituals = ethnic/particular. Japanese critics reproducing Western racial aesthetics in evaluating Marian Anderson.

**Soshi-kaimei** (Hurh, W9) — Japanese colonial decree forcing Koreans to adopt Japanese names. Hurh's paradigmatic act of colonial identity erasure. → Constantino "miseducation" (W3).

**Modernization theory** (Latham, W11) — Framework holding that all societies travel a universal trajectory from "traditional" to "modern"; US technical assistance can accelerate this. Latham's thesis: not a Cold War invention but a crystallization of imperial assumptions dating to 1898. → "Colonialism of capacity" (Kramer via Latham); developmental stage.

**"Colonialism of capacity"** (Kramer, cited in Latham, W11) — Post-1902 shift in US Philippine colonial ideology: replacing racial extermination with "aggressively optimistic" tutelage. Filipinos could evolve toward self-government — under indefinite US control. Latham identifies this as the direct forerunner of Cold War modernization.

**"Underdevelopment" / developmental stage** (Latham, W11) — Replacement for "backwardness": frames poverty as improvable stage on universal trajectory, not racial destiny. Appears liberal; produces the same hierarchy. Shifts race-talk into development-talk register.

**American exceptionalism** (Dower, W12) — The belief that the US is uniquely virtuous and exempt from historical comparison with other imperial powers. The ideological mechanism converting serious historical analysis into selective precedent-citation. → Fradera's "exceptionality" (W1) as the legal-structural expression.

**"West and the Rest"** (Dower, W12) — Methodological paradigm in which "the West" functions as the universal standard against which non-Western societies are measured; insulates Western powers from being measured against non-Western templates. → Latham's "developmental stage" (W11).

**Bogus casus belli** (Dower, W12) — A fabricated or manipulated incident used to justify military aggression. Mukden/railway incident (Japan, 1931) ↔ WMD claims (US, 2003). Places the US in a history of imperial deception rather than exceptionalist deviation.

**"Islam + occupation" thesis** (Malkasian, W14) — The Taliban's alignment with what it fundamentally meant to be Afghan (Islam + resistance to foreign occupation) was a *necessary* condition for US defeat — not the cause, but the condition without which other factors were insufficient.

**Pashtunwali** (Malkasian, W14) — Unwritten Pashtun code of honor (namus), revenge (badal), hospitality (melmastia), and subservience to no man. Created fierce resistance to outsiders and tribal fragmentation preventing unified military effectiveness.

**"A piece of America inserted into the Afghan landscape"** (Toynbee/Cullather, W14) — Description of Lashkar Gah planned suburb. Physical embodiment of modernization ideology's civilizational assumption: that America's spatial and cultural forms are universal templates. → Thomasites as "second army of occupation" (Constantino W3).

## Theme 6: Self-Determination vs. Imperial Control

**Non-annexation pledge / Teller Amendment** (W2) — Congress's preemptive disclaimer of sovereignty over Cuba (1898). Anti-imperialist legislative victory. Significance measured by what followed: the Platt Amendment.

**Informal empire / protectorate (Platt Amendment)** (W2) — Cuba had nominal sovereignty but US intervention rights, fiscal veto, and permanent military bases. Sovereignty in name, protectorate in practice. Guantanamo Bay lease (Platt Art. VII) persists.

**Intervention clause (Platt, Art. III)** (W2) — The US may intervene to preserve "adequate government" — where the US defines "adequate." Invoked 1906, 1912, 1917, 1920. → Section 5g (W5); UNTCOK validation (W6); all versions of conditional sovereignty.

**Filipino agency** (Kramer, W2) — Kramer's insistence that colonized Filipinos shaped, appropriated, and contested colonial racial structures rather than merely receiving them. The Philippine-American War as armed resistance forcing ideological crisis on the civilizing mission.

**Natural law sovereignty** (Mabini, W3) — Authority resides inherently in the people themselves; externally imposed sovereignty is morally void regardless of military outcome. The philosophical foundation of Mabini's resistance — and eventually of the Atlantic Charter forty years later.

**"Imperialism of free nations"** (Duara, W4) — 20th-century imperial form exercised by US, Japan, USSR: client-states, anticolonial ideology, developmental investment, regional economic blocs. Manchukuo (1932–1945) is the prototype; postwar US is the "apogee."

**Strategic autarky** (Duara, W4) — The concept that imperial powers need self-sufficient regional economic blocs for long-term competition — requiring client-states to be industrialized. Explains Manchukuo's developmental character and anticipates Marshall Plan logic.

**"Emperor as puppet"** (Reischauer, W4) — Reischauer's frank proposal to preserve Hirohito as a "puppet" and future ally, explicitly invoking Japan's Manchukuo experience as the model. Instantiates Duara's client-state framework in a planning document.

**"Neocolonial Revolution"** (Dower, W5) — Dower's paradox for occupied Japan: genuine social transformation (labor organizing, democratic mobilization, cultural opening) occurring within the framework of American military occupation. Revolution was real; its context was imperial.

**"Embracing Defeat"** (Dower, W5) — Dower's title concept: Japanese society actively shaped the occupation's transformations rather than passively submitting. Centers Japanese agency. → Kojima's complication of "embrace" as demanded performance (W9).

**Indirect rule** (Dower W5; SWNCC 150) — MacArthur's strategy of governing through the Emperor and existing Japanese institutions. Contrasts with Korea's direct rule. Enables managed democratic transformation.

**"Independence without liberation"** (Chang, W5) — Chang's thesis: formal independence (sovereignty, elections) achieved without liberation from colonial economic structures, political collaborators, or social hierarchies. Korea's defining outcome; also a structural description of American postwar empire broadly. → Platt Amendment (W2); Afghanistan (W14).

**Decolonization management** (Chang, W5) — US occupation strategy: active management of Korea's transition from Japanese colonialism to ensure Cold War-compatible outcomes, not neutral facilitation of self-determination.

**Korean People's Republic (KPR)** (Chang W5; SWNCC 176) — Grassroots Korean government formed September 1945 under Lyuh Woon-hyung, with people's committees across the country. Dissolved by US Military Government under SWNCC 176 Section 5g.

**"Semblance of sovereignty"** (Chang, W5/W6) — Elections gave Korea the procedural form of self-determination without popular control or democratic institutions rooted in genuine popular movements. → Platt Amendment (W2); Basic Law (W6).

**"Fear of Communism has in fact become fear of democracy"** (Roger Baldwin, Chang W5) — ACLU founder's observation: anti-communist logic was used to suppress the KPR and Korean labor movement, both democratic popular movements rather than Soviet instruments.

**Section 5g (SWNCC 176)** (W5) — Prohibition on recognizing "any self-styled Korean provisional government." Chang's key analytical target — dissolves KPR, enables support for rightists, embodies "independence without liberation" in a single operative clause.

**Paternalism / compliance vs. agency** (US Hodge flyer, W5) — US: comply to earn democracy; Soviet: be the creator of your own happiness. Hodge's public paternalism and his private racism (Koreans as "childish") are two registers of the same ideology.

**Associated States** (Eisenhower, W11) — Eisenhower's term for French Indochina territories: neither fully independent nor formally colonial. Reflects the ambiguity of American informal empire. → Platt protectorate (W2).

**"Free World"** (Eisenhower, W11) — Cold War ideological category grouping democracies, colonies, and client states against communism; erases distinctions between self-governing and occupied peoples. → Duara "imperialism of free nations" (W4).

**Double yoke** (Ho Chi Minh, W11) — Ho's phrase for 1940–1945 Vietnam under simultaneous French colonial and Japanese occupation. France's twice-capitulating to Japan delegitimized any French claim to resume colonial rule after 1945.

**Tutelage** (Latham W11; Beveridge W2; Taft) — The logic that "underdeveloped" peoples need US guidance before they are "ready" for self-government. Taft's "fifty or a hundred years" is its baldest statement; Point Four is its technocratic renovation. → "Colonialism of capacity" (W11).

**Buffer state** (Cullather W14; Malkasian W14) — A neutral state maintained between competing powers. Afghanistan operated as a buffer: British India/Russia, then US/USSR, then US counterterrorism vs. Pakistani "strategic depth." Structurally incapable of developing sovereign institutional coherence because every neighboring power has an interest in keeping it divided. → Duara's client-state formation (W4).

**"Any Afghan government was imperiled"** (Malkasian, W14) — Malkasian's most provocative claim: alignment with the US destroyed the legitimacy that sovereignty requires. "Independence without liberation" extended to its logical extreme: nominal independence was impossible because the government required the very foreign presence that delegitimized it.

## Theme 7: Education and Cultural Transformation

**Thomasites / "second army of occupation"** (Constantino, W3) — The ~600 American teachers arriving on the USS Thomas in July 1901. Constantino's phrase is the most economical description of the connection between education and colonial conquest.

**English as wedge** (Constantino, W3) — English-language instruction as double displacement: separating Filipinos from pre-colonial history and from non-English-speaking countrymen, producing class divisions rooted in colonial access.

**Industrial education** (May, W3) — American colonial policy priority (1909–30): manual training and handicraft production over academic instruction; explicitly rooted in "backward races" reasoning applied to Filipinos, African Americans, and Native Americans.

**Commercialization / standardization** (May, W3) — Frank White's principles for the Bureau of Education: produce salable articles, enforce uniform quality. Transformed schools into colonial economic infrastructure.

**Monroe Commission (1925)** (May, W3) — External review that indicted industrial education as educationally and economically inadequate. The colonial program's own evaluators condemned it.

**Westernization / Verwestlichung** (Jarausch, W9) — Jarausch's preferred term for Germany's post-1945 cultural-political reorientation, broader than "Americanization." Three mutually reinforcing layers: personal encounters, political Westbindung, popular absorption of American culture. Transnational acculturation, not imposition.

**Normative Westernization** (Jarausch, W9) — Westernization oriented on the positive values of the American and French Revolutions (human rights, civil society, free public sphere) rather than passive adoption of US consumer culture. Required critical German agency.

**Westbindung** (Adenauer/Jarausch, W9) — Policy of anchoring West Germany in the Western alliance; became bipartisan consensus at Godesberg 1959. Political dimension of Westernization.

**Transnational acculturation** (Jarausch, W9) — Cultural change as bidirectional, contested process across national borders — not simple diffusion from conqueror to conquered.

**Soft power** (Callam et al., W9) — Influencing nations through cultural attraction rather than military coercion. Anderson's 1953 tour as Cold War soft power continuing occupation's institutional project.

**African American cultural ambassadors** (Callam et al., W9) — State Department strategy of deploying Black artists (Anderson, Armstrong, Ellington) abroad to rebut Soviet propaganda about American racism. Places burden of defending democracy on those it discriminated against.

**Passive resistance / Isa** (Kojima, W9) — Refusing to perform the occupier's demanded cultural competence (English fluency) as psychological self-preservation. "It is foolish for Japanese to speak this language like foreigners." → Mabini (W3).

**Opportunistic collaboration / Yamada** (Kojima, W9) — Using occupation's institutional structures for personal advancement while privately disdaining them. Wartime militarism resurfaces as democratic transformation proves thin veneer.

**Pragmatic adaptation / Michiko** (Kojima, W9) — Real fluency and material acceptance without either Isa's paralysis or Yamada's aggression. → Adenauer's strategic collaboration under constraint (W6–W7).

**Performed competence** (Kojima, W9) — Occupation demands Japanese teachers demonstrate English fluency to American evaluators — not for students' benefit but for the occupier's ranking. Theater serving American authority, not Japanese education.

**"Grateful pupil"** (Shibusawa, W9) — See also Theme 5. The mechanism by which miseducation (Constantino W3) reaches its endpoint: the colonized subject who confirms occupier benevolence is the "grateful pupil" the occupation produced.

**Point IV program** (Latham W11; Cullather W14) — Truman's 1949 proposal reframing colonial intervention as mutual development. Defined itself against "the old imperialism — exploitation for foreign profit." Latham/Cullather show this anticolonial framing concealed structural continuity with imperial tutelage.

**"American cultural enslavement"** (NLF Program, W11) — NLF phrase for US cultural influence under the Diem regime. Echoes Constantino's "miseducation" (W3) and Week 9's "performing democracy." The structure of imposed culture persists across six decades and three case studies.

**HAVA as cultural experiment** (Cullather, W14) — HAVA's Lashkar Gah planned suburb as physical embodiment of modernization ideology's civilizational assumption: that America's spatial and cultural forms are universal templates. Pashtun nomads "met the bulldozers with rifles" — not because the project was underfunded but because their political identity was incompatible with what American engineers were trying to make them.

## Theme 8: Collaboration & Resistance by Occupied Peoples

**Anti-Imperialist League** (W2) — Founded Boston 1898; 500,000+ members; included Twain, Carnegie, Gompers, Addams. Defeated when Senate ratified Treaty of Paris (February 1899) by one vote. Its intellectual contradiction (universal consent + Schurz's racial republicanism) is its defining weakness.

**Irreconcilables** (Mabini, W3) — American military term for Filipino leaders who refused to recognize US sovereignty; their deportation removed the moderates most capable of genuine peace-brokering.

**"Kill and burn" order / Smith case** (NYT/Smith, W3) — General Smith's instruction to make Samar a "howling wilderness." Root's review held Smith accountable only for intemperate verbal expression, not operational policy — colonial accountability calibrated to protect the enterprise.

**Korean People's Republic (KPR)** (Chang, W5) — As collaboration/resistance: the KPR represents what authentic popular mobilization looked like before the US dissolved it — organized, purposeful democratic mobilization, not chaos.

**Autumn Strikes (1946)** (Chang, W5) — General strike of ~300,000 Korean workers, violently suppressed by US forces. Failure of the labor emancipation promised by occupation's democratization rhetoric.

**Korean National Police (KNP)** (Chang, W5) — Colonial police apparatus revived by the US occupation, staffed by collaborators who served Japanese colonialism. Richard Robinson: "the police did more to ruin the reputation of the United States in Korea."

**Jeju April 3 Uprising** (Chang, W6) — Armed resistance against the 1948 separate elections, framed by rebels as a "save-the-nation-movement" against partition — showing resistance came from sovereignty claims, not just communist ideology.

**Adenauer as collaborator-under-constraint** (W6/W7) — Paradigmatic occupied actor: working within occupation structures to advance national interests, accepting Allied terms as price of a West German state. Parliamentary Council's "Basic Law" vs. "constitution" terminology negotiation as paradigm of agency-within-constraint.

**"Double inversion" / Black wives** (Jaima, W9) — (1) Black American women became employers of white German maids; (2) sent to restore racial order, they inadvertently disrupted it through cross-racial intimacy.

**"Unofficial ambassadors"** (Alvah/Jaima, W9) — US military dependents overseas as informal vectors of democratic modeling. Black military wives occupied this role contradictorily — their racial position destabilized the ideology they were meant to embody.

**"While whales fight, shrimps' backs burst"** (Korean proverb, Hurh, W9) — Korea's geopolitical self-description: great-power competition conducted on Korean bodies. → Chang "independence without liberation" (W5).

**Accumulated occupations** (Hurh, W9) — Hurh's childhood spans Japanese colonialism, American military government, and North Korean invasion — three distinct occupying powers in one decade. Korea as the course's sharpest case for occupation as structural condition rather than contingent event.

**Haebang (liberation)** (Hurh, W9) — Korean term for August 15, 1945 liberation from Japan. Hurh's account complicates the concept: US/USSR immediately imposed a division denying Korean self-determination. → Chang "independence without liberation."

**"Strange partnership" (as collaboration model)** (Ladejinsky, W10) — The occupation's most successful collaboration model: indigenous reformer (Hiroo Wada) using the occupier's authority to accomplish what domestic politics had blocked. → KDP obstruction (Kim W7) as negative case; Elite capture (SIGAR W14) as Afghan inversion.

**Ho Chi Minh's arc (1945→1951→1962)** (W11) — 1945 Declaration: nationalist, universalist, pro-American. 1951 Laodong Manifesto: communist, Cold War-aligned, anti-American. 1962 NLF Program: nationalist-communist, broad coalition. The arc shows Ho was not a pre-existing enemy — he was made one by US decisions.

**Viet Cong Program's coalition strategy** (W11) — Broad coalition (workers, peasants, intellectuals, national bourgeoisie, landlords, religious/ethnic minorities). Ho understood effective resistance required breadth, not ideological purity.

**Sunni Awakening (Sahwa)** (Sky, W13) — 2006–07 movement of Sunni tribal leaders who allied with US forces against al-Qaeda in exchange for political incorporation. The surge's success depended on this collaboration. Maliki's subsequent betrayal is Sky's proximate cause of ISIS's rise.

**Counter Terrorism Service (CTS)** (Sky, W13) — Elite, cross-sectarian US-trained Iraqi force; Sky calls it "the most successful U.S. initiative in Iraq since 2003." Model for occupation institution-building. → Ladejinsky's "strange partnership" as WWII precedent (W10).

**"Bloody hands" standard** (Diyala officials, Crocker cable W13) — Criterion for distinguishing rehabilitable Ba'athists (those who joined for career necessity) from those who committed crimes. The pragmatic standard Germany's reverse course applied to ex-Nazis; the CPA refused to apply it.

**Taliban as resistance** (Malkasian, W14) — The Taliban's ideological coherence — representing Islam and Afghan resistance in a way the US-backed government structurally could not match — is Malkasian's explanation for why it survived twenty years. Not a military but an identity argument.

**"Islam + occupation" (as resistance structure)** (Malkasian, W14) — Occupation itself produced its own most powerful enemy by entering a country whose national identity had been forged by resistance to exactly what the US was doing. → Fradera's "dangerous symmetries" (W1) operating through identity, not rights-language.
